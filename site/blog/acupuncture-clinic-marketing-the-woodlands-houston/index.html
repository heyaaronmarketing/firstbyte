Tuesday, 2:15 p.m. Two of your three treatment rooms are empty. The phone rings and it’s someone from Spring asking whether you take Blue Cross. An Instagram DM comes in at the same time: “Can acupuncture help me get pregnant? We start IVF in March.” Your front desk answers the first one with “it depends on your plan” and leaves the second one for you, because nobody wants to say the wrong thing.

Both messages are the job of acupuncture marketing in miniature. People want to know if you’ll take their insurance, and whether you can help with the thing that hurts or worries them. Answer both honestly and quickly, in public, and you get booked.

To get more acupuncture patients in The Woodlands and Houston, own the local search results for the conditions you actually treat, publish a clear insurance and pricing page, and write condition pages that are careful about what the evidence supports. Then build a few referral relationships with chiropractors, physical therapists and fertility clinics, and turn first visits into packages or memberships. This guide walks through each part, with the Texas rules that sit on top of it.

The Texas rules that shape every acupuncture ad

Acupuncturists in Texas are licensed by the Texas State Board of Acupuncture Examiners, a nine-member board the Legislature created in 1993 inside the Medical Practice Act. The Texas Medical Board provides its staff and gives final approval to its licensing decisions, disciplinary actions and rules, per the Texas Medical Board’s page on the acupuncture board. So when you read “acupuncture board,” think TMB building, TMB process.

Three rules matter most for marketing:

  • Your title. Under 22 TAC §183.15, a licensee uses “Licensed Acupuncturist,” “Lic. Ac.” or “L. Ac.” immediately after their name on materials about the practice, and only licensees may use those titles. If you’re also a Texas-licensed physician, chiropractor or one of the other listed professions, you’re exempt from the “immediately after” part.
  • No false or misleading ads, and no physician talk. 22 TAC §184.27 bans false, misleading or deceptive advertising, bans holding yourself out as a physician or surgeon, and lets you say “board certified” only if the ad names the full certifying board and you’re currently certified.
  • “Dr.” needs context. Plenty of acupuncturists hold a DAOM or DACM. The acupuncture rule points additional titles to the Healing Art Identification Act, and Chapter 104 requires people who use “doctor” outside the listed professions to say what authority or degree the title comes from. “Dr. Lin, DAOM, L.Ac.” on a page works better than a bare “Dr. Lin,” which on a medical-looking site can read like an M.D.

Where we see clinics slip is in the places nobody reviews: a Google Business Profile name with “Dr.” stuffed in, an old Facebook bio, a Groupon listing written by someone at Groupon. Audit those once a year with the same care as the website.

The market is small enough that reputation travels. the Texas DSHS Health Professions Resource Center counted 1,199 acupuncturists in Texas in September 2023. That’s the whole state. In The Woodlands, Conroe and Spring, a patient comparing options might find six or eight clinics, a couple of chiropractors offering dry needling, and a med spa or two with “acupuncture” on the menu.

Insurance, Medicare and cash pay: say it plainly on the site

The insurance question is the most common call, and most clinic websites answer it with a single line: “We accept most insurance.” That line creates more phone calls than it books.

Medicare is the easiest to explain because the rules are specific. Per Medicare’s acupuncture coverage page, Part B covers acupuncture only for chronic low back pain that has lasted 12 weeks or longer with no known systemic cause and isn’t tied to surgery or pregnancy. It covers up to 12 treatments in 90 days, eight more if the patient improves, and no more than 20 a year. And Medicare doesn’t pay licensed acupuncturists directly: the treatment has to come from a doctor or another provider, such as a nurse practitioner or physician assistant, who has a master’s or doctoral degree in acupuncture or Oriental medicine and a current state acupuncture license. If your clinic doesn’t have that setup, say so on the page, kindly. Medicare patients would rather hear it from your website than at checkout.

Commercial plans vary by employer and plan year, so we’d build the insurance page around a process rather than a list of logos:

  1. “Send us your card” form or text line. You verify benefits before the first visit and call back with the patient’s actual cost.
  2. Plans you’re in-network with, by name, with a “last updated” month.
  3. Out-of-network and superbill option, explained in two sentences.
  4. HSA and FSA cards accepted. IRS Publication 502 lists acupuncture as an includible medical expense, which is why these cards usually work, but tell patients to confirm with their plan administrator.
  5. Cash prices for the first visit, follow-ups and packages. Real numbers.

Publishing cash prices scares some owners. In our experience it filters out the bargain shoppers you didn’t want and pulls in people who’ve already decided they can afford you. If you’re worried about undercutting a package, show the single-visit price and the per-visit package price side by side.

Condition pages for pain, fertility and stress, written carefully

People don’t search for “acupuncture.” They search for “acupuncture for sciatica The Woodlands,” “fertility acupuncture Houston” or “acupuncture for anxiety near me.” Condition pages are how you rank for those, and they’re also where clinics get into trouble.

The FTC’s position is that health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence. Its the FTC’s Health Products Compliance Guidance, published December 20, 2022, covers health claims broadly, well beyond supplements, and says health-benefit claims generally need randomized, controlled human trials. Patient testimonials don’t substitute for that. Your board rule on misleading advertising sits on top.

So match the confidence of each page to the evidence behind it:

Condition pageWhat the research saysHow we’d write it
Chronic low back painNCCIH’s summary of the research calls the evidence for chronic low back pain moderate quality; a 2017 American College of Physicians guideline lists acupuncture as a first-line option.Your strongest page. Cite the guideline, describe a typical course of treatment, link the Medicare rules.
Knee osteoarthritis, neck pain, headachesNCCIH describes benefits over sham for osteoarthritis and migraine prevention, with small differences over sham for migraine.“Research suggests it may help some people.” Mention working alongside their doctor or PT.
Fertility and IVF supportIn a 2018 JAMA trial of 824 women, live births were 18.3% with acupuncture and 17.8% with sham, a nonsignificant difference. NCCIH notes a 2021 review also found no difference.Talk about comfort, stress and support during treatment, coordinated with their fertility clinic. Never imply better pregnancy odds.
Stress and anxietyNCCIH doesn’t summarize anxiety evidence on its main acupuncture page.Describe the experience of a session and what patients tell you. No outcome promises, and a line about seeing a mental health professional.

The fertility page deserves special care. Houston has a large fertility medicine community, and many of those clinics are good referral partners. A page promising “boost your IVF success” loses them. A page that says “we work alongside your reproductive endocrinologist and schedule around transfer dates” gets you on their handout.

Google Business Profile and local SEO for acupuncture clinics

Most first-time acupuncture patients pick from the map pack. The profile basics are boring and they decide the outcome.

  • Primary category set to the acupuncture category, not “massage” or “wellness center,” even if you do both.
  • Business name exactly as it appears on your sign. No “Best Acupuncture The Woodlands” suffix; Google’s guidelines don’t allow it, and it reads badly anyway.
  • Services listed by condition and modality: cupping, electroacupuncture, herbal consults, fertility support, each with a one-line description.
  • Photos of the treatment rooms, the parking lot and the entrance. A lot of Woodlands clinics sit in office parks off Research Forest or in strip centers on FM 1960, and first-timers get lost.
  • Booking link straight to your scheduler’s new-patient flow.

Then build a condition page for each of your top five conditions and a location page if you have a second office in Katy or the Heights. Google Ads for acupuncture: keywords, negatives and the health rules

Search ads work well for acupuncture when they’re tight. Three or four ad groups, each tied to a condition page:

  • General intent: acupuncture near me, acupuncturist The Woodlands, acupuncture Spring TX, acupuncture Conroe.
  • Pain: acupuncture for back pain, acupuncture for sciatica, acupuncture for neck pain, dry needling alternative.
  • Fertility support: fertility acupuncture Houston, acupuncture IVF support. Keep the ad copy as careful as the page.
  • Brand: your clinic name and your practitioners’ names.

The targeting rules are where health advertisers get surprised. Google treats health as a sensitive category in personalized advertising. Per Google’s health in personalized advertising policy, ads about physical or mental health conditions can’t use Customer Match, your data segments, lookalikes or audience expansion, and “treatments for pregnancy and infertility” is listed as an example. In plain terms: don’t plan on retargeting fertility page visitors or uploading your patient list. Keyword targeting and location do the work, which is fine, because the keyword already tells you what the person wants.

Ad copy that has worked in accounts we’ve run for health providers (illustrative, adapt to your clinic):

“Licensed Acupuncturists in The Woodlands | New Patient Visit [your price] | We Verify Your Insurance Before You Come In | Evening Appointments on Research Forest”

Budget-wise, a single-location clinic can learn a lot with $1,000 to $2,000 a month in a 10- to 15-mile radius. That’s an illustrative range, not a benchmark. Track booked new-patient appointments, not clicks. Our Houston cost-per-click benchmarks help you gut-check click costs before you commit.

Instagram and Facebook: show the room, explain the needles

The biggest barrier for new patients is fear of needles and not knowing what happens in a session. Social content that answers that does more than any promo.

What tends to work: a 30-second walkthrough of a first visit, a practitioner showing how thin the needles are next to a hair, a cupping session with the patient’s permission and face out of frame, and short answers to the questions your front desk hears every week. Post the same clips to your Google Business Profile.

Meta has its own restrictions for health advertisers. Search Engine Land describes tiers where some health and wellness accounts lose the ability to optimize for lower-funnel events like purchases or bookings, and some lose pixel data sharing entirely. Check Events Manager for restrictions before you assume your “Booked” event still reaches Meta. Instant forms with a fast callback often beat pixel-optimized campaigns for clinics. HIPAA: booking pixels and review replies

If you bill insurance electronically, you’re very likely a HIPAA covered entity, and your website tracking matters. HHS’s bulletin on online tracking says tracking tools on pages behind a login, and on pages like appointment forms that collect health information, can disclose protected health information, and a vendor that receives it is a business associate that needs a signed agreement. A cookie banner doesn’t count as a HIPAA authorization. A federal court in Texas vacated part of that guidance on June 20, 2024, the part about public pages that don’t collect health information, but the booking-form part still stands.

The practical version for a small clinic: keep Meta and Google tags off your intake and booking pages unless your vendor will sign a business associate agreement, and send conversions back as coded events (“NP Booked”) rather than “Fertility Consult Booked.” Our guide to tracking form fills and calls covers the setup.

Review replies are the other HIPAA trap. A California dental practice paid $23,000 to settle with HHS after replying to online reviews with patient names and treatment details. For an acupuncture clinic, “We’re sorry your fertility treatment didn’t go as hoped” confirms someone is a patient and what for. Reply without confirming anything: thank them, invite them to call the office manager, sign with a name.

Reviews: ask everyone, the same way, without strings

Reviews win the map pack and calm first-timers. The rules are straightforward. Google’s prohibited content policy bans incentives for reviews, discouraging negative reviews, selectively asking only happy patients, and asking staff to collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content. the FTC’s reviews rule, effective October 21, 2024, adds civil penalties of up to $51,744 per violation for fake reviews and review suppression.

A text that works after the third or fourth visit, when patients have felt a difference:

“Hi [first name], thanks for coming in this week. If you have a minute, an honest Google review helps other people in The Woodlands find us: [link]. Thank you either way. — [clinic name]”

Send it to every patient, not just the ones who smiled. Our complete guide to Google reviews has more templates.

Referral relationships: chiropractors, PTs and fertility clinics

The best acupuncture clinics we see in north Houston get a steady share of patients from other providers. Chiropractors and physical therapists send patients who’ve plateaued. OB-GYNs and fertility clinics send patients who want support during treatment. Massage therapists send the ones with stubborn neck and shoulder pain.

Earning those referrals is mostly about making the other provider look good:

  • A one-page referral sheet per specialty with what you treat, what you don’t, and how fast you can see a patient.
  • A short written update to the referring provider after the first visit and at the end of a course, with the patient’s permission.
  • Lunch at the office to introduce yourself. Keep it modest.

One firm line. Texas law makes it an offense to knowingly pay or accept anything of value for securing or soliciting patients for a licensed provider, per Texas Occupations Code §102.001. Cross-referral fees, per-patient kickbacks and “marketing fees” tied to referral counts are out. If you’re unsure about a co-marketing deal, ask a healthcare attorney before you sign it. Packages and memberships that patients don’t resent

Acupuncture works in courses, so packages make sense clinically and financially. A monthly membership (say, two sessions a month at a set price) smooths your revenue through the slow weeks after New Year’s resolutions fade and during August, when half of The Woodlands is out of town.

Write the terms like you’d want them written for you: what’s included, how long sessions last, whether unused sessions roll over, how to cancel, and whether the price can change. The FTC’s click-to-cancel rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit on July 8, 2025, but the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act still applies to online subscriptions, and a membership that’s hard to cancel generates exactly the one-star reviews you don’t want. Let people cancel by email or text. It costs you almost nothing.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more acupuncture patients in The Woodlands?

Start with a complete Google Business Profile using the acupuncture category, real photos and a booking link, plus a steady stream of honest reviews requested from every patient. Add condition pages for pain, fertility support and stress that are careful about evidence, a clear insurance and pricing page, and referral relationships with chiropractors, physical therapists and fertility clinics. Paid search on condition keywords fills gaps.

What title must a Texas acupuncturist use in advertising?

Texas rule 22 TAC 183.15 requires licensees to use Licensed Acupuncturist, Lic. Ac. or L. Ac. immediately after their name on materials about their practice, and only licensees may use those titles. Dual-licensed providers such as physicians or chiropractors are exempt from the placement requirement. Acupuncturists may not hold themselves out as physicians, and doctor titles should name the degree they come from.

Does Medicare cover acupuncture in Houston?

Medicare Part B covers acupuncture only for chronic low back pain lasting 12 weeks or longer, up to 12 sessions in 90 days and 20 per year if the patient improves. Medicare doesn’t pay licensed acupuncturists directly, so care must come from or be supervised by an eligible provider such as a physician, nurse practitioner or physician assistant who meets the training and licensing requirements.

Can an acupuncture clinic advertise fertility treatment on Google?

Yes, but carefully. Google treats fertility as a sensitive health category, so Customer Match, your data segments and lookalike audiences aren’t available for those ads, and keyword targeting does the work. The claims matter more: a 2018 JAMA trial found no difference in IVF live birth rates between acupuncture and sham, so describe support and comfort during treatment rather than better pregnancy odds.

Is it OK to use a Meta pixel on an acupuncture booking page?

If your clinic bills insurance electronically, it is likely a HIPAA covered entity, and HHS guidance says tracking tools on booking forms that collect health information can disclose protected health information. Keep Meta and Google tags off intake and booking pages unless the vendor signs a business associate agreement, and send coded conversion events that don’t reveal a condition. Meta also restricts many health advertisers’ events.
