713,430. That’s how many Asian residents the Asian Chamber of Commerce Houston counts in the Houston area for 2023, about 9.5% of everyone here. Rice University’s Rice University’s Kinder Institute put the share at 1.8% back in 1980.

Most Houston and The Woodlands businesses still market to that group as one audience called “Asian,” usually with a red-and-gold graphic in late January. That single audience doesn’t exist.

A Vietnamese grandmother in Alief who listens to Radio Saigon Houston, a Telugu-speaking software engineer in Katy and a Filipino nurse in Pearland don’t share a language or a holiday calendar. They don’t use the same apps or the same grocery store either. What they share is a census checkbox.

This guide is built from the numbers first: who lives where, which languages are actually spoken at home, and where each community gets its information. Then it covers the practical part: platforms, translation, the 2027 holiday calendar and the mistakes we’d steer any client away from. It’s a companion to our guide to Hispanic marketing in Houston, and a lot of the mechanics (separate language pages, phones answered in-language) carry over.

How many Asian Americans live in Houston, and from where?

Start with the size. Rice’s Kinder Institute released the first findings from its Asian American Community Study in January 2026, covering Harris, Fort Bend, Montgomery and Brazoria counties. It counted more than 655,000 Asian residents in the region, with Asian Indians the largest group at over 150,000. Fort Bend’s Asian population grew 117% from 2010 to 2023. Montgomery County’s grew 180%, which surprises fewer people in The Woodlands every year.

The groups are close in size, which is what makes Houston unusual. The Asian Chamber’s 2023 figures, drawn from Census data, put Asian Indian residents at 167,084 and Vietnamese at 158,957, with Chinese residents at 122,546. Filipino (78,540) and Pakistani (50,887) residents come next, then Korean (28,045). Pew’s analysis of 2017-2019 Census data put Houston’s Vietnamese population second among U.S. metros, tied with San Jose and behind only Los Angeles.

Income and education vary a lot inside the total. In the Kinder survey of 2,100 residents, 46% of Asian households earned over $100,000 a year and 25% earned under $50,000. Among Vietnamese households, 30% earned over $100,000 and 19% earned under $25,000. Party identification split almost evenly in thirds, per ABC13’s coverage of the study, and most respondents described themselves as moderate. Any campaign built on a single “affluent Asian professional” persona misses a big share of the market.

Which languages Houston’s Asian American residents speak at home

Population tells you who. Language tells you what to write. Inside Houston city limits, the City of Houston’s top-25 languages table (2021 American Community Survey) shows Vietnamese as the third most spoken language at home, after English and Spanish, with 29,060 speakers. Chinese, which the Census groups together (Mandarin and Cantonese), is fourth at 27,838.

Below those two, South Asian languages spread out: Hindi, Urdu, Telugu, Gujarati and Tamil all show up in the city’s top 20. That fragmentation is the point. An Indian American audience in Sugar Land or Katy doesn’t have one home language, and in our experience most read English comfortably, so English-language marketing with the right cultural cues usually beats a Hindi translation. The same is true for many Filipino families, where Tagalog is spoken at home but English is the default for business.

Vietnamese is different. Pew Research Center found 56% of Vietnamese Americans age 5 and older are proficient in English, and only 36% of Vietnamese immigrants are, against 74% of Asian Americans overall per Pew’s key facts about Asian Americans. If your customers include older Vietnamese homeowners in Alief, Mission Bend or southwest Houston, Vietnamese-language ads, pages and phone answering will reach people that English never will.

Two details catch first-timers. Chinese comes in two scripts: Simplified for most mainland Chinese immigrants, Traditional for many Taiwanese, Hong Kong and older Cantonese-speaking families. And Vietnamese depends on its diacritics. “Ma,” “má,” “mà” and “mã” are different words, so a font or a CMS that strips accent marks turns your copy into nonsense.

Local in-language media in Houston

Houston has a real in-language media scene, and for some audiences it outperforms anything you can buy on Meta. A few outlets with documented local roots:

  • Chinese. Southern News Group, founded in Houston by Wea Lee, publishes community newspapers and runs the ITV channel. Its Southern Chinese Daily News site still publishes the Houston Chinese yellow pages (休斯顿黄页) as an e-book, which tells you something about how many Chinese-speaking customers still look up a dentist or a roofer.
  • Vietnamese. Radio Saigon Houston is a Vietnamese-language AM station that ABC13 profiled in 2021 as a connection point for the area’s Vietnamese community. Call-in shows and paid segments with a host who speaks to your service are worth more than a 30-second spot.
  • South Asian. Voice of Asia, a weekly founded in 1987 by Koshy Thomas, covers the Houston Asian American community, and Indo-American News has been published weekly from southwest Houston for decades.
  • Filipino. In our experience the reach here runs through community associations, parishes and Facebook groups more than a single publication. We couldn’t verify a current Houston Filipino-language paper, so we won’t name one.

Rates and circulation change, so ask each outlet for current numbers and a sample of an ad that ran for a business like yours. Then give every placement its own phone number or landing page URL so you can tell what worked. The call tracking guide covers how.

Platforms: WeChat, Zalo, KakaoTalk, WhatsApp and in-language Facebook and YouTube

Each community’s home country has its own default messaging app, and immigrants often keep it because that’s where family is. Sinch’s country-by-country rundown lists WeChat as the most popular messaging app in China, KakaoTalk in South Korea, Zalo in Vietnam, WhatsApp in India and Messenger in the Philippines. Zalo alone had 79.2 million monthly active users in the third quarter of 2025, per Tuoi Tre.

On Meta, you can’t target by ethnicity. Meta removed detailed targeting options tied to race and ethnicity, among other sensitive topics, on January 19, 2022, per Search Engine Land. What you can target is language and location. Meta’s multi-language ads feature lets one ad carry a Vietnamese, Chinese and English version and shows each person the one that matches their language setting.

On Google, the rules changed this fall. Google’s language targeting help page says that starting September 2026, the campaign-level language setting for Search is gone, and Search ads match based on the language of the ads themselves. So a Vietnamese Search campaign now lives or dies on Vietnamese ad text and Vietnamese keywords like “nha khoa gần tôi” (dentist near me) or “sửa máy lạnh” (AC repair). Our search team builds these as separate campaigns so the reporting stays clean.

Translation vs. transcreation for Houston’s Asian languages

Machine translation gets you words. It doesn’t get you an ad that a Vietnamese speaker in Alief would believe was written for them.

Transcreation means a native speaker rewrites the message for the audience, keeping the intent and changing whatever needs changing: the headline, the offer, the photo, sometimes the call to action. A “Free estimate!” headline translated word for word into Vietnamese can read stiff or even suspicious. A native writer might lead with the fact that your technicians speak Vietnamese, because to an older homeowner that’s the actual benefit.

For the website, Google’s guidance on multilingual sites recommends a separate URL for each language version, links between them, and no automatic redirects based on a guessed language. So build /vi/ or /zh-hans/ pages. A Google Translate dropdown in the footer doesn’t count, because Google can’t index it as a Vietnamese page and your Vietnamese ads have nowhere good to land.

The rule we give every client: don’t launch a language until someone answers the phone in it. A Vietnamese ad that leads to an English-only voicemail costs you the click and some trust. The 2027 holiday calendar: Lunar New Year, Tết, Diwali and more

Holidays are where most brands show up, and where most of the cringe happens. These are the 2027 dates, verified, with what we’d do around each.

Lunar New Year and Tết, Saturday, February 6, 2027. Per Royal Museums Greenwich, it opens the Year of the Goat. Vietnam’s lunar calendar puts Tết Nguyên Đán on the same day this year. Call it Lunar New Year, or Tết in Vietnamese-language materials. “Chinese New Year” is accurate for Chinese customers and wrong for Vietnamese and Korean ones (Koreans call it Seollal). Start campaigns two to three weeks early. Families buy gifts, book restaurants and schedule home repairs before the holiday, not on it.

Holi, March 22, 2027 (some regions mark March 23, per Office Holidays). A spring festival of colors, big with Hindu families. Fine for a light social post; most businesses shouldn’t build a promotion around it.

Asian American, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander Heritage Month, May. Better used for spotlighting staff or local partners than for discounts.

Mid-Autumn Festival, Wednesday, September 15, 2027. Mooncake season for Chinese families and Tết Trung Thu for Vietnamese families, when kids carry lanterns. Bakeries, gift shops and restaurants should plan for the two weeks before.

Diwali, Friday, October 29, 2027. The biggest shopping moment for many Indian American households: jewelry, clothes, sweets, home cleaning and decor, even cars. Local temples sometimes publish slightly different dates based on U.S. time zones, so confirm with a temple calendar before you print anything.

Mistakes to avoid (we’ve seen all of these)

Most of the damage comes from shortcuts rather than bad intentions. These are the ones we’d catch in a creative review.

The flag one deserves a sentence. Many Vietnamese Americans in Houston are refugees or children of refugees from the fall of Saigon in 1975. Houston Landing describes businesses in Little Saigon flying the yellow flag with three red stripes of the former South Vietnam, while the current red flag with a yellow star is rarely seen. Use the wrong one in a Tết ad and you’ll hear about it within the hour.

Stock photos are the other common one. A Sugar Land dental practice that shows a generic East Asian family for its Diwali post has told its Indian patients it wasn’t paying attention. Use photos of your real staff and customers (with permission), and when you use stock, match it to the actual audience.

A first-quarter plan for a Houston business

Here’s how we’d start, for a business that already serves some Asian American customers and wants more:

  1. Weeks 1 and 2: look at your own data. Pull customer ZIP codes and names from your CRM, and ask your front desk which languages callers prefer. You’re looking for a pattern rather than a demographic report.
  2. Weeks 3 to 5: pick one language and staff it. One bilingual employee on the phones beats a five-language website.
  3. Weeks 6 to 9: build the landing page and transcreated ads. Separate URL, native-speaker copy, the right script for Chinese, correct diacritics for Vietnamese.
  4. Weeks 10 to 13: run and measure. Meta multi-language ads in a radius around your hub, Google Search ads in that language, one in-language media test with its own tracking number. Judge each on booked appointments or jobs.

Time it so the launch lands two weeks before Lunar New Year or Diwali, and you get a built-in reason to talk to people. Our paid social team runs the Meta side for clients across Greater Houston.

Frequently asked questions

How large is the Asian American population in Houston?

Rice University’s Kinder Institute counted more than 655,000 Asian residents in Harris, Fort Bend, Montgomery and Brazoria counties in its 2026 study, nearly 10% of the population. The Asian Chamber of Commerce Houston puts the Houston area at 713,430 for 2023. Asian Indians are the largest group, followed closely by Vietnamese and Chinese residents, then Filipino, Pakistani and Korean residents.

What Asian languages are most spoken in Houston?

Inside Houston city limits, Vietnamese is the third most spoken language at home after English and Spanish, and Chinese is fourth, according to the City of Houston’s table of 2021 American Community Survey data. Hindi, Tagalog, Urdu, Telugu, Gujarati, Korean and Tamil also rank in the city’s top 20. Many South Asian and Filipino residents read English comfortably, so the language choice depends on the audience.

Can I target Asian Americans with Facebook ads in Houston?

Not by ethnicity. Meta removed detailed targeting options tied to race and ethnicity in January 2022. You can target by language and location, such as Vietnamese speakers within a radius of Alief or Sugar Land, and Meta’s multi-language ads feature shows each person the version of an ad in their own language. Strong in-language creative does most of the targeting work.

When is Lunar New Year and Diwali in 2027?

Lunar New Year, called Tết by Vietnamese families and Seollal by Korean families, falls on Saturday, February 6, 2027, the start of the Year of the Goat. Diwali falls on Friday, October 29, 2027, though some Houston temples may publish a slightly different date based on U.S. time zones. Start holiday campaigns two to three weeks before each date.

Should my Houston business translate its website into Vietnamese or Chinese?

Only if someone on your team can answer calls and messages in that language. If you can, build separate pages at their own URLs, such as a /vi/ section, written by a native speaker rather than machine translated. Use Simplified or Traditional Chinese depending on your audience, and make sure Vietnamese diacritics display correctly. A translate widget alone won’t rank or convert.
