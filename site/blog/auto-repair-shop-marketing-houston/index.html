Tuesday, 10:40 a.m. Four bays, two of them empty. The phone rings and it’s the third person today asking “how much for brakes?” Your service writer says “depends on the car, bring it in,” and the caller says thanks and hangs up.

That call is most of auto repair shop marketing in one moment.

To get more customers for an auto repair shop in Houston, win the Google Maps results for the services you’re best at and keep a steady stream of honest reviews coming in. Run search ads only on high-intent repair keywords, with hard negatives for DIY, parts and job seekers. Answer the price question better than the shop down the street does.

Then keep the customers you already have coming back, which is cheaper than everything above. Independent shops, tire shops, transmission specialists and collision centers all run on the same engine. The weights change.

Below is how we’d set it up for a shop in Spring, The Woodlands, Cypress or anywhere inside the Beltway, with the actual settings.

Where Houston drivers actually find a mechanic

Nobody browses for a mechanic. Something breaks, a light comes on, or the car makes a new noise on I-45, and the driver searches. “Auto repair near me.” “Brake repair Spring TX.” “Transmission shop Conroe.” The top of that results page is ads, then the map pack, then the websites.

The cars are getting older, which is good for independents. S&P Global Mobility put the average age of U.S. light vehicles at 12.8 years in 2025, with passenger cars at 14.5, across 289 million vehicles on the road, and noted the repair opportunity as vehicles roll off warranty. A 12-year-old Silverado isn’t going back to the dealer for brakes. It’s going to whoever shows up first in Google Maps with good reviews and an open bay.

Location matters more than shops like to admit. Houston drivers drop cars near home or near work, then Uber or get a ride. A shop on FM 1960 or near the SH-249 and Grand Parkway interchange can pull commuters as well as neighbors, and your marketing should say so. “Drop off on your way to the Energy Corridor, we’ll text you photos by lunch.”

So the map pack is the first fight. Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the full playbook, and the next two sections are the shop-specific parts.

Google Business Profile categories and services for repair shops

Your primary category is the single strongest setting you control. Pick the one that matches the work you most want.

  • General repair: Auto repair shop as primary.
  • Specialists: Transmission shop, Brake shop, Tire shop, Auto body shop, Oil change service, Auto air conditioning service or Car battery store as primary when that really is your main business.
  • Secondary categories: add the other real services you do. A general shop that does a lot of AC work in July should have the AC category. Don’t add categories for work you send out.

Then fill in the Services list under each category with plain-English entries and a short description: “Brake pad and rotor replacement, most cars and light trucks, same-day when parts are in stock.” Add your hours accurately, including Saturdays (Saturday is when a lot of commuters can actually drop off). Add the “appointment” link to a real booking page. Upload photos of your bays, your techs at work, your waiting area and your sign from the street, so a first-timer can find the driveway.

Two shop mistakes we see constantly. Hours that say “open 24 hours” because someone hit the wrong button, which sends angry 9 p.m. callers your way. And a profile that lists the owner’s cell number, so every Maps call goes to a phone in a pocket under a car.

Reviews: how to ask without breaking Google’s rules

Reviews win map rankings and they win the phone call. Most shops are terrible at asking.

The rules first, from Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps: you can’t offer incentives for reviews (no $10 off the next oil change), you can’t discourage negative reviews, and you can’t “selectively solicit positive reviews,” which means no review gating where only happy customers get the link. In April 2026, Google also added language against merchants telling staff to collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content, which may include asking customers to name a particular tech, per PPC Land. The scope isn’t fully settled, so we’d stop running “get 10 reviews mentioning your name” contests.

What works and stays inside the lines:

  1. Send every customer the same review link by text within an hour of pickup, when the car feels good and the bill is fresh.
  2. Have the service writer mention it at checkout in one sentence. No script, no pressure.
  3. Reply to every review, especially the bad ones, with specifics and an offer to call. Future customers read the reply more closely than the complaint.
  4. Put the link on the invoice and the digital inspection report.

Local Services Ads for auto repair: what changed in 2026

Local Services Ads are the pay-per-lead boxes at the very top of some searches. For years they were mainly a home services product. Auto is very much in now.

Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes Auto Repair Shop, Auto Body Shop, Auto Glass Repair Service, Brake Shop, Car Battery Store, Car Inspection Service, Oil Change Service, Tire Shop, Transmission Shop, Towing and several more. In August 2026, Search Engine Roundtable reported Google split the old generic auto categories into those specific services and introduced “pre-badge” ads that can start serving after preliminary checks while onboarding finishes. Google’s own page notes that the Google Verified badge isn’t available for auto verticals, so auto LSAs run without it.

You pay per lead (a call or message) rather than per click, and you can dispute leads that weren’t real customers. Availability still varies by service and area, so the only way to know for your shop is to start the sign-up and enter your address.

With strong Google reviews, we’d test LSA alongside regular search ads, starting with your most profitable service category. If you have 14 reviews and a 3.9 rating, fix that first. LSA ranking leans hard on reviews and responsiveness. We compare the two ad types in detail in Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads; the logic carries over to auto.

The “how much for brakes?” problem: pricing and estimates

Back to Tuesday’s phone call. Every shop knows you can’t quote a brake job blind. Every customer knows “bring it in” sometimes means “we’ll find $1,400 of other stuff.” The shop that wins is the one that closes that trust gap.

Practical version:

  • Publish “starting at” prices online for routine work: oil change by oil type, front brake pads per axle, battery installed, alignment, diagnostic fee. Add “most cars and light trucks, parts vary by vehicle.” A range is far better than nothing.
  • Say plainly whether the diagnostic fee is credited toward the repair if they approve it. Many callers are really asking “will I pay you just to tell me what’s wrong?”
  • Send a digital inspection with photos and short notes by text before you ask for approval. A photo of a worn pad next to a new one sells the job honestly.
  • Split the estimate into “do now,” “soon” and “watch.” It cuts the feeling of being upsold, and the “soon” list becomes your follow-up marketing.
  • Train whoever answers the phone to give the range and then ask for the appointment.

Shops worry that showing prices invites shoppers. Some of them will shop. But a page that answers the price question also gets more calls from the people who just wanted a straight answer, and your ads will convert better because the landing page matches the search.

Texas inspection changes, Houston heat and the seasons that drive repair demand

For decades, the annual safety inspection sent a stream of cars into shops, and a good writer could turn a sticker visit into a brake job. That changed. Under House Bill 3297, Texas ended the safety inspection program for non-commercial vehicles on January 1, 2025, per TxDMV’s bulletin on the change. Drivers now pay a $7.50 Inspection Program Replacement Fee at registration instead ($16.75 for a new vehicle, covering two years).

Emissions testing didn’t go away. It’s still required before registration in 17 counties, including Harris, Montgomery, Fort Bend, Brazoria and Galveston. So if you’re a certified emissions station in the Houston area, those visits continue; the bundled safety check doesn’t. And the repeal covered non-commercial vehicles only, so check with DPS on what still applies to commercial vehicles you service.

So replace the lost touchpoint on purpose. A free courtesy inspection with any oil change does most of the work. A pre-road-trip check promoted before Thanksgiving and spring break helps too, and the emissions visit deserves a digital inspection report like any other service.

Then plan around Houston’s seasons. The July and August heat is hard on batteries and AC systems; one Houston shop owner told ABC13 in 2023 that local heat can cut a battery’s expected life by about a third. Push AC and battery campaigns in late spring, before the first heat wave. Wait until everyone’s stranded and you’re bidding against every shop in town. Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30, so if you do flood or storm damage work, have that page and ad group ready to switch on the same afternoon.

Fleet and commercial accounts: steady work between walk-ins

A walk-in customer might visit twice a year. A plumbing company with 12 vans might bring you a vehicle every week. North Houston is full of service fleets (HVAC, plumbing, pest control, landscaping, electrical), plus oilfield service trucks, delivery vans and church and school buses.

Fleet work comes from outreach and decent terms. Ads barely figure in.

  1. Build a fleet page: services, turnaround promise, pickup and delivery, monthly invoicing, and who to call.
  2. Make a list of 40 local fleets within five miles. Drive the lot after hours if you have to, then ask for the operations manager rather than the owner.
  3. Offer a first-vehicle inspection at no charge and a written priority-bay policy.
  4. Report by vehicle each month (mileage, work done, what’s coming due). Fleet managers stay where the paperwork is easy.

Many of those fleets are businesses we work with on the other side, so we see what they care about: downtime. If you can say “van in at 7, back by 3 or we tell you by 10 a.m.,” you have a pitch. Our home services industry page shows the kinds of companies that run these fleets.

Retention: service reminders, texts and consent

The cheapest car to fill a bay is one that’s already been in your bay. Retention is where shops make their margin, and it’s mostly automation your shop management software already supports.

  • Service reminders by mileage or date for oil changes, tire rotations and the “soon” items from the last inspection.
  • Declined-work follow-up at 30 and 90 days, with the inspection photo attached. This one pays for the software by itself in most shops we’ve seen.
  • A once-a-month email with something useful, like an AC check before summer or a hurricane-season prep list. Nobody misses another coupon blast.

Texting needs care. Service updates about a car the customer just dropped off are different from marketing texts. Get clear opt-in for marketing messages, separate from the service-update consent, and keep the record. Texas also brought marketing texts under its telephone solicitation law through SB 140, effective September 1, 2025, with a registration requirement and a private right of action for violations, though there are exemptions (including some tied to current or former customers and businesses that mostly sell from a physical location), per Morgan Lewis’s summary of SB 140. Ask your attorney which applies to your shop before you start a promo text program.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more customers for my auto repair shop?

Start with your Google Business Profile: the right primary category, accurate hours, real photos and a steady flow of reviews requested by text after pickup. Then add search ads on specific service keywords like brake repair or transmission repair near your shop, with negatives for DIY, parts and jobs. Publish starting prices for routine work, and use service reminders to bring past customers back.

Are Google Local Services Ads available for auto repair?

Yes. Google’s Local Services Ads list now includes Auto Repair Shop, Brake Shop, Transmission Shop, Tire Shop, Oil Change Service, Auto Body Shop and other auto categories, and Google split the old generic auto categories into specific services in 2026. Availability varies by area and service, and the Google Verified badge isn’t offered for auto verticals. Start the sign-up with your address to check your shop.

What keywords should an auto repair shop use in Google Ads?

Bid on specific services with local or near-me terms, such as brake repair near me, check engine light diagnosis, car AC repair Houston or transmission shop Conroe. These convert better than broad auto repair keywords. Add negative keywords for DIY searches (how to, YouTube, manual), parts shoppers (parts, AutoZone, junkyard) and job seekers (jobs, hiring, salary), and review the search terms report weekly.

Does Texas still require vehicle inspections?

Texas ended safety inspections for non-commercial vehicles on January 1, 2025, and drivers now pay a $7.50 Inspection Program Replacement Fee at registration. Emissions testing is still required in 17 counties, including Harris, Montgomery, Fort Bend, Brazoria and Galveston. Commercial vehicles were not part of that repeal, so shops should check current DPS rules for commercial inspections.

Can I offer a discount for Google reviews at my shop?

No. Google’s policy prohibits offering discounts, free services or payment in exchange for reviews, and it also bans discouraging negative reviews or only asking happy customers. Since April 2026 Google also discourages telling staff to collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content. Send every customer the same review link after pickup and reply to every review instead.
