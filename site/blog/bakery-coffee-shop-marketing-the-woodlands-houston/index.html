Saturday, 7:10 a.m. The line is out the door, the kolaches are gone by 8:30, and someone is asking if you can do a two-tier unicorn cake for next Saturday. Tuesday at 2 p.m., you can hear the espresso machine hum in an empty room. And there’s an Instagram DM from Thursday asking about 60 dozen cookies for a company event on Research Forest. Nobody has answered it.

Most bakery and coffee shop owners we talk to don’t have a traffic problem on Saturday morning. They have a Tuesday problem, an order-taking problem, and no idea which of their marketing actually brings people in.

Bakery and coffee shop marketing in The Woodlands and Houston works best when you treat the business as three revenue streams. Walk-in traffic runs on Google Maps, photos and the morning commute. Custom cakes and special orders run on Instagram and fast replies. Catering and office orders run on relationships and a simple ordering page. Each needs its own marketing, and the last two usually carry the margin.

This guide is for independent bakeries, cafés and coffee shops, plus home bakers working under Texas cottage food law, from Market Street to Old Town Spring to Katy.

A pastry case can be full and the business can still struggle, because walk-in margins are thin after labor and rent in a place like Hughes Landing. Custom and catering orders are planned, made in batches and paid up front. We’d put at least half of any marketing effort there.

Foot traffic in Market Street, Hughes Landing and Town Center districts

A shop in a walkable district plays a different game than one in a strip center on FM 2920. In The Woodlands Town Center, along Market Street, around Hughes Landing and the Waterway, and in places like Sugar Land Town Square or Kingwood Town Center, you’re selling to people who are already out. Shoppers, office workers, families at a weekend event, hotel guests.

What matters there:

  • Your Google Maps pin and photos. People standing on the sidewalk search “coffee near me” and pick from the map. Accurate pin placement and a street-view photo of your door matter more than you’d think when you’re tucked behind a fountain.
  • District events. Holiday events, concerts and weekend markets in these districts bring crowds that aren’t regulars. Post the day before (“open until 9 during the event, hot chocolate by the door”) and extend hours when it pays.
  • Parking reality. If customers have to use a garage, say which one and how long it takes to walk. It reduces “too hard to get there” friction.
  • Neighbor tenants. Salons, boutiques and fitness studios nearby are natural partners for a punch card swap or a morning pastry drop.

Strip-center shops along Woodlands Parkway, Kuykendahl or 1488 win differently. Drive-by visibility, a sign that’s readable at 40 mph, and being the obvious stop on the way to work or school. The morning commute and the school run

Coffee is a habit business. a Toast consumer survey fielded in September 2025 found about 90% of respondents buy coffee out at least once a week, 36% buy three to five times a week, and 16% buy every single morning. The same survey found brand loyalty was the top reason for only 1% of people. Convenience and habit win.

In the Houston suburbs, habit follows the commute and the school run. Parents drop off at a Conroe ISD or Tomball ISD campus, then head to I-45, the Grand Parkway or the Hardy Toll Road. If you’re on that route and they know you can get them in and out in four minutes, you’re in the habit. If they have to turn left across three lanes, you’re not.

Practical moves:

  • Open early enough. If the nearest elementary drop-off ends at 7:45, a 7:30 opening misses most of it. Check your nearest campuses’ bell schedules.
  • Run order-ahead with a pickup shelf, and say “order ahead, skip the line” on the door, on Google and in every post for a month.
  • Schedule ads for the morning. A small Meta or Google budget from 6 to 9 a.m. inside a three-mile radius is a cheap way to stay top of mind with commuters.
  • Fix the slow afternoon with a reason. An after-school deal for kids, a 2 p.m. cold brew special in summer, or a remote-worker table.

We’ve run breakfast-daypart marketing for Toasted Yolk, and the pattern holds: morning customers decide fast and come back on routine, so being easy beats being clever.

Google Business Profile: menu, photos, hours and categories

For a bakery or coffee shop, the Google Business Profile is the front window for everyone who hasn’t walked past yet. Most we look at are half-finished.

Start with the menu. Food and drink businesses can use Google’s menu editor to add sections, items, descriptions and prices, and changes can take 24 to 48 hours to show on Maps and Search, per Google’s menu editor help page. You can upload a photo or PDF of the menu, and if Google finds menus from several sources, you can pick your preferred one. Pick yours. A third-party delivery menu with marked-up prices showing as your “official” menu costs you walk-ins.

Two bakery-specific things. Seasonal items should show up the week they launch: king cake in January, pan de muerto in late October, pumpkin anything in September. And holiday hours need updating as special hours before Thanksgiving and Christmas, when “is it open” searches spike and a wrong answer means a family drove across Spring for nothing.

Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the rest of local ranking. For a bakery the short version is photos, reviews, accurate hours and a category that matches what you mostly sell (Bakery, Coffee shop, Cake shop, Donut shop or Café as primary, and the others as secondary only if true).

Instagram and TikTok content that sells pastries

Food is the easiest content category there is, and most bakeries still post the same flat-lay of a croissant on marble. The content that brings people in shows motion, timing and a reason to come today.

TikTok has made local discovery more direct. In February 2026 it launched an opt-in Local Feed in the U.S. that ranks posts about nearby dining, events and shopping by location, topic and timing, per TechCrunch. The feed is limited to users 18 and older who share precise location. If your videos are tagged to your location and posted when things are happening (“fresh batch at 3,” “the Saturday line moves fast, here’s proof”), they have a better shot at reaching people within driving distance.

A workable posting rhythm for a two-person shop:

  • Three short videos a week, filmed on a phone: something coming out of the oven, a cake decorating time-lapse, a regular’s order being made.
  • One Story a day with what’s in the case right now, or what’s sold out.
  • One finished custom cake post a week, with the occasion and the lead time in the caption (“booked 10 days out, starts at $X for 20 servings”).

Use the same videos on Instagram Reels and TikTok, minus watermarks. Our platform comparison for local businesses goes deeper on which platform suits which audience; for most bakeries in the Houston suburbs, Instagram carries the custom orders and TikTok brings the new walk-ins.

Custom cake orders: the high-margin funnel

Custom cakes are where independent bakeries make real money, and where they lose the most through slow replies. A mom planning a sixth birthday in Cypress messages four bakeries. The first to answer with a price range and an open date usually wins.

How we’d build it:

  1. A custom order page with “starting at” prices by size or servings, lead times, a gallery, and an inquiry form that asks for date, servings, flavor, design idea and budget. The budget field saves everyone time.
  2. A reply standard. Answer every inquiry within a few hours during business hours, even if it’s “got it, quote by tonight.” Set an auto-reply on Instagram DMs that points to the form.
  3. Deposits. Take a deposit to hold the date. It turns a maybe into a booking and cuts no-shows.
  4. Search ads for high-intent terms. “Custom cakes The Woodlands,” “birthday cake near me,” “wedding cake Conroe,” “smash cake Spring TX,” “gender reveal cake Katy.” Negatives: recipe, how to, Walmart, Costco, HEB, cake mix, classes, jobs.
  5. Follow-up after pickup. Ask for a review and a photo from the party. Party photos with real kids and real tables make better portfolio posts than studio shots (get permission before posting).

A Google Ads test for custom cakes doesn’t need to be big: $500 to $1,000 a month in a tight radius is enough to learn (illustrative, and it depends on how many orders you can take). Our speed-to-lead guide covers why a fast reply often matters more than the ad itself.

Catering and office orders

The Woodlands has a lot of offices: Research Forest, Hughes Landing, the towers around Lake Robbins and Timberloch, plus medical offices along I-45. Every one has someone ordering breakfast for a Monday meeting or a client visit. Office managers want one thing: an order that shows up on time, with an invoice they can expense.

What makes you the easy choice:

  • A catering page with boxed packages and per-person prices (“breakfast box for 10, pastries, fruit, coffee for 10”), minimum order, lead time and delivery area.
  • Online ordering with invoicing or a company card on file. Phone tag loses office orders.
  • Drop-offs. Bring a sample box to the front desks of nearby offices and medical practices with a menu and a QR code. It’s old-fashioned and it works.
  • A reorder reminder. If a company orders every quarter for board meetings, a reminder email two weeks ahead is easy money.

One tax detail worth asking your CPA about: Texas Comptroller Publication 94-117 says bakery items sold without plates or eating utensils aren’t subject to Texas sales tax, while ready-to-eat food generally is. Catering packages with plates, coffee service and setup can be taxed differently from a box of muffins. Price your quotes accordingly.

Loyalty apps and the regulars you already have

Regulars are the cheapest marketing a coffee shop has. A loyalty program just makes sure you can reach them again.

Point-of-sale companies have numbers on this, with the usual vendor caveat. Toast’s 2026 loyalty data reports that enrolling a guest in loyalty raised return rates from about 7% to nearly 30% in its own platform study, and that more than half of diners in a 2026 survey left a program because rewards were too hard to earn. Treat the exact figures as a vendor’s, and the direction as believable.

What we’d set up:

  • Use the loyalty built into your POS (Square, Toast, Clover) before buying a separate app. One system means one customer list.
  • Keep the reward close. Something like “every 8th drink free” beats a points system nobody can calculate.
  • Collect phone and email at sign-up, then actually use them: new seasonal items, holiday preorder deadlines, a slow-day offer.
  • Preorders for Thanksgiving pies and Christmas cookies go to loyalty members first. It fills your production schedule early.

Local events and farmers markets

Farmers markets are where a lot of Houston bakeries started, and they’re still one of the best ways to get a product into new mouths.

In The Woodlands, the farmers market runs Saturdays at the Grogan’s Mill shopping center, 7 Switchbud Place, sponsored by the Grogan’s Mill Village Association. Per The Woodlands Township’s listing, hours are 9 a.m. to 1 p.m. from October through June and 9 a.m. to noon in July through September, rain or shine. Tomball, Conroe, Kingwood and Houston’s inner-loop neighborhoods all have their own markets with different crowds.

Make a market booth do marketing work:

  • A QR code on the table that goes to your custom order page and loyalty sign-up.
  • Samples of the thing you most want people to come back for, not just whatever’s left.
  • A “market regulars” email or text: what you’re bringing this Saturday, with preorder pickup at the booth.
  • Photos and video from the booth for Sunday’s posts.

School carnivals, HOA events, church fundraisers and youth sports tournaments in Spring and Magnolia are worth a look too. A donated cookie tray with your name on it reaches families who will need a birthday cake within the year.

Texas cottage food law for home bakers after the 2025 changes

Texas changed its cottage food law in 2025, and the changes matter for anyone baking from home in Montgomery or Harris County.

Under SB 541, effective September 1, 2025, the annual gross income cap for a cottage food operation rose from $50,000 to $150,000, with the state adjusting it annually for inflation using CPI-U. According to the Texas DSHS cottage food page, operators can now sell most foods except a list of exclusions (meat, poultry, seafood, ice cream and other frozen ice products, low-acid canned goods, raw milk products, and CBD or THC products). Foods that need time and temperature control for safety are allowed only if the operation registers with DSHS. Local governments can no longer require permits or fees for cottage food production or direct sales.

The marketing-relevant parts:

  • Online sales are fine, shipping isn’t. The bill struck mail order. Internet sales are allowed when you, an employee or a household member personally deliver the food. So your ads and website should target the area you can actually drive to.
  • Labels go online first. For online sales, required labeling information has to be posted on your website before the customer pays. Every product page needs it.
  • The disclosure. Labels must include a statement that the product was made in a private residence not subject to governmental licensing or inspection. Don’t hide it; customers who buy from home bakers expect it.
  • Training. Operators need an accredited food handler course.
  • A registration number can replace your home address on labels if you register with DSHS. Good for privacy if you’re posting your work publicly.

Home bakers can run Instagram, TikTok, Google Ads and a Google Business Profile like any other business. If you deliver and don’t want your address public, you can set the profile up as a service-area business with no address shown.

Delivery apps vs. direct ordering for bakeries

Delivery apps bring new customers and take a big slice. Per DoorDash’s merchant pricing page, DoorDash Marketplace delivery commissions run 15%, 25% or 30% depending on the plan, with pickup orders at 6%.

Bakeries have a few quirks that make apps less attractive than they are for a burger place. Cakes and delicate pastries don’t travel well with a stranger driving. Morning coffee orders are small, so the percentage hurts more. And the people ordering a dozen cookies for an office are usually happy to pick up or pay a delivery fee to you directly.

What we’d do:

  • Use apps for discovery, with a limited menu of items that travel well.
  • Push direct order-ahead pickup everywhere else: Google, Instagram bio, the door, the bag.
  • Put a card in every app order with a reason to order direct next time.
  • Keep custom cakes and catering off the apps entirely.

We ran the full commission math, platform options and pricing parity questions in our direct ordering vs. delivery apps post, and the same logic applies to cafés.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more customers for my bakery in The Woodlands or Houston?

Finish your Google Business Profile with the menu, fresh photos and accurate hours, then post short videos of what’s coming out of the oven. Build a custom order page with starting prices and a fast reply process, since custom cakes carry the best margins. Add a catering page for nearby offices, a simple loyalty program, and small morning ads around your shop during commute hours.

What is the Texas cottage food sales limit in 2025 and 2026?

Senate Bill 541 raised the annual gross income limit for Texas cottage food operations from $50,000 to $150,000, effective September 1, 2025, and the state adjusts it each year for inflation. Operators can sell most foods except listed exclusions such as meat, seafood and ice cream, and time and temperature controlled foods require DSHS registration. Mail-order shipping isn’t allowed; personal delivery is.

Should a coffee shop use DoorDash and Uber Eats?

They can help with discovery, but the fees are steep for small coffee orders. DoorDash Marketplace charges 15%, 25% or 30% on delivery depending on the plan, and 6% on pickup. Most coffee shops do better listing a short menu of items that travel well on apps while pushing direct order-ahead pickup through Google, Instagram and in-store signs.

How do bakeries get more custom cake orders?

Show your work, show your prices and answer fast. Post a finished custom cake each week with the lead time, build a custom order page with starting prices and an inquiry form that asks for date, servings and budget, and reply within a few hours. Search ads for terms like custom cakes or birthday cake near me in a tight radius around your shop work well.

What should a bakery put on its Google Business Profile?

Pick a primary category that matches what you mostly sell, such as Bakery, Coffee shop or Cake shop. Add your menu through Google’s menu editor with prices, upload fresh photos of the case, drinks, storefront and parking, set accurate hours plus special holiday hours, and add order-ahead and custom order links. Reply to reviews and post seasonal items the week they launch.
