Marketing Beverage and Supplement Brands With Creators (Without Getting in Trouble)

Creator marketing for beverage and supplement brands: FTC disclosures, health claim substantiation, FDA structure/function rules, alcohol limits and ROI.

Thursday afternoon. A creator you seeded two weeks ago posts a 40-second TikTok about your magnesium gummies. It’s great. Natural, funny, 300,000 views by dinner. Somewhere around second 28 she says they “basically cured” her anxiety, and the only disclosure is the platform’s paid partnership label under the caption.

Your growth lead wants to put $5,000 behind it as a Spark Ad tonight. Your co-founder wants to know whether that’s a problem.

It is. Two problems, actually, and they’re the two that sink most beverage and supplement creator programs: the claim and the disclosure. Both are easy to get right if you build them into the brief instead of finding them in the comments.

To market a beverage or supplement brand with creators without getting in trouble, disclose every material connection clearly inside the content itself, keep creators to claims your company can substantiate (structure/function language for supplements, never disease claims), follow the extra rules for caffeine and alcohol, and check platform ad policies before you turn organic posts into paid ads. Then measure creator spend like any other channel.

We ran a 1,200-creator launch for Vital Proteins and creator bursts for Celsius, so this is the checklist we actually work from. It isn’t legal advice; your regulatory counsel signs off on claims.

The FTC rules: free product counts, and the label isn’t enough

The FTC’s Endorsement Guides were revised in 2023, and the agency’s plain-English FTC’s Endorsement Guides FAQ answers most of the questions brands ask. The parts that matter most for CPG:

  • Free product is a material connection. It doesn’t matter whether you mailed it, gave a discount code or handed it out at an event. If a creator got your product free and posts about it, they need to disclose that.
  • Platform tools may not be enough. The FAQ says it’s “always best to add your own disclosure” even when a platform offers a paid partnership label.
  • Video needs disclosure in the video. A disclosure shown on screen and said out loud is more likely to be clear and conspicuous. A hashtag buried at the end of a caption is easy to miss.
  • Put it up front. A disclosure in the middle or at the end of a post is less likely to work.
  • Brands have to train and monitor. Advertisers need “reasonable programs” to train and monitor the people promoting them, and health claims call for closer oversight.

The FTC has shown it means this for food and beverage. In November 2023 it sent warning letters to the American Beverage Association, the Canadian Sugar Institute and a dozen dietitians and influencers over paid TikTok and Instagram posts about aspartame and sugar. None of the videos the FTC reviewed had a disclosure inside the video, tags like “#ad” and “Paid partnership” required scrolling to see, and some posts named the sponsor only as “ameribev.” The letters noted penalties of up to $50,120 per violation, per Keller and Heckman’s write-up.

If you collect reviews on your site or Amazon, the FTC’s consumer reviews and testimonials rule (effective October 2024) also bans buying fake reviews and undisclosed insider reviews. Seeded creators who leave product reviews count. Our FTC influencer rules post and fake reviews rule explainer cover both in more depth.

Health claims: what a creator can say about your product

Here’s the part brands underestimate. A creator saying something about your product in a post you paid for, shipped product for or turned into an ad is legally your claim. If you can’t back it up, they can’t say it.

The FTC standard. In December 2022 the FTC replaced its 1998 supplement advertising guide with the Health Products Compliance Guidance. Health claims need “competent and reliable scientific evidence,” which generally means randomized, controlled human clinical trials. The same standard applies whether the product is a food, a beverage or a supplement, and it covers social media and influencer marketing. A “clinically tested ingredient” claim implies the finished product delivers the benefit at the dose you sell, not just that some study exists on the ingredient. That’s from Covington’s summary of the guidance.

The FTC followed up in April 2023 by sending notices of penalty offenses to roughly 670 marketers of supplements, functional foods, OTC drugs and homeopathic products. The letters don’t accuse anyone of anything. They do mean a company that later makes unsupported claims can face civil penalties.

The FDA layer for supplements. Per FDA’s structure/function claims page, a supplement can make structure/function claims (how a nutrient affects the normal structure or function of the body, like “calcium builds strong bones”) without FDA approval. The company must have substantiation, must notify FDA of the claim within 30 days of marketing it, and the label must carry the disclaimer: “This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.” Disease claims aren’t allowed for supplements at all, because only drugs can make them. Conventional foods and beverages can make structure/function claims tied to nutritive value without notifying FDA.

Creators don’t think in those categories. They think in stories: “my joints stopped hurting,” “I haven’t been sick all winter,” “this fixed my bloating.” Each of those drifts toward a disease or treatment claim. That’s why the brief needs an approved claims list in plain language, and a do-not-say list next to it.

Energy drinks and caffeine: extra care with audience and dose

Energy drinks get more scrutiny than almost any other beverage, mostly because of who’s watching. The FDA says 400 milligrams a day is an amount not generally associated with negative effects in healthy adults, that energy drinks generally run 54 to 328 milligrams per 16 ounces, and that medical experts advise against energy drinks for children and teens.

Mainstream brands that belong to the trade group follow the American Beverage Association’s energy drink guidance: no marketing to children under 12, no selling or marketing in K-12 schools, a label advisory that the product isn’t recommended for children, pregnant or nursing women or people sensitive to caffeine, total caffeine from all sources declared on the label, and no promoting mixing with alcohol.

For a creator program, that turns into a few working rules:

  • Check each creator’s audience age breakdown before you sign. A gaming or dance creator whose audience skews young is the wrong partner, however good the views look.
  • No content filmed at or about schools, and no high school athletes.
  • No “drink three before the game” bits. Show one can, in a normal adult context.
  • No mixing with alcohol on camera.
  • Restrict paid amplification to adults even where the platform would let you go younger.

Performance and focus claims (“clean energy,” “helps you push through a workout”) still need support. If your can has 200 milligrams of caffeine, “energy” is easy to substantiate. “Burns fat” is a much harder claim, and it pulls you into weight-loss ad policies too.

Alcohol is a different rulebook

If you make beer, wine, spirits, hard seltzer or canned cocktails, the federal regulator for advertising is the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB), not just the FTC. TTB’s advertising rules apply to social media, and its current guidance is Industry Circular 2024-1, published November 1, 2024. It says brands can satisfy mandatory statements (name, address, class designation) through links or link-sharing pages, and that influencers can tag a compliant brand page to meet those requirements. The full list is on TTB’s industry circulars page.

The industry codes add audience rules. The Beer Institute and Brewers Association require placements where at least 73.6% of the audience is of legal drinking age, and DISCUS raised the spirits standard to 73.8% in 2023. In practice that means every creator needs audience data showing about three-quarters of followers are 21 or older, checked before contract.

Platforms layer on more:

  • Meta sets its floor at 18, so U.S. alcohol brands have to set 21+ targeting themselves on ads and Pages, per Sprout Social’s rundown.
  • TikTok opened U.S. alcohol ads in 2024 with targeting to users 25 and older, per Marketing Brew. TikTok’s alcohol ad policy requires a responsible drinking message on the ad and landing page, and bars featuring anyone under 25, pregnant people, excessive drinking, or alcohol as a prize. Creator content for alcohol began running on TikTok by early 2026, per Fast Company. Cast creators 25 and up for TikTok alcohol work.
  • Texas has its own rules through TABC, including limits on suppliers paying for advertising that promotes a specific retailer. Our retail sell-through post covers how that affects “find it at” ads.

Non-alcoholic brands don’t carry these rules, but a mocktail brand that films creators in a bar setting may still get reviewed like an alcohol ad. Keep the context unambiguous.

Meta and TikTok ad policies for supplements

Organic posts and paid ads face different reviewers. A creator post that’s fine on its own can get rejected the moment you boost it.

  • Meta bans ads for products on its unsafe supplements policy list, which includes anabolic steroids, chitosan, comfrey, DHEA, ephedra and human growth hormone. Its health and wellness policy restricts weight-loss and dietary supplement ads to people 18 and older and prohibits content that makes people feel bad about their appearance. Before-and-after weight shots are where most supplement ads get flagged.
  • TikTok allows supplements in the U.S. subject to age restrictions and policy compliance, according to its weight management policy. Weight-loss or muscle-gain claims must target 18 and older, and ads can’t claim the product alone, without diet or exercise, causes results, or that results are easy or guaranteed. Body-shaming and “perfect body” framing is prohibited.

Our rule: write the paid version of the brief first. If the creator’s content would pass as an ad, it’ll pass as an organic post. The reverse isn’t true.

Running a seeding program that stays clean

Seeding is how most beverage and supplement brands start with creators: send product to a lot of people, see who posts, pay the ones whose content works. It’s also where disclosure falls apart, because nobody signed anything.

How we structure it:

  1. Tier the creators. Nano (under 10,000 followers) for volume and authenticity, micro for niche credibility (dietitians, trainers, run clubs), macro for reach on launch day. Our micro vs. macro post and creator vetting guide go deeper.
  2. Put a one-page note in every box. What the product is, the approved claims, the do-not-say list, and a plain request to disclose (“If you post, please say #gifted or #ad at the start and out loud in videos.”).
  3. Get usage rights in writing before paying anyone. The Vital Proteins program had usage rights built into the nano-to-macro tiers from the start, which is what made scaling the winners as ads possible.
  4. Monitor what goes live. Check every post within 48 hours. If a claim or a missing disclosure shows up, ask for an edit. Keep a record that you asked.
  5. Move the best creators to paid, always-on contracts. Monthly content drops beat one-off posts for both performance and compliance, because the creator learns your rules.

A sample brief for a hydration powder, to show the level of specificity we mean:

Brief fieldExample
Hook ideasPost-run in July heat on the Waterway, a mid-morning slump at a desk, a youth soccer sideline (adult drinking, not the kids)
Approved claims“Electrolytes to help you rehydrate,” “1,000 mg sodium per stick,” “no added sugar”
Do not sayCures cramps, treats dehydration illness, better than an IV, safe for kids, any medical condition
Disclosure“#ad” in the first line, spoken in the first five seconds, plus the platform’s paid partnership label
Usage rightsPaid ads for 90 days, organic reposts for 12 months

The claims in that example are placeholders. Your own approved list comes from your label, your substantiation file and your counsel.

Whitelisting: partnership ads and Spark Ads

Whitelisting means running paid ads through a creator’s handle, so the ad shows their name and face instead of your logo. It’s usually where creator programs start paying for themselves.

  • Meta partnership ads run on Facebook and Instagram with both the creator and the brand on the ad. The creator grants permission through Meta’s branded content tools; Instagram’s partnership ads help page covers the process.
  • TikTok Spark Ads let you boost a creator’s actual post. Per TikTok’s Spark Ads instructions, the creator turns on ad authorization, generates a code, and picks an authorization period of 7, 30, 60 or 365 days. You paste the code into Ads Manager under Tools, then Creative library, then Spark ads posts.

Two compliance points people miss. A whitelisted ad is your ad, so it has to pass the claims review and the platform’s ad policy, including the age restrictions above. And the disclosure inside the video has to survive the edit; if you trim the first five seconds for a tighter hook, you may have cut the spoken “ad.”

For Vital Proteins, whitelisted creator ads through Spark and partnership formats scaled the winning posts and returned 6.2x ROAS. For Celsius, creator and CTV bursts were timed to in-store promos and endcap resets, so the paid creator content pushed people toward shelves that were already stocked. Our paid social team runs both formats.

Measuring creator ROI without fooling yourself

Creator programs produce a lot of numbers, and most of them flatter. Earned media value is the worst offender. It’s a modeled estimate of what the impressions would have cost as ads, useful for comparing creators to each other and not much else.

Numbers that hold up:

MetricFormulaUse it for
Cost per thousand views (organic)Creator fees + product cost ÷ (views ÷ 1,000)Comparing creators and tiers
Creator ad ROASAttributed revenue ÷ ad spend on whitelisted postsDeciding which posts to scale
Fully loaded ROASAttributed revenue ÷ (ad spend + fees + product + management)Judging the program as a whole
Hook rate3-second views ÷ impressionsSpotting creative worth testing as ads
Code and link revenueRevenue on creator codes and tracked linksA floor on direct sales, never the full picture
Incremental liftExposed group sales ÷ holdout group sales − 1Proving the program grows the business

If you sell mostly at retail, add the store metrics from our sell-through guide: locator visits, retailer cart clicks and velocity in markets where creators ran versus markets where they didn’t.

The Vital Proteins numbers show the pattern. Seeding to more than 1,200 creators produced 480 million organic and paid impressions and $14.6 million in earned media value, and the number we’d actually defend in a budget meeting is the 6.2x ROAS on creator ads. Our post on measuring influencer ROI has the full framework, and UGC vs. influencers helps if you’re deciding between the two.

Your pre-flight checklist

Before any creator content goes live or gets amplified, run it through this:

  • Is there a disclosure at the start, on screen and spoken, in addition to the platform label?
  • Is every claim on the approved list, and is there substantiation in the file for it?
  • Any disease, cure, treatment or prevention language? Cut it.
  • For energy drinks: is the creator’s audience overwhelmingly adult, with no school settings and no mixing with alcohol?
  • For alcohol: is the audience at least about 74% 21+, is the creator 25+ for TikTok, and are the TTB mandatory statements reachable?
  • Does the paid version pass Meta and TikTok ad policy, including 18+ targeting for weight and supplement claims?
  • Do you have usage rights in writing for the way you plan to use it?

Back to Thursday’s TikTok. The fix is a polite note asking the creator to re-cut without the “cured my anxiety” line, with a spoken disclosure at the top, then running the new version as a Spark Ad. It costs you a day. The alternative could cost a lot more.

Frequently asked questions

Do influencers have to disclose free products?

Yes. Under the FTC’s Endorsement Guides, free product is a material connection, whether it arrived in a seeding box, through a discount code or at an event. Creators should disclose it clearly at the start of the post, and in videos both on screen and out loud. The FTC recommends adding your own disclosure even when the platform offers a paid partnership label, so build that into every brief.

Can supplement influencers make health claims?

Only claims the brand can substantiate. The FTC’s 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance generally expects randomized, controlled human trials for health claims, and that applies to creator posts the brand pays for or amplifies. Supplements can make structure/function claims, such as supporting normal joint function, with the FDA disclaimer and notification. Disease claims like curing anxiety or preventing colds are not allowed for supplements.

What is the FDA supplement disclaimer?

Supplements making structure/function claims must carry this statement: This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. The company must also have substantiation for the claim and notify FDA within 30 days of first marketing it. Conventional foods and beverages making similar claims do not need the disclaimer.

Can alcohol brands work with influencers on TikTok and Instagram?

Yes, with limits. TTB’s Industry Circular 2024-1 covers alcohol advertising on social media, including how influencers can meet mandatory statement rules by tagging a compliant brand page. Industry codes require audiences that are roughly 74 percent legal drinking age. Meta requires brands to set 21-plus targeting in the U.S., and TikTok limits U.S. alcohol ads to users 25 and older and bars featuring anyone under 25.

How do you measure ROI on creator marketing for a CPG brand?

Track creator ad ROAS on whitelisted posts, fully loaded ROAS including fees and product, cost per thousand views by creator tier, and code and link revenue as a floor. For retail brands, add store locator visits and velocity in markets where creators ran compared with markets where they did not. Treat earned media value as a comparison tool between creators, not as revenue.
