$249 or $40,000. Both are real quotes for “a logo” in Houston, and both can be the right price.

The gap comes from what’s attached to the logo. A contest file is a mark. A brand identity is a mark plus the rules that make every truck, sign, menu and Instagram post look like the same company. A full rebrand is all that plus strategy, naming help and the cost of swapping it onto everything you own.

The short answer: in Houston, a logo alone typically costs $250 to $3,000, a brand identity (logo, colors, type, guidelines) $5,000 to $25,000, and a full rebrand with strategy and rollout $25,000 to $100,000 or more. Filing fees are extra: $350 per class for a federal trademark and $25 for a Texas assumed name certificate.

Below: what each tier should include, how long it takes, what the trademark and DBA steps cost, and where Houston and The Woodlands businesses tend to overpay.

For context on the people doing the work, graphic designers nationally earned a median of $62,960 a year in May 2025, and about 20% were self-employed, per the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook. A $3,000 logo from a solo designer is roughly a week or two of their paid time once you count the calls, the concepts and the revisions. That’s a reasonable price for skilled work, and it’s why a $150 logo usually means someone skipped the thinking.

Logo only, brand identity or full rebrand: which one do you need?

Most businesses buy the wrong tier, usually in one of two directions. A new food truck in Katy pays an agency $20,000 for a brand book it will never open. A 30-year HVAC company in Conroe with 14 vans buys a $300 logo and then pays a sign shop to “fix it” every time it goes on something new.

Our rough guide:

  • Logo only makes sense when you’re testing an idea, opening a single small location, or you know the brand will change once you have customers. Keep the scope small and the files clean.
  • Brand identity fits most established local businesses: a dental group in The Woodlands, a law practice near the Galleria, a home services company with a fleet. You need the logo to work on a truck, a polo shirt, a favicon and a billboard on I-45, and you need a document so the next vendor gets it right.
  • Full rebrand is for a real change in the business: a merger, a new name, moving upmarket, or a reputation you need to leave behind. If the reason is “we’re tired of it,” read how to rebrand without losing customers first.

How long does branding take?

Timelines slip for the same reason every time: the client side. Feedback rounds that should take three days take three weeks because the decision-maker is traveling, or because six people have opinions and nobody has the final say.

For a brand identity, plan on six to ten weeks if one person approves each stage within a few days. A full rebrand with research, naming and a new website runs three to six months, and the rollout (signs, vehicles, uniforms) often runs past that.

Name one decision-maker before the kickoff. You can still ask your team and your best customers for input, but one person signs off.

Trademark and DBA costs in Texas: what to budget

A logo isn’t protected just because you paid for it. Here’s what the filings cost and what each one actually does. We’re a marketing agency, not a law firm, so treat this as a budget guide and talk to a trademark attorney before you file.

Assumed name (DBA). If your LLC or corporation does business under a name other than its legal one, it files an assumed name certificate with the Texas Secretary of State. The fee is $25, and the certificate lasts up to ten years, per the Texas Secretary of State’s Form 503 instructions. Sole proprietors and general partnerships file with the county clerk instead, according to the Secretary of State’s name filing FAQs. In Houston that’s the Harris County Clerk at 201 Caroline Street, and the certificate is valid for ten years, per the City of Houston’s Office of Business Opportunity. A sole proprietor in The Woodlands would file with the Montgomery County Clerk.

The important part: a DBA doesn’t give you rights to the name. The SOS instructions say the filing doesn’t count as use of the name for priority purposes, and the office doesn’t check it against other names on file.

Texas state trademark. Registering a mark with the Texas Secretary of State costs $50 per class on Form 901. It’s inexpensive and it covers Texas only.

Federal trademark. Since January 18, 2025, the USPTO base application fee is $350 per class of goods or services. There’s a $200 per-class surcharge if you write your own description instead of picking from the ID Manual, $200 more for each additional 1,000 characters of that free-form text, and a $100 surcharge for insufficient information. If you file before you’re using the mark, the statement of use is another $150 per class, and the Section 8 declaration of continued use, due between the fifth and sixth year, is $325 per class. All per the USPTO’s fee summary.

As of November 5, 2026, the USPTO reported an average of four months to first examination and ten months total pendency, per the USPTO’s wait-time page. That’s the reason to run a clearance search before the designer starts, not after the signs are ordered.

A practical budget for a small local business with one service class: about $350 in USPTO fees if you file cleanly, plus attorney time for the search and filing if you use one (fees vary a lot, so get a flat quote). Two or three classes multiply the government fee.

Who owns your logo? The contract line Houston businesses miss

This one costs people real money. Copyright in a design starts with the person who created it. If an employee designs your logo as part of their job, the business owns it. If a freelancer or outside studio designs it, the rules change.

Under U.S. Copyright Office Circular 30, a commissioned work from an outside creator only counts as a “work made for hire” if it falls into one of nine specific categories: a contribution to a collective work, part of a motion picture or audiovisual work, a translation, a supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer material for a test, or an atlas. A standalone logo isn’t on that list. So a contract that just says “work for hire” may not do what you think.

What we’d want in any branding contract: a signed written assignment of all rights in the final logo and identity to your business, effective on final payment. Also the master files, a statement that the designer used no licensed stock elements or fonts you’d need to keep paying for, and the font license details. Have your attorney review the language. This is the same kind of problem as getting your domain back from a web designer, and it’s much easier to solve before the project starts.

What Houston businesses overpay for in branding

We see the same overspending patterns across Houston, Spring and The Woodlands. Some of it is vendors upselling. A lot of it is owners buying confidence instead of decisions.

  • Twenty concepts. More options feel like more value. In practice the designer spends the hours sketching instead of refining, and you end up choosing by committee. Two or three strong directions shown on a real truck, sign and Instagram grid beat twenty logos floating on white.
  • A strategy deck nobody uses. Research is worth paying for when it ends in decisions: who you’re for, what you say first, what you stop saying. A 60-page PDF of personas that never changes a headline is an expensive bookmark.
  • Paying twice for files. A cheap logo delivered only as a JPG means paying a sign shop, then an embroiderer, then a printer to rebuild it. Each one charges a setup or vectorizing fee, and each version drifts a little.
  • Fonts you don’t own. A designer picks a licensed typeface, and two years later your web developer finds out the license doesn’t cover the website.
  • Trademark packages bundled into design. Some vendors sell “trademark protection” add-ons. Get filing help from an attorney who can tell you whether the mark is clear, and compare the price to the government fee above.

The rollout is where the real money goes, and it’s often left out of the branding budget completely. New signs, vehicle wraps, uniforms, printed forms, the website and your Google Business Profile photos all have to change. A ten-van fleet rewrap can cost more than the identity work itself. Sign approvals differ depending on where the building sits: inside The Woodlands (covenants and the Township’s design standards), inside an incorporated city like Shenandoah or Conroe, or in unincorporated Harris County. Our business sign rules guide explains who approves what.

Phase the rollout. Website, social profiles and Google Business Profile go first, because they’re cheap and they’re where most customers see you. Signs and vehicles follow on their normal replacement cycle unless the old brand is actively hurting you. Keep your phone number, your business name in Google, and your reviews tied to the same profile, so a new look doesn’t reset what customers already trust.

How to compare branding proposals in Houston and The Woodlands

Ask every designer or agency the same questions, in writing:

  1. How many concepts do we see, and how many revision rounds are included?
  2. What exactly is in the final file package? (Compare it to the checklist above.)
  3. Is there a written assignment of rights on final payment, and are fonts and any stock elements licensed to us?
  4. Who runs the trademark clearance search, and when?
  5. Is rollout planning included, or only the design?
  6. Who on their team does the work, and can we see three identities they’ve done for local businesses at a similar size?

A cheaper proposal that answers all six well is often a better deal than a pricier one with vague answers. Price per concept is a poor yardstick; you only need one good one.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a logo cost for a small business in Houston?

Most small businesses in Houston pay between $500 and $3,000 for a logo from a freelance designer, with online contests starting around $249. That usually buys two or three concepts, a few revision rounds and the files you need. If you also need colors, fonts, templates and a guidelines document so every vendor uses the brand the same way, budget $5,000 to $25,000 for a full brand identity.

How much does it cost to trademark a logo in Texas?

A federal trademark application with the USPTO costs $350 per class of goods or services, with surcharges if you write your own description. A Texas state trademark through the Secretary of State costs $50 per class but only covers Texas. Attorney fees for the clearance search and filing are extra and vary. The USPTO reported about ten months average total pendency as of November 2026.

Does a DBA protect my business name in Texas?

No. An assumed name certificate is a public notice of who is behind a business name. The Texas Secretary of State says the filing does not establish priority to the name and the office does not check for conflicts. LLCs and corporations file with the Secretary of State for $25, while sole proprietors file with their county clerk. For real protection, look into a trademark.

How long does a rebrand take for a business in The Woodlands?

A brand identity project usually takes six to ten weeks when one decision-maker approves each stage quickly. A full rebrand with research, naming, messaging and a new website typically runs three to six months. Replacing signs, vehicle wraps and uniforms can take longer, and sign approvals in The Woodlands go through Township covenant review, so start that process early in the plan.

Do I own my logo if I paid a designer for it?

Not automatically. Copyright starts with the creator, and a standalone logo from an outside designer generally does not qualify as a work made for hire under federal copyright rules. Get a signed written assignment of all rights to your business on final payment, plus the master files and font license details. Have an attorney review the contract before the project starts.
