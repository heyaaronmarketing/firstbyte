“Can we just stick a few signs out by the road for the grand opening?”

We hear some version of that a lot, usually a week before they open, usually holding a box of 18-by-24 corrugated signs they already paid for. The short answer in The Woodlands is no. The short answer in Houston is also no. The longer answer is more useful, because a lot of signs are allowed, and the businesses that know the rules get more out of their storefront than the ones who learn them from a code officer.

This is a working guide to business sign rules in The Woodlands, with the City of Houston, unincorporated Harris County and the state highway rules alongside for comparison. We’re marketers, not lawyers, and the rules below come straight from the documents we link. Read the source before you print anything, and treat this as the map rather than the permit.

First question: who controls the ground your sign stands on?

Most sign confusion north of Houston comes from one fact. The Woodlands isn’t a city.

It’s a special-purpose district run by The Woodlands Township, and voters chose to keep it that way in November 2021, per the Township’s incorporation page. Roads and policing are handled by Montgomery and Harris counties, as ABC13 explained before that vote. So the Township doesn’t write sign ordinances the way Houston does. Commercial property in The Woodlands is governed by recorded covenants and the The Woodlands Commercial Planning and Design Standards, which are deed restrictions that run with the land. the Township’s Covenant Administration page says plainly that the Township “does not enforce ordinances,” but it monitors and enforces residential and commercial covenants.

That gives you four different rulebooks within a 20-minute drive of Market Street:

  • Inside The Woodlands: Township covenants and the Commercial Planning and Design Standards for anything on the lot. County and state law for anything in the road.
  • Shenandoah, Oak Ridge North, Conroe: incorporated cities with their own sign ordinances and their own permit desks. A shop on the I-45 frontage in Shenandoah is not under Township covenants, even if the mailing address says The Woodlands.
  • Spring, Klein, Cypress and other unincorporated Harris County: Harris County permits on-premise signs.
  • City of Houston: the Houston Sign Code and Houston Sign Administration.

Before you design anything, find out which of those you’re in. Your lease, your landlord and your property tax statement will tell you faster than Google will.

The Woodlands commercial sign rules, sign by sign

The The Woodlands Commercial Planning and Design Standards were revised and recorded in August 2026. Community Impact reported in October 2026 that the commercial standards had been through a three-year revision that touched window graphics, among other things. If you’re working from a PDF a landlord emailed you in 2022, get the current one from the Township’s Commercial Standards page.

Article VI holds the sign standards. The general rules in Section 6.3 do most of the work:

  • Approval comes before fabrication. Any temporary or permanent sign visible from the outside of a building “must be Submitted and Approved by the Plan Review Committee prior to fabrication and installation” (6.3.C). Order the sign first and you may own a very expensive piece of plastic.
  • Name only. “Only Business Clarifiers or Business Names are permitted on Signs” (6.3.F), and the name must match across your monument sign, building sign and window graphics (6.3.G). Phone numbers, websites, tag lines and hours aren’t permitted on monument or building signs unless specifically allowed (6.3.L).
  • Watch the words “The Woodlands.” The developer holds trademarks on them. Applications that include those words get forwarded to its representative, who has 20 days to object, and an objection blocks approval (6.3.B). “Woodlands Pilates Co.” needs more lead time than “Juniper Pilates.”
  • No sign spinners, no tree signs. Signs can’t be held by people, placed in trees or mounted on structures not built for signs (6.3.H).

A-frames and menu boards

Good news for restaurants and retail around Hughes Landing and Market Street: A-frames are pre-approved, so you don’t submit them, as long as you follow Section 6.4.D. One per tenant, near the main entrance. No more than 6 square feet. It can’t block foot traffic or accessibility, it can’t be chained down, and it comes inside every night at close. The message is limited to general products or services and special events, with no prices or specific product names, and only the top 12 inches can carry your name or logo. Restaurants also get one pre-approved menu board up to 4 square feet and no taller than five feet including the stand (6.4.E).

That “no prices” rule surprises people. “Happy hour 3-6” is about a special event. “$5 margaritas” is a price. We’d write the first one.

Window signs

Section 6.6.M starts from no. Signs aren’t allowed in exterior windows or doors where they can be seen from outside. The exceptions are small: one “Open” sign per occupied tenant space up to 2 square feet; one white vinyl name graphic up to 2 square feet (two on double doors); and a set of hours, website and emergency info that together can’t exceed 1 square foot. Interior signs should sit about two feet back from the glass so they aren’t primarily visible from outside (6.6.A).

If your Houston location has posters taped to every pane, your Woodlands location won’t.

Temporary signs, opening banners and leasing signs

“Coming Soon” signs, business banners and “Now Open” signs are not pre-approved (6.5). They go through review like everything else. Once approved, Section 6.7 sets the limits:

  • Opening banners can go up no more than four weeks before and come down no later than two weeks after initial opening, can’t exceed 3 feet high by 8 feet wide, and use a single neutral background of white, gray or black (6.7.H). A window graphic version can’t exceed 50 square feet.
  • Temporary signs in general must be on the site they pertain to, can’t carry product, price or other advertising unless specifically allowed, and ground-mounted ones max out at six feet tall and 32 square feet (6.7.A).
  • For Lease signs: one per vacant space per street frontage, two per site at most, 4 square feet each, and gone within seven days of signing the lease (6.7.E).
  • Now Hiring for an established business: one sign up to 4 square feet in a window or door (6.7.I).

Reviews go through Plan Review Committees, and the Development Standards Committee (four Township-appointed residents and three developer appointees) is the only body that can grant variances, per the Township’s Covenant Administration page. Applications go through the Township’s Civic Access Portal, and commercial questions go to Covenant Administration at 281-210-3800. Ask for site-specific sign criteria too (6.3.A); many shopping centers have their own sign criteria layered on top of the Township’s.

Houston sign code basics: permits, contractors and fees

Inside Houston city limits, signs are a permit question first. Houston Sign Administration regulates and permits advertising signs under the Houston Sign Code, with height, size and lighting limits that depend on the street category. Permits are required before signs are erected, altered or repaired, and they generally go to licensed sign contractors.

The numbers that matter for a typical storefront, from Houston’s on-premise sign permit page:

  • Up to five signs per business. One ground or projecting sign per frontage, two total, with more allowed only on frontages over 350 feet. Up to four wall signs and three marquee signs.
  • Off-premises advertising is prohibited. Your sign advertises your business, on your property.
  • Owners can permit and install their own non-electrical signs only in limited cases: ground signs of 60 square feet or less and 8 feet or less in height, or wall signs no higher than 16 feet above grade. Hire anyone else and they need a sign contractor license.
  • Fees start around $69.46 for the minimum operating permit, plus a $33.56 administrative fee and $97.25 each for plan review and inspection, covering the first 32 square feet. Operating permits renew annually.
  • Processing runs 4 to 11 business days. Construction permits are good for 180 days from the site inspection.

Banners are where Houston gets strict. The Sign Administration FAQ calls the code “very banner prohibitive” and says “a banner by itself can’t be permitted.” Back it and frame it and it becomes a wall sign, permitted as such. The only other route is a Limited Use Banner. Per the Limited Use Banner application, it can be displayed for a maximum of seven consecutive days within any 30-day period, can’t exceed 40 square feet, must be attached flush against the building wall with no ropes or ties, can’t flap in the wind, and only one is allowed per premises at a time. Each seven-day run needs its own permit.

The same FAQ lists portable signs, rolling billboards and bandit signs among signs that aren’t allowed, along with anything banned by the attention-getting device ordinance. That ordinance, Section 28-37 in Chapter 28 of the Houston Code, covers banners, pennants, balloons and certain lights. We’d assume the feather flags you see along Westheimer fall under it until Sign Administration tells you otherwise.

Spring, Klein and Cypress: unincorporated Harris County

A lot of businesses with a Spring or Cypress address aren’t in any city. In unincorporated Harris County, the Harris County Engineer’s sign permit page says permits are required for all new and renovated signs, sorted into toll road, scenic district, major thoroughfare and freeway categories. Construction of off-premise signs is prohibited outright. Expect a site plan showing every existing and proposed sign, a survey for new ground signs, 3 to 5 business days for processing and 180 days to finish construction.

If your shopping center along FM 2920 or the Grand Parkway has an owners’ association, it probably has sign criteria too. As in The Woodlands, private restrictions stack on top of public rules. They don’t replace them.

Bandit signs: the cheapest ad that costs the most

Bandit signs are the corrugated plastic signs on stakes that bloom in esplanades every Saturday morning: “We Buy Houses,” “Roofing $0 Down,” “Grand Opening This Way.” They’re illegal on public right-of-way almost everywhere in Texas, and that includes the medians on Woodlands Parkway and Research Forest.

State law is short. Section 393.002 of Chapter 393 of the Texas Transportation Code says a person may not place a sign on the right-of-way of a public road unless state law authorizes it. A sheriff, constable or trained volunteer authorized by the county commissioners court can confiscate those signs (393.003), and signs worth under $25 can be thrown away without the usual notice. Under Section 393.007, a district, county or municipal attorney can sue for a civil penalty of $500 to $1,000 per violation, and each day of a continuing violation can be a separate penalty.

Houston adds its own. Sections 28-38 and 28-39 of Chapter 28 of the Houston Code ban advertising on curbs, sidewalks, utility poles, trees and traffic signs, with fines of $300 to $500 per violation and each day a separate offense. City employees can impound the signs, and lightweight stake signs can be disposed of immediately. a City of Houston committee presentation on bandit signs lists the usual illegal spots: esplanades, the strip between sidewalk and curb, overpasses, utility poles, light standards and signal boxes.

On state highways (I-45, SH 242, the Grand Parkway and their frontage roads), TxDOT’s right-of-way manual says TxDOT may remove unauthorized signs immediately and without notice, store them, and dispose of unclaimed ones. Inside incorporated cities, the city usually handles removal.

Our opinion, from the marketing side: bandit signs are bad advertising even before the fines. The phone number on a stake sign is right there for any code officer to call. A sign in the median says “this company cuts corners” to the exact homeowners you want to hire you. And the response is untrackable, so you’ll never know whether the 200 signs paid for themselves. If you need roadside reach, a permitted billboard is the legitimate version; our Houston billboard cost guide has real numbers.

What we’d do instead of more signs

Signs do one job: they tell people who are already driving past that you exist and where the door is. Build the rest of your local visibility where the rules work in your favor.

  • Google Business Profile, verified before opening day. “Near me” searches start in Maps, not in the median. Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the setup.
  • A sign that matches your profile. The Township requires the same name across your signs. Use that exact name on Google too. Mismatched names confuse customers and complicate verification.
  • Paid social and search around the location. A radius around Hughes Landing or your Spring strip center reaches the same people a banner would, for longer than two weeks. Our paid social team runs opening campaigns like this.
  • Reviews from your first customers. The Google reviews guide has scripts that stay inside Google’s rules.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need approval for a business sign in The Woodlands?

Almost always. The Woodlands Commercial Planning and Design Standards require any temporary or permanent sign visible from outside the building to be submitted and approved by the Plan Review Committee before it is fabricated or installed. A few signs are pre-approved, including one compliant A-frame per tenant and a small restaurant menu board. Grand opening, coming soon and now open banners all need approval.

Can I use an A-frame sign in The Woodlands?

Yes, within limits. Section 6.4.D of the Township standards pre-approves one A-frame per tenant near the main entrance, up to 6 square feet. It cannot block traffic or accessibility, cannot be chained down and must come inside each evening at closing. The message can cover general products, services and special events, but no prices or specific product names, and only the top 12 inches can show your name or logo.

How long can a grand opening banner stay up in The Woodlands?

Under Section 6.7.H of the Township standards, an opening banner can go up no more than four weeks before initial opening and must come down within two weeks after. It cannot exceed 3 feet by 8 feet and must use one neutral background color, white, gray or black. The banner still needs approval first, because coming soon and now open banners are not pre-approved signs.

Do I need a permit for a banner in Houston?

Yes. Houston’s sign code is very restrictive on banners. A banner alone cannot be permitted, though a backed and framed banner can be permitted as a wall sign. Otherwise the only option is a Limited Use Banner permit, which allows one banner up to 40 square feet, mounted flush on the building wall, for up to seven consecutive days in any 30-day period. Each display period needs its own permit.

What is the fine for bandit signs in Houston and Texas?

In Houston, placing advertising signs on curbs, sidewalks, poles or other public property carries a fine of $300 to $500 per violation, and each day counts separately. Texas Transportation Code Chapter 393 bans signs on public road right-of-way statewide and allows a civil penalty of $500 to $1,000 per violation, per day. Signs can also be confiscated and thrown away without notice.
