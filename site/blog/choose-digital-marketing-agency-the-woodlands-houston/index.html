Thursday afternoon, a conference room off Research Forest. Three proposals on the table. One from a downtown Houston agency with a beautiful deck and a $7,500 monthly retainer. One from a national firm whose rep flew in from somewhere colder, at $3,900 a month plus 15% of ad spend. One from a two-person shop in Spring for $1,800, sent as a Google Doc.

All three say “data-driven.” All three promise “transparent reporting.” None of them answers the owner’s real question, which is which one will still be worth paying in month nine.

This is how we’d choose a digital marketing agency in The Woodlands or anywhere in Greater Houston if we were sitting on your side of that table. We’re an agency in The Woodlands ourselves, so read it with that in mind. We’ve tried to write the version we’d hand a friend, including the parts where the honest answer is someone else.

Before you call anyone, write a one-page brief

Most agency searches go wrong before the first call. The owner hasn’t decided what the agency is for, so every pitch gets judged on charm.

Write one page. It should say:

  • The outcome you’re buying. Booked jobs, consults, online orders, tickets. Pick the one number that pays your bills, and say what it is today if you know.
  • Budget, split in two. What you can pay an agency each month, and what you can put into media (ad spend) on top. Owners who give one lump number get proposals that can’t be compared.
  • What’s running now, and who holds the logins for the website, Google Ads, Google Business Profile, Meta and analytics.
  • Your capacity. If 30 more calls a week would swamp your front desk in Kingwood, say so. An agency that hears that and plans around it is worth more than one that doesn’t ask.
  • Your calendar. Hurricane season for a restoration company, August for a tutoring center, the rodeo weeks for a restaurant near NRG.

Send the same brief to every agency. You’ll learn a lot from who reads it. If you’re still deciding whether an agency is even the right model, compared with a hire or a fractional CMO, our fractional CMO vs. agency vs. in-house breakdown covers that decision first.

Local vs. national agencies: what proximity buys you in Houston

“Local” isn’t a virtue on its own. A Houston agency that runs your Google Ads from a template is no better than a template from Ohio. But proximity does buy some specific things, and it’s worth knowing which ones matter for your business.

Where local pays off: local SEO and Google Business Profile work, where knowing that a Conroe plumber shouldn’t be bidding in Pearland saves real money. Shoot days. Billboards on I-45 and Houston radio, where relationships with local sellers help. Events. Anything that needs someone to drive over on a Tuesday.

Where it barely matters: an online store shipping to all 50 states, or a software company selling to buyers who’ve never been to Texas. For those, pick the best team for the channel and stop worrying about the ZIP code.

For what it’s worth, we run national brands from an office in The Woodlands. Geography alone doesn’t decide much. The people on your account decide nearly everything.

Specialist or full-service?

A specialist does one channel deeply: Google Ads only, SEO only, paid social only. A full-service agency handles strategy across channels, plus creative and often the website.

Go specialist when one channel produces most of your revenue and someone on your team can coordinate the rest. A personal injury firm living on Google Ads, or a Shopify brand living on Meta, often does better with a team that does nothing else.

Go full-service when you have no marketing person and the channels depend on each other. Ads need landing pages. Landing pages need copy and photos. Somebody has to decide that the spring promotion runs on Instagram and in search at the same time with the same offer. Three specialists who never talk to each other will each blame the other two.

Two things to ask either kind. First, who does each piece of the work, and is any of it subcontracted? White-labeled SEO and offshore ad management are common, and they aren’t automatically bad, but you should know. Second, what will they tell you to stop doing? A specialist who says your SEO is the problem when your website is the problem isn’t helping.

What agencies in Houston and The Woodlands typically charge

Pricing is where proposals are hardest to compare, so separate the pieces.

Nationally, Clutch’s 2026 digital marketing pricing guide puts typical U.S. agency rates at $100 to $149 an hour for services like PPC, SEO and social, with most reviewed projects falling between $10,000 and $49,999. That’s across the whole country, but Houston-area hourly rates we see sit in the same neighborhood.

The ranges below come from our experience: our own pricing, plus competing proposals that prospects in The Woodlands, Spring, Katy and inside the Loop have shown us. They aren’t a survey, and you’ll find quotes outside them in both directions.

Ask every agency to fill in the same four lines: management fee, recommended media, software or tool costs, and one-time setup. Then you’re comparing like with like. The $1,800 quote in the opening scene may become $3,100 once you add the call tracking and reporting software it quietly assumed you’d buy.

Very low prices deserve suspicion. A $299 SEO package is usually automated reports and little else. How to read a proposal (and spot the copy-paste ones)

Read page one first. It should be about you. If the proposal could go to a dentist in Sugar Land and a roofer in Tomball with only the logo swapped, it probably did.

A good proposal usually comes after the agency asked for read-only access to your accounts. It tells you something about your own business you didn’t know: that 40% of your Google Ads budget goes to searches for jobs at your company, say, or that your Business Profile lists the wrong primary category. That one finding is worth more than ten pages of capabilities.

Look for a first 90 days written in weeks, not adjectives. “Weeks 1 and 2: rebuild conversion tracking for calls and forms. Weeks 3 to 6: restructure search campaigns by service line.” If the plan is “optimize, refine, scale,” you’ve learned nothing.

Check that the KPIs match your brief. You asked for booked jobs. If the proposal promises impressions, reach and “engagement,” it’s measuring the agency’s activity instead of your result. Our guide to tracking calls and form fills shows what real conversion tracking includes, so you can tell whether a proposal plans for it.

Case studies you can actually verify

“Client in home services: +312% leads.” Every agency has slides like that. Most can’t be checked, and the ones that can’t be checked are the ones to discount.

When you see a result, ask four things. Whose account was it? What was the period? What was the baseline? And what else changed at the same time, such as a new location, a price cut, or a storm that sent every restoration company’s calls through the roof for a month?

Some notes on each step.

Partner badges tell you less than they look like they do. Under Google’s Partner requirements, a Google Partner needs a 70% optimization score, $10,000 in ad spend across managed accounts over 90 days, and at least half its account strategists certified. Premier Partners are roughly the top 3% of partners in their country. You can confirm a badge in the Google Partners directory. The badge proves a minimum level of activity and housekeeping. It doesn’t prove anyone made a client money.

Third-party reviews help if you know how they’re checked. Per Clutch’s help center, Clutch confirms each reviewer’s identity and work history, and publishes reviews it can’t confirm with a “Not Verified” label. Read those labels.

Testimonials now carry legal weight. The FTC’s rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, effective October 21, 2024, bans fake testimonials and undisclosed insider reviews, with civil penalties up to $51,744 per violation, per Morgan Lewis’s summary of the FTC rule. That doesn’t make every testimonial true. It does mean an agency that won’t put a name on its praise is telling you something.

The company itself. For a Texas agency, the Texas Comptroller’s franchise tax account status search lets you look up the business by name or Secretary of State file number. Takes two minutes. You’d be surprised how often the “agency” is a sole proprietor with a nice website, which is fine if they’re upfront about it.

The best proof is a live account. Ask the agency to screen-share a current client’s Google Ads or Meta account, with that client’s permission, and walk you through the last 90 days. Good agencies have a client or two happy to allow it. Our own case studies name the clients, and you should hold us to the same standard you hold anyone else.

Is meeting in person worth the drive?

In Houston, “local” can mean 45 minutes on I-45 at 5 p.m. So the point of a local agency isn’t a weekly coffee. It’s being able to meet when it matters: kickoff, the quarterly review, a shoot day at your location, the week something breaks.

If you do meet, use the time well.

  • Insist that the person who will run your account day to day is in the room. Not only the founder, and not only the salesperson.
  • Give them read-only access beforehand and ask them to open your Google Ads or Meta account on the screen and talk through what they see. Ten minutes of that beats an hour of slides.
  • Ask what they’d stop doing in week one. Strong practitioners always have an answer.
  • Watch who answers the technical questions. If the salesperson fields every one, you’ve met the pitch. You haven’t met the work.

National agencies can pass the same test over video. The format matters less than whether the doer shows up.

Contracts and account ownership: settle three things before you sign

We’ve written a whole post on marketing agency contract red flags, so here are only the three that decide whether you can leave cleanly.

  1. Term and exit. Know the minimum term, the notice period and whether it auto-renews. A short initial term followed by month-to-month with 30 days’ notice is common and fair for ongoing management.
  2. Who owns the accounts. Your Google Ads account should be in your name with you as an Admin; per Google’s access level guide, Admin is the level that can add and remove users, while Standard can edit campaigns but can’t manage access. On your Google Business Profile, you should be the primary owner and the agency a manager. Google’s owners and managers page says managers can’t add or remove users or delete the profile, and that new owners must wait seven days before removing other owners. Same idea for your Meta business portfolio, your domain registrant and your analytics.
  3. What leaves with you. Creative, landing pages, keyword research, call recordings and reporting data. Get it in writing.

If you already have an agency and aren’t sure who owns what, our guide to account ownership and switching agencies goes asset by asset.

A scoring matrix for comparing agencies

Gut feel picks the best presenter. A matrix makes you write down why. Score each agency 1 to 5 on each line, multiply by the weight, and add it up. Have one colleague score separately, then compare notes before you talk numbers.

Understood our business20%Restated our brief accurately and found something in our accounts we didn’t knowGeneric capabilities deck
The people doing the work20%We met the account lead, know their experience and how many accounts they carry“A dedicated team” with no names
Proof we could verify15%Named references we called and a live account walkthroughUnnamed percentages only
Channel fit15%Specialist or full-service matches our brief, no hidden subcontractingPitched services we didn’t ask for and can’t use
Measurement plan15%KPIs tied to our revenue number, tracking setup in the first monthImpressions, reach and “engagement”
Terms and ownership10%We own every account, fair notice period, assets transfer on exitAgency-owned accounts, long lock-in
Price clarity5%Fee, media, tools and setup on separate linesOne bundled number

Price gets only 5% on purpose. Your budget already filtered out the agencies you can’t afford. Among the ones you can, clarity matters more than a few hundred dollars a month. A cheap agency that wastes $2,000 of ad spend is the expensive one.

Making the call

Before you sign, call two references, and ask them three things. What went wrong, and how did the agency handle it? Who actually works on your account now, and is it the same person as at the start? Would you hire them again at the same price?

Then put a 90-day review on the calendar the day you sign. Pull out the one-page brief and the proposal, and check one against the other: did the tracking get fixed, did the plan happen, and is the number you care about moving? Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a digital marketing agency in The Woodlands or Houston?

Start with a one-page brief: the outcome you want, your fee and ad budgets, what’s running now and who holds the logins. Send it to three or four agencies. Compare proposals on whether they understood your business, who will do the work, proof you can verify, the measurement plan and contract terms. Meet the person who will run your account, and call two references before signing.

How much does a marketing agency cost in Houston?

In our experience, Google Ads management runs about $1,000 to $2,500 a month or 10% to 20% of ad spend, local SEO for one location about $800 to $2,000 a month, and small-business multi-channel retainers about $1,500 to $5,000 a month. Ad spend is extra. Clutch’s 2026 guide puts typical U.S. agency rates at $100 to $149 an hour. Ask every agency to separate fees, media, tools and setup.

Is it better to hire a local marketing agency or a national one?

It depends on the work. A Houston or The Woodlands agency helps most for local SEO, Google Business Profile, shoots at your location, billboards, radio and events, and it usually has lower minimums. National agencies often have deeper benches and documented processes, which suits ecommerce and national brands. In both cases, the people running your account matter far more than where the office is.

How can I verify a marketing agency’s case studies?

Ask whose account it was, the period, the baseline and what else changed. Request a named reference and a screen-share of a live client account with that client’s permission. Confirm Google Partner status in Google’s Partners directory, read third-party reviews and check their verification labels, and look up a Texas agency in the Comptroller’s franchise tax account status search.

Who should own my Google Ads and Google Business Profile accounts?

You should. Your Google Ads account should be in your business’s name with you as an Admin, since Admin is the access level that can add and remove users. On Google Business Profile, you should be the primary owner and the agency a manager, which lets them work on the profile without adding or removing users or deleting it. Apply the same rule to Meta, analytics and your domain.
