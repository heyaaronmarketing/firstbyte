Forty-seven percent. That’s the share of consumers who say they won’t use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, according to BrightLocal’s 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey. Seventy-four percent only care about reviews from the last three months.

So a Spring dentist with 140 reviews, the newest from last March, can lose to a newer practice in The Woodlands with 38 reviews and three from this week. We see that pattern in Houston map results constantly.

This guide is the system we set up for local businesses: how to get your review link, when and how to ask, what to say back, what to do about the one-star from someone who was never a customer, and which rules changed in 2025 and 2026.

How do you get more Google reviews?

Short answer: ask every customer, every time, within a day of finishing the job, by text or email, with your direct Google review link. Make it one tap. Reply to every review within a few days. Don’t offer anything in exchange and don’t only ask the happy ones; both break Google’s rules and, for incentives, federal law.

That’s the whole method. The rest of this page is the detail that makes it work at a 20-truck HVAC company in Conroe or a two-chair salon on Market Street.

Most businesses don’t have a review problem. They have an asking problem. In BrightLocal’s 2026 numbers, 78% of consumers were asked for a review in the past year and 83% of those people left one. The customers are willing. Somebody has to send the link.

Why Google reviews matter so much in Houston right now

Reviews do two jobs, and owners usually only think about one of them.

The first is ranking. Google says plainly that “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking” on Google’s own help page on local ranking. Practitioners agree: in Whitespark’s 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors, high Google ratings, the number of native Google reviews with text, and review recency all landed in the top 11 factors for the map pack, and review signals gained weight compared with the prior survey. “Sustained review flow” (steady reviews over months rather than a burst) ranked 14th.

The second job is conversion. The bar keeps rising. In the same BrightLocal survey, 68% of consumers want at least four stars before they’ll use a business, up from 55% a year earlier, and 31% want 4.5 or better.

And Google isn’t the only place people read them anymore. Google’s share as a review source fell from 83% to 71%, while ChatGPT and other AI tools jumped from 6% to 45%. Those AI answers pull heavily from reviews, so the words your customers use matter too. A review that says “they fixed our slab leak in Kingwood the same day” is doing more work than “great service!!”

Houston adds its own pressure. The metro added more people than any other in the country between mid-2024 and mid-2025, per ABC13’s report on the Census estimates. New residents in Cypress, Conroe and along the Grand Parkway don’t have a neighbor to ask, so they read reviews, and they read the recent ones first.

Step 1: get your Google review link and QR code

Per Google’s help page on review links and QR codes, the steps are short. Sign in at business.google.com, open your profile, select Read reviews, then Get more reviews. You’ll get a link to copy and a QR code to download. The QR code only generates from a computer browser, not the phone app, which trips people up.

Things to do with that link the same afternoon:

  • Save it as a text shortcut on every phone that talks to customers. On an iPhone that’s Settings, General, Keyboard, Text Replacement; type “rvw” and it expands to the full link.
  • Put it in your invoice template, your job-complete email and your email signature.
  • Add it to your CRM or field service software’s follow-up automation (most of them, from Jobber to the dental practice systems, have a review-request field).
  • Print the QR code on a small counter card for walk-in businesses, and on leave-behind cards for techs.

Customers need to be signed in to a Google account to post. Most are. The ones who aren’t can create one with any email address, and a link that opens straight to the star screen helps a lot.

Step 2: build the ask into the job, not your memory

Here’s the uncomfortable pattern we see when we look at review histories. Reviews arrive in clumps: the week after the owner read an article like this one, then nothing for four months. That’s exactly the “burst” Google and the ranking surveys don’t love.

The fix is to tie the ask to an event that already happens every time. Job marked complete in the dispatch software. Patient checked out. Invoice paid. Order delivered. The request goes out automatically within an hour or two, with one reminder two or three days later if nothing happened. No third reminder; that’s where people start to get annoyed.

Timing matters more than wording. In accounts we’ve run, review requests sent the same day convert far better than requests sent a week later, when the AC is just “working” again and nobody remembers how hot the house got. For a remodel, ask at the final walkthrough, while the client is standing in the new kitchen.

A personal ask in person, followed by the text, beats either one alone. The tech says, “If you’ve got 30 seconds, a Google review really helps a small company like ours. I’ll text you the link.” Then the text arrives before they’ve pulled out of the driveway.

Review request scripts that work (copy these)

Short, specific and signed by a person. Those three things beat every template trick we’ve tested. Here are versions by situation.

SituationChannelScript
Home service job doneText, same day“Hi Maria, it’s Josh from [company]. Thanks for having us out to fix the AC in Spring today. If you have a minute, a Google review helps us a lot: [link]. Thank you!”
Patient or client visitText or email, 2 hours after“Thanks for coming in today, Daniel. We’d appreciate a quick Google review about your visit: [link]. Reply here if anything wasn’t right and we’ll call you.”
Restaurant or retailCounter card with QR“Enjoyed it? Tell Houston on Google. Scan to leave a review.”
Big project (remodel, pool, build)In person, then email“Would you be willing to write a Google review about the project? Mentioning what we built and where helps other homeowners in The Woodlands find us: [link].”
ReminderText, 2-3 days later“Hi Maria, just a quick reminder in case the link got buried: [link]. No worries if not!”

Notice what the remodel script does. Asking customers to mention the project and the area is fine. Telling them what to say, or asking for five stars, isn’t (more on that below). And the “reply here if anything wasn’t right” line in the second script offers a service recovery path without blocking anyone from reviewing. Every customer still gets the link.

Texting customers in Texas has its own rules, especially for anything promotional. A transactional review request to someone you just served is different from a marketing blast, but read our Texas SMS rules guide before you automate texts at scale.

What does Google not allow when asking for reviews?

This is where good businesses get hurt, usually by a well-meaning office manager.

Google’s Maps content policy prohibits offering “incentives such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services” for reviews. It bans discouraging negative reviews and “selectively solicit[ing] positive reviews from customers,” which is review gating: the “How did we do? 1-5” screen that only sends happy customers to Google. It bans pressuring people to review while on the premises. And since 2026 it explicitly bans asking staff to collect a certain number of reviews, and soliciting reviews that include specific content.

That staff-quota rule matters for anyone who runs review contests among techs or hygienists. Recognizing the tech who gets named in reviews is fine. A $50 bonus for whoever brings in the most reviews this month is now squarely against the policy.

Federal law sits on top of Google’s rules. The FTC’s Consumer Review rule bans fake reviews, buying reviews, incentives tied to positive or negative sentiment, undisclosed reviews from employees and insiders, and suppressing negative reviews with threats. Civil penalties run up to $53,088 per violation, per Venable’s summary of the FTC’s Consumer Review rule, and the FTC sent warning letters to ten companies in late 2025. Our breakdown of the fake reviews rule goes through it line by line.

Google enforces at scale, too. It blocked or removed more than 292 million policy-violating reviews and took down over 13 million fake Business Profiles in 2025, according to Search Engine Roundtable’s write-up of Google’s 2025 Maps numbers. If your reviews have been vanishing, this post on disappearing reviews covers the usual causes, including review filters that catch legitimate reviews sent from one Wi-Fi network in your lobby.

How should you respond to Google reviews?

Reply to all of them. In BrightLocal’s 2026 survey, 89% of consumers expect owners to respond, 80% are likely to use a business that responds to all reviews, and half are put off by templated replies. Nineteen percent now expect a same-day response.

The mechanics, from Google’s guide to managing reviews: open your profile, select Read reviews, then the reply icon. Google checks replies against its content policies; most clear in about ten minutes, some take up to 30 days. The reviewer gets notified when your reply posts.

For positive reviews, two or three sentences. Thank them by first name, mention the actual job, and where it’s natural, the service and area: “Thanks, Chris. Glad the new water heater in your Tomball house is working well, and thanks for the kind words about Luis.” Don’t paste the same paragraph 40 times. Readers notice, and it wastes the one place you get to talk publicly.

Never include patient details in a reply, even to confirm someone was a patient. Medical, dental, therapy and chiropractic practices should use a single neutral reply for clinical complaints. HIPAA doesn’t stop applying because the patient brought it up first.

How to handle a negative Google review

A one-star review stings more than it hurts. A business at 4.7 with a few honest complaints and calm replies often looks more believable than a perfect 5.0. What hurts is a defensive reply, or a pile of recent bad reviews with no answers.

A reply that works has four parts. Thank them for the feedback, own the part that’s fair without arguing the rest, say what you changed, and give a direct way to reach a named person. Keep it under 80 words. You’re writing for the next 500 people who read it, not for the reviewer.

Example: “Mr. Davis, you’re right that we missed the arrival window twice, and that’s on us. We’ve changed how we schedule Friday afternoon calls in Cypress. I’d like to make this right; please call me directly at [number]. Kevin, Service Manager.”

What about reviews that aren’t real? Someone who was never a customer, a competitor, a former employee, or a review with hate speech or personal info can be reported. From the review, use the flag or “Report review” option, and track it in Google’s reviews management tool (linked from the same help page). Reviews that are merely unfair, or that describe a real bad experience, will not come down. Don’t pay anyone who promises to remove them. That’s a scam category of its own.

Then do the boring thing that actually fixes ratings: keep asking. One bad review against 15 new good ones in a quarter barely moves the average.

How many Google reviews do you need to compete in Houston?

There’s no magic number, but there are useful targets.

Start with 20. That’s the line where 47% of consumers start considering you at all. Then look at who’s in the map pack for your top search in your town (say, “dentist The Woodlands” or “roofer Pearland”) and note their counts. Your 12-month goal is to land inside that range with more recent reviews than any of them.

Recency is the cheaper race to win. A competitor with 600 reviews built over nine years might be getting four a month now. If you get 12 a month, you’ll look more alive to searchers and to Google long before you catch their total. For most service businesses, a review rate of 10% to 20% of completed jobs is realistic with an automated ask; in accounts we’ve run, businesses that add a personal in-person ask land toward the top of that range.

Multi-location businesses should track this per location. A strong average across four offices can hide a Katy office that hasn’t had a review since summer.

Your first 30 days: a review system that runs itself

If you only do one section of this page, do this one.

  1. Day 1. Get the link and QR code. Fix anything wrong on the profile while you’re in there (hours, categories, phone). Our Business Profile guide has the full list.
  2. Days 2-5. Pick the trigger (job complete, checkout, invoice paid) and set up the automated text or email with one reminder. Write two scripts from the table above in your own voice.
  3. Week 2. Train the people who finish jobs on the 15-second in-person ask. Print counter or leave-behind cards.
  4. Week 2. Reply to every review from the last 90 days that you haven’t answered. Yes, the old ones.
  5. Weeks 3-4. Check the numbers: requests sent, reviews received, average rating, reply time. Fix the step that’s leaking.
  6. Monthly after that. Five minutes to compare your count and recency with the three businesses in your map pack.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get a direct link for customers to leave a Google review?

Sign in at business.google.com, open your Business Profile, select Read reviews, then Get more reviews. You can copy the link or download a QR code, though the QR code only generates in a computer browser. Save the link as a text shortcut on staff phones and add it to invoices, follow-up emails and your CRM so every customer gets it automatically.

Can I offer a discount or gift card for Google reviews in Texas?

No. Google’s policy prohibits payment, discounts, free goods or services in exchange for reviews, and the FTC’s Consumer Review rule bans incentives tied to positive or negative sentiment, with civil penalties above $53,000 per violation. Raffles for reviewers fall in the same category. Ask every customer the same way, with no reward, and put your effort into timing and making the link easy.

Is it against Google policy to only ask happy customers for reviews?

Yes. Google’s policy bans selectively soliciting positive reviews, which includes the common review gating setup where a survey sends only four- and five-star customers to Google. You can ask unhappy customers to contact you, but every customer must still receive the same review link. Profiles caught gating can lose reviews or face restrictions, so use one link for everyone.

How long does it take Google to remove a fake review of my business?

It varies from a few days to several weeks, and many reports are declined. Flag the review from your profile, then track it in Google’s reviews management tool, where you can appeal a decision once. Reviews from non-customers, competitors, former employees, or with hate speech or personal information have the best chance. Reviews describing a real bad experience usually stay, so reply calmly instead.

Do Google reviews help my business show up in the map pack in The Woodlands?

Yes. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking, and Whitespark’s 2026 ranking factors survey put ratings, review count and review recency among the top factors for the map pack. Reviews won’t overcome distance or a wrong primary category, though. A business in The Woodlands still needs an accurate profile and service pages, with reviews pushing it up among nearby competitors.
