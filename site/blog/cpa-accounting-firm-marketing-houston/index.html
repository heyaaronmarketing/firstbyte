CPA and Accounting Firm Marketing in Houston: Better Clients, Fewer 1040s

March 11, a little after 9 p.m., in a four-partner firm off Research Forest Drive. The conference room table is covered in folders: 1040s for people who found the firm by searching “tax preparer near me” in January, each paying a few hundred dollars and each convinced their return is the most urgent one in the building.

In the inbox sits an email from the owner of a $6 million HVAC company in Spring. Her bookkeeper quit, her current CPA doesn’t return calls until May, and she wants monthly financials, quarterly planning and someone to talk to about buying a second location. She came through a client referral. She’s the client the firm actually wants, and right now nobody has time to call her back.

That gap is most of what CPA marketing in Houston is about. You probably don’t need more clients. You need the right ones, arriving in the months when you can serve them, already knowing roughly what you charge.

For a small or mid-size accounting firm, that means picking one or two industries you’re good at and saying so publicly. Publish pricing that filters out bad fits. Bid on business-intent searches instead of “CPA near me” in February, and do your content and outreach in the off-season, when your people have time. The rest of this post is how we’d set that up, with the Texas rules that shape it.

Why “more clients” is the wrong goal for most firms

Accounting has a capacity problem that most service businesses don’t. A plumber can add a truck. You can’t add a senior manager in February, and every new client in tax season competes for the same 70-hour weeks.

So start with the math, not the ads. Look at last year’s client list and sort it by fee per hour of staff time. In most firms we talk to, a small group of business clients carries the profit, and a long tail of simple individual returns eats the calendar in exactly the weeks when the business clients need you most.

Those fee ranges are illustrative. Use your own numbers. The point is the third column: monthly and advisory work spreads across the year, so marketing aimed at it smooths your capacity instead of piling more onto April.

Pick a niche that Houston actually has

Generalist firms compete on price and proximity. Firms with a niche compete on “they already know my business,” which is a much easier sale and supports a much higher fee.

Houston gives you a lot of niches with real depth:

  • Energy contractors and oilfield services. Fabrication shops, trucking, inspection and rental companies along the Beltway and up I-45 have job costing, equipment depreciation, fuel tax and boom-bust cash flow problems a generalist won’t speak to. Our B2B marketing guide for energy and industrial companies explains how these owners buy.
  • Restaurants and franchise operators. Tip reporting, sales tax, food cost and multi-unit rollups. Owners in The Woodlands, Katy and Sugar Land talk to each other, so one good restaurant client tends to bring three more. See how they market themselves in our restaurant marketing post.
  • Real estate investors. Rental portfolios, short-term rentals, cost segregation, 1031 exchanges and entity structure. Houston’s rental market is big enough that a firm can live on this alone, and property managers are a natural referral source (our property management marketing post shows who those managers are trying to reach).
  • Medical and dental practices. The Texas Medical Center and the suburban specialty practices around it.
  • Construction and home services trades. Remodelers, HVAC and plumbing companies with WIP schedules, retainage and fleet costs.

You don’t have to turn away everyone else. The niche is what you market. Referrals from existing clients can stay broad. But your website, your ads and your LinkedIn posts should make one kind of owner think “these people get it.”

Texas State Board of Public Accountancy advertising rules

Texas CPAs work under the Board’s advertising rule, 22 TAC §501.82. It bars communications that are false, fraudulent, misleading or deceptive, and its list of what counts as misleading is worth reading slowly, because a lot of standard marketing copy sits close to the line. The examples include claims of unjustified favorable results, self-praise not based on verifiable facts, untrue comparisons with other accountants and testimonials that can’t be verified.

What that means on a website or in an ad:

  • Drop “Houston’s best CPA” and “top-rated tax firm” unless you can point to the verifiable source. “Rated 4.9 on Google from 140 reviews” is a fact. “Best” is self-praise.
  • Don’t promise outcomes. “We’ll cut your tax bill” or “average client saves $18,000” invites a misleading-results problem unless you can back it up for the claim as written.
  • Use testimonials only from real, identifiable clients who gave written permission, and keep the permission on file.
  • Skip comparisons to named or implied competitors (“unlike big-box tax chains, we…” is borderline; describe what you do instead).

The rule also has a record-keeping piece that catches firms off guard. For electronic or direct mail communications, you’re expected to keep a copy and a list of recipients for at least 36 months after the last distribution, and broadcasts must be recorded and kept for the same period. There are exceptions, including communications to current clients and ones the recipient asked for. In practice: save every email campaign and its send list, and keep your ad creative in a dated folder. We’re marketers, not your ethics counsel, so run anything unusual past the Board’s rules or your attorney.

Confidentiality, Section 7216 and reviews

Two sets of rules matter here, and they reach further into marketing than most firms assume.

The Texas confidentiality rule, 22 TAC §501.75, says a CPA shall not voluntarily disclose information communicated by a client without the client’s permission, with exceptions for things like subpoenas, peer review and Board investigations. Federal law adds Internal Revenue Code Sec. 7216, a criminal provision covering preparers who knowingly or recklessly disclose or use tax return information. Per The Tax Adviser’s rundown of Sec. 7216, using return information to market nontax services generally requires the taxpayer’s consent, and for individual 1040 filers that consent has to be a separate written document with mandatory language, signed before the use. Penalties can include a fine of up to $1,000 and up to a year in prison.

So before you build a marketing program, check these:

  • Email lists. Pulling your 1040 client list into a newsletter that pitches bookkeeping or advisory is a marketing use. Get 7216 consent first, or build the list from people who opt in on your website.
  • Ad platform uploads. Uploading client emails to Google or Meta for retargeting or lookalikes is a disclosure to a third party. Don’t do it with tax clients unless counsel has signed off on the consent you have.
  • Case studies. Even an anonymized story can identify a client in a small industry. Get written permission.

Reviews are where firms slip most often. You can and should ask for Google reviews, but your replies can’t confirm that the reviewer is a client or mention anything about their situation. A safe reply to an angry review looks like this:

“Thanks for taking the time to write. We take every concern seriously, and because of our confidentiality obligations we can’t discuss any client matter here. Please call our office manager at [number] so we can talk directly.”

For asking, Google’s rules apply like anywhere else: no discounts for reviews and no asking only the happy clients, per Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps. The best moment is right after a deliverable the client values, like a finished return with a smaller balance due than they feared or a clean first set of monthly financials. Google Ads: “CPA near me” vs. “small business accountant”

Search ads work for accounting firms, but only if you pick the searches that bring your target client. The two most obvious keywords bring very different people.

“CPA near me” and “tax preparer near me” are mostly individuals, mostly in February through April, and mostly price shopping a 1040. “Small business accountant Houston,” “outsourced bookkeeping for contractors” and “restaurant CPA” are business owners, spread across the year, looking for an ongoing relationship. If your goal is the HVAC owner from the opening, the second group is where your budget belongs.

Negatives matter more than usual, because “CPA” is also an exam, a career and a word people type when they want to reach the IRS:

A few settings we’d use. Run business campaigns weekdays, roughly 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., when owners search and someone can answer. Use location targeting set to people in or regularly in your area, drawn around where your clients actually are: The Woodlands, Spring, Conroe and north Harris County for a Woodlands firm, or Katy, Cypress and the Energy Corridor for a west side one. Send every ad group to a page about that service or niche. Never the homepage.

Two platform notes. Google’s Local Services Ads list includes a “Tax services” category (and “Financial planning services”), per Google’s Local Services Ads category list, so test it if individual tax work is something you want. And Google’s government documents and services policy specifically says tax preparation services aren’t restricted by it, though we’d still keep anything that looks like an IRS seal out of your ads. Off-season content: May through December is your marketing season

Your marketing calendar should be the inverse of your workload. In tax season, nobody at the firm has time to write, film or call. In May, June, July and the weeks after the October extension deadline, they do.

What to make in those months:

  • Niche pages. One solid page per niche explaining the problems you solve, what an engagement looks like and a starting price. These pages rank, and they convert the ad traffic above.
  • Year-end planning content in October and November. Entity selection, equipment purchases, retirement plan deadlines. Business owners search this before December, and it’s the best season to start an advisory relationship.
  • Houston disaster content, ready to publish. After Hurricane Beryl, the IRS postponed a range of federal filing and payment deadlines to Feb. 3, 2025, for taxpayers in Texas counties including Harris and Montgomery, per the IRS announcement. A plain-English “what this means for your business” page published within a day or two is exactly what worried owners search for after a storm. Keep a template ready for the next one.
  • A short monthly email to people who opted in, with one useful thing per issue. Our email marketing basics covers list building.

Batch it. One partner and one marketing person can record four short videos and outline six articles in a single afternoon in June, then publish through the fall. Our post on content marketing for service businesses has a simple workflow.

LinkedIn for accountants: partners, not the firm page

In accounts we’ve seen, firm pages on LinkedIn get very little reach. Partners and managers posting as themselves do much better, and business owners and CFOs are already there.

What we’d have each partner do, about 30 minutes a week:

  • Post once a week about one real issue you see in your niche, written in plain language with no client details. “Three things I see in every contractor’s WIP schedule” beats a reshared firm announcement.
  • Comment on posts from owners, bankers, attorneys and wealth managers in your niche. Comments get you seen by their networks.
  • Connect with the bankers and commercial lenders at local banks. They refer more business clients than almost anyone.
  • Keep the profile headline specific: “CPA for Houston real estate investors and property managers,” not “Partner at XYZ CPAs.”

Paid LinkedIn ads are expensive per click. They can work for advisory or a firm with a high-fee niche, targeted by job title and company size, but we’d start organic and add paid only once your niche page converts.

Pricing pages: filter out bad fits before the first call

Most CPA websites say “contact us for pricing.” Owners read that as “expensive and slow.” A pricing page with starting ranges does two things: it tells the right clients you’re in their range and tells the wrong ones to keep looking.

A simple structure that works:

  • Packages by client type, each with “starting at” monthly pricing, what’s included (bookkeeping cadence, payroll, sales tax, tax returns, a planning meeting) and what isn’t.
  • Minimums, stated plainly: “Our business engagements start at $1,000 a month.” That one line saves your partners hours of discovery calls.
  • A short intake form asking revenue range, industry, current accounting setup and timeline. Use the answers to route and prioritize.
  • Individual tax pricing, if you still do it, with a clear floor.

Firms worry that posting prices invites shoppers. Some will shop. But the calls you do get are better, and your Google Ads conversion rate goes up because the landing page answers the main question. We’ve seen the same pattern across professional services, including in our law firm marketing post.

Capacity planning around tax season

The worst thing marketing can do for a CPA firm is work in February. If your ads produce a dozen good business leads during the second week of March and nobody calls them back until May, you paid for leads that went to someone else.

Practical moves:

  • Cut or pause generic campaigns from February through mid-April, and keep only your niche and business campaigns running at a lower budget.
  • Change your form confirmation in season: “We’re booking new business clients for consultations starting April 21. Pick a time now.” Then actually hold those slots.
  • Have one person own new-business follow-up in season, even if it’s only a 10-minute call to qualify and schedule. Speed still matters; our speed-to-lead guide shows why.
  • Track inquiries by source with call tracking and form tracking, so you know in May which channel produced the clients you kept. Our GA4 and Google Ads tracking guide covers setup.

Hiring may get slightly easier. Texas passed SB 262 in 2025, adding a licensure pathway with a bachelor’s degree, two years of experience and the CPA exam alongside the 150-hour route, and candidates can be licensed under it starting Aug. 1, 2026, per TXCPA. We wouldn’t build a growth plan on that, though. Plan marketing around the team you have.

A first 90 days for a Houston accounting firm

If we were starting from scratch with a 3-to-15-person firm:

  1. Days 1 to 30. Sort clients by fee per hour and pick one niche. Write the niche page and the pricing page. Fix the Google Business Profile: primary category, accurate hours, real photos of the team, and services listed. Start asking business clients for reviews after deliverables.
  2. Days 31 to 60. Launch one business search campaign and one niche campaign with the negatives above. Set up call and form tracking. Have each partner start the weekly LinkedIn routine.
  3. Days 61 to 90. Publish two or three niche articles, start a monthly email to opted-in contacts, and review search terms weekly. Book coffees with five bankers and three attorneys in your niche.

Judge it by consultations booked with your target client type and by engagement letters signed. Leads don’t count until they’re the right leads.

Frequently asked questions

How do CPA firms get more clients?

Most good CPA clients still come from referrals, so start by asking happy business clients and building relationships with bankers and attorneys. Then add a niche page and pricing page on your website, Google Ads on business-intent searches like small business accountant plus your area, a steady flow of Google reviews and partner posts on LinkedIn. Schedule most marketing work for the off-season when your staff has time to follow up.

Should an accounting firm use Google Ads?

Yes, if you choose the keywords carefully. Searches like CPA near me bring mostly individual tax clients in February through April, while searches like outsourced bookkeeping Houston or restaurant accountant bring business owners year-round. Run business and niche campaigns, add negatives for jobs, the CPA exam and IRS phone searches, and send each ad group to a matching service page. Google Local Services Ads also offer a Tax services category.

What are the Texas advertising rules for CPAs?

The Texas State Board of Public Accountancy rule at 22 TAC 501.82 bars false, misleading or deceptive communications. Examples include unjustified favorable results, self-praise not based on verifiable facts, untrue comparisons with other accountants and unverifiable testimonials. It also expects firms to keep copies and recipient lists of electronic and direct mail communications for at least 36 months, with some exceptions. Check unusual claims with the Board rules or your attorney.

Can a CPA respond to Google reviews?

Yes, but carefully. Texas confidentiality rules and federal Sec. 7216 limit what you can disclose, so a reply should never confirm the reviewer is a client or mention their situation. Thank them, say confidentiality prevents discussing any client matter publicly, and offer a phone number to talk directly. You can ask clients for reviews, but Google prohibits incentives and asking only happy clients.

Should CPA firms put prices on their website?

We think so. Starting-at prices by client type, a stated engagement minimum and a short intake form help the right business owners see you fit their budget and keep poor fits from booking discovery calls. You get fewer inquiries but better ones, and paid search converts better because the page answers the price question. Keep the numbers accurate and note what each package includes.
