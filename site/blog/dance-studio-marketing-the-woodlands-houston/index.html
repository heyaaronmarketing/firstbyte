The second Tuesday in August, 9:40 p.m. Six voicemails, four web forms and a Facebook message that just says “price?” Most are about a 4-year-old and a ballet class. Two are from parents who left another studio after a recital bill they didn’t see coming.

That week decides a lot of your year. Conroe ISD and Katy ISD both start school on August 12, 2026, and families in The Woodlands, Conroe, Katy and Cypress settle their after-school schedules within a couple of weeks of the first bell.

Dance studio marketing in The Woodlands and Houston comes down to three jobs. Be in front of parents before that schedule hardens. Make the full cost of a dance year easy to understand. And keep families through the recital so they re-enroll in the fall without being sold again.

This is written for studio owners with recreational classes, maybe a competition team, and a spring recital, anywhere from Magnolia and Tomball down to Pearland and Sugar Land.

Your marketing calendar is the school calendar

Parents buy dance the way they buy every after-school activity: around school dates. Here’s how the 2026-27 year lines up for two big districts, and what we’d run in each stretch.

Dates come from Conroe ISD’s 2026-27 calendar, My Neighborhood News on Katy ISD’s calendar and its January 2026 fall break vote. Cy-Fair, Tomball, Magnolia, Humble and Klein all publish their own calendars and don’t line up perfectly, so pull the districts your families actually attend each spring and adjust. If your recital lands in late May, it sits right on top of the last week of school, so start the re-registration push early enough that it isn’t buried under graduations and end-of-year events.

The bigger point: registration should open in July, not the week school starts. A parent who books a spot on July 20 doesn’t take the 9:40 p.m. call from the studio down the road on August 12. Our back-to-school marketing post covers the broader season.

Parents are the buyer

The dancer might be four. The buyer is a parent juggling a sibling’s soccer practice, a drive on FM 2978 or the Grand Parkway, and a budget. Before they call, they want answers to a short list, and your website should give them without a phone call:

  • Can I get there from school pickup? Show class times by age on one page.
  • What does the whole year cost, recital included? (Next section.)
  • Is this a competition studio or a fun one? Most studios are both. Say which track a new student starts on.
  • Can I watch? A viewing window or a monthly watch week answers a lot of nerves.
  • What do they wear, and where do I buy it?
  • Do boys take class here? Studios that show boys in classes get more boys.

Write class descriptions for parents. “Ballet and Tap Combo, ages 3 to 5, 45 minutes. Kids learn to follow directions, take turns and stand in a line, and they think they’re just dancing.” That last line sells more spots than “introduction to ballet technique.”

Recital economics and why costume fees belong on your website

The recital is the finale of your product, your biggest marketing event of the year and, for many studios, a real chunk of revenue. It’s also where parents feel nickel-and-dimed if nobody told them the numbers in August.

Fee structures vary a lot. In Dance Teacher, the owners of one studio described three models they’ve used: a $120 flat recital fee covering two tickets, a performance DVD, a T-shirt, flowers and a program; a $129 fee including a costume, tights and accessories, with extra costumes at $85; and a $12 monthly fee across a nine-month season that folded in registration, a costume, a T-shirt and a DVD. Costume prices covered in another Dance Teacher piece ranged from $70 per costume for elementary students at a Minnesota studio (families kept them) to a $350 nonrefundable costume rental fee at a Connecticut ballet school. The advice from those owners: disclose costume costs at registration, give an amount and a due date, and say whether the family keeps the costume.

We’d go a step further and publish a “what a dance year costs” page. It doesn’t lose you enrollments. It loses you the families who would have left angry in March and told every parent at pickup. A simple version:

Put the costume deposit date in your billing system and send a reminder two weeks before. The deposit is a cancellation moment for some families, so it helps if it lands after the winter showcase, when kids are excited, rather than right after the holiday credit card bills.

Trial classes that turn into fall enrollments

Most studios offer a trial class. Fewer run it like a sale.

  • Book a specific class. “Come try any class” produces no-shows. A booked Tuesday 4:30 with a name on the roster gets kids in the door.
  • The first ten minutes matter most. Greet the child by name at the door, name tag on, teacher hands-on with the new kid in the first exercise. The parent at the window is watching their child’s face, not the choreography.
  • Older kids get placement. A 10-year-old who danced elsewhere wants to know she won’t be with the 6-year-olds. Offer a placement class and say so.
  • Follow up the same day. A text from the front desk with the enrollment link and “we’re holding her spot in Tuesday 4:30 until Friday.” Our speed-to-lead guide explains why hours matter.
  • Run trial weeks in August and January when demand peaks, and keep a standing trial option the rest of the year.

Track the whole chain by source: inquiries, trials booked, trials attended, enrolled, and still enrolled at the recital. The last step is the one that tells you whether a channel brings families who stay.

Meta ads, minors and the rules you work within

Facebook and Instagram are where parents of young dancers spend their scrolling time, so they’re the main paid channel for most studios. A few rules shape how you can use them.

Targeting teens is restricted. Since February 2023, advertisers can only use age and location to reach teens on Facebook and Instagram, and gender is no longer an option, per Meta’s announcement. Interest-based teen targeting was already gone. So for a 15-year-old’s contemporary class, you advertise to the parent.

Targeting adults is less precise than it used to be, too. Meta removed detailed targeting exclusions from Ads Manager in 2025 and has been consolidating interest options, per Linear Design’s running list of Meta targeting changes. In practice we target adults in a radius around the studio and let the creative do the filtering. An ad that says “Ballet and tap for ages 3 to 5, Tuesdays at 4:30, five minutes from Kuykendahl and Research Forest” finds the right parents because everyone else scrolls past.

Then there’s the content itself. Your best creative is kids dancing. That’s fine with consent and some care.

Meta has also added protections around accounts that mostly feature children. In July 2025 it said adult-managed accounts that primarily feature children would be placed in its strictest message settings, get Hidden Words turned on to filter offensive comments, be harder to find in search, and not be recommended to potentially suspicious adults, per Meta’s July 2025 safety update. The announcement talks about parents and talent managers, and we don’t know exactly how Meta treats a studio business account. The direction is clear anyway. Keep the public feed a mix of teachers, the studio, older students and group shots, and save the close-ups for your private parent app or group.

For lead forms, keep them short and ask one qualifying question (child’s age). Our post on fixing bad Facebook leads covers the settings.

Competition teams need their own marketing

Recreational families and competition families want different things, and one Instagram feed rarely serves both. A grid full of trophies and tricks can scare off the parent of a shy 5-year-old. A grid full of tutus doesn’t recruit a serious 12-year-old.

  • Recruit comp mostly from inside. Your rec classes are the pipeline. Invite promising students and their parents to watch a team rehearsal, and hold auditions on a published date in late spring.
  • Give the team its own page. Commitment (hours a week, required classes), the travel calendar, a season cost estimate with [your price] placeholders filled in honestly, and the audition date.
  • Post results, accurately. Real placements from real events, with parent permission for any names. Never round up.
  • Use Instagram highlights to split the feed: “Rec classes,” “Competition team,” “Recital.” Parents go straight to the one they care about.

Comp parents are also your loudest reviewers and your most active referrers. Treat the team parents’ group chat as a channel you’re part of, with a clear weekly update from the studio, so rumors don’t fill the gap.

Summer camps and the Texas rules to check first

Summer camps cover the June and July revenue gap and introduce kids who’ll enroll in the fall. They also sit near two sets of Texas rules worth reading before you print a flyer.

The first is youth camp licensing. Texas tightened youth camp requirements after the July 2025 Hill Country floods, and KPRC reported only 18 youth camps were fully licensed in mid-May 2026 as camps worked through new emergency plan rules. But the DSHS parent resource on youth camps says the law doesn’t apply to programs HHSC regulates as child care, programs that don’t offer outdoor activities, or programs that focus on one specific activity “like gymnastics, baseball, or music.” An indoor, dance-only camp looks like it falls outside the youth camp law. A “dance, crafts and splash day” camp might not. DSHS has a lookup tool; check before you plan the week.

The second is child care licensing. Texas exempts single-skill programs only when they meet conditions, including that the program doesn’t advertise or present itself as a child-care facility, day-care center or licensed before- or after-school program, tells parents it isn’t state-licensed and about the physical risks involved, and runs DPS background checks on employees and volunteers who work with children, per Texas Human Resources Code § 42.041(b)(18). So the words in your marketing matter. Say “dance camp,” “ballet intensive” or “musical theater week.” Don’t say “summer care” or “after-school pickup and care.” Have your attorney read the camp page.

Marketing the camps themselves is simple. Open registration in February or March, sell current families first with an early deadline, then run Meta ads to parents in your radius with the themed weeks, times and price. Half-day options sell well for 3- to 6-year-olds. Post each week as a Business Profile update.

Google Business Profile, search ads and reviews

When a parent types “dance classes for kids near me,” the map results usually beat the websites. Get the profile right first.

  • Category. Dance school as primary. Add others only for what you actually run.
  • Booking link to your trial class or registration page, set as the preferred link, per Google’s help page on booking links.
  • Photos of the lobby, the viewing window, each studio room, the parking lot at pickup time and a recital group shot. Parents check whether drop-off looks safe.
  • Posts for registration dates, camps and recital tickets.

Search ads work well for dance because the searches are specific: ballet classes The Woodlands, toddler dance classes Spring TX, hip hop dance classes for kids Katy, competitive dance studio Cypress, adult ballet Houston. Build an ad group per style or age, each pointing at that class’s page, and set location to people in or regularly in your area using Google’s advanced location options.

Negatives to add on day one: dance club, nightclub, salsa night, dance shoes, dancewear store, jobs, teacher, free, YouTube, TikTok tutorial, and quinceañera or pole unless you offer them. Run ads hardest from mid-July through September and again in early January; trim them in March and April unless you’re selling camps.

Reviews come easiest after the first month and after the recital, from the same link sent to every family. Google’s rules ban incentives, asking only happy families, and staff quotas or staff-name contests, per Google’s prohibited and restricted content policy. Retention: the recital is your re-enrollment campaign

Dance studios have predictable cliffs. September, when a kid who never settled in quits. January, after the holiday break. Around the costume deposit. And the weeks after the recital, when summer starts and the habit breaks.

What we’d do about each:

  • Open fall re-registration before the recital, with a deposit that holds the family’s current day and time. The slot is the thing they’re afraid to lose.
  • Send a short progress note from each teacher in December. Two sentences about what the child learned. It lands right before the January decision.
  • Keep summer contact. One summer class or a camp week keeps the habit alive. Families who disappear from June to August are the ones who don’t come back.
  • Recital week is review week. Families are proud and filming everything. Send the review link the next day.
  • Pick one communication channel (an app or email) and send a weekly update on a fixed day. Surprises cause cancellations, especially money surprises.

If you text families about promotions, read our Texas SMS rules post first. Schedule updates and promo blasts aren’t treated the same.

What to spend, what to track, and a first 90 days

The number that matters is cost per enrolled student, by source, and how many of those students were still dancing at the recital. Cost per lead flatters Meta forms and hides the families who never showed up.

For a single-location studio, an illustrative paid budget is $600 to $1,500 a month from mid-July through September, split between Google Search and Meta, then a lighter level the rest of the year with bumps in January and for camp registration. Your own tracked results should replace those numbers within one season.

Frequently asked questions

When should a dance studio in The Woodlands start fall registration marketing?

Open registration and turn on ads in July. Conroe ISD and Katy ISD both start school on August 12, 2026, and parents in The Woodlands, Conroe and Katy set after-school schedules within a couple of weeks of that date. Families who book in July are off the market by August. Keep a trial week and fast phone and text replies running through August and September for late deciders.

Can I target teenagers with Facebook ads for my dance studio?

Only in a very limited way. Since February 2023, Meta lets advertisers reach teens using only age and location, and gender targeting for teens was removed. Interest targeting for teens was already gone. For most studios it works better to advertise to parents in a radius around the studio, with creative that names the class, age range, day and time so the right families recognize it.

Should a dance studio publish its costume and recital fees?

Yes. Parents compare studios on the full cost of a dance year, and surprise recital and costume bills are a common reason families leave or post angry reviews. Publish registration, tuition, costume, recital fee and ticket costs with due dates, and say whether families keep costumes. Studio owners quoted by Dance Teacher recommend disclosing costume costs at registration with an amount and due date.

Do dance summer camps in Texas need a youth camp license?

Possibly not, but check. A Texas DSHS parent resource says the youth camp law doesn’t apply to programs focused on one specific activity, programs without outdoor activities, or programs regulated as child care. A dance-only indoor camp may fall outside it, while a mixed-activity camp might not. Separately, single-skill programs exempt from child care licensing must not advertise as child care. Confirm with DSHS and your attorney.

How do I get more students for my dance studio in Houston?

Be visible before the school year locks in: open registration in July, run Google Search ads on specific class and age searches, and advertise to parents near the studio on Facebook and Instagram. Make costs and class times easy to find online, book trial classes into specific sessions, follow up the same day, and open fall re-registration before the recital so current families stay.
