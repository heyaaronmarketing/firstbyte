Second Monday in August at a center in Cypress. The director has a waitlist of 14 for infants and can’t take any of them, because she’s one infant teacher short. Meanwhile the 3-year-old room has five open spots, and the pre-K room just lost four kids to the school district’s program.

Saturday, a couple touring with a newborn pulls out a phone in the hallway. They’ve already looked up her state inspection history, and they want to ask about a citation from last spring.

That’s child care marketing in Houston. The problem is rarely “not enough interest.” It’s getting the right families into the classrooms you can staff, at the time of year they’re deciding, with a tour process that turns a curious parent into a signed enrollment.

To increase enrollment at a daycare or child care center, market the specific classrooms with open seats and staff to cover them. Win the Google Maps results within a few miles of home and work routes, and make tours bookable online. Own your public inspection record instead of hoping parents won’t look. Then follow up fast, because parents tour three or four centers before they choose.

This guide is for independent centers and small multi-site operators from Conroe to Pearland.

How Houston parents actually search for child care

Parents pick child care near home or on the drive to work, and almost never somewhere far from both. A family in Spring commuting down I-45 might consider centers near home, near the Grand Parkway, or close to an office in Greenspoint. Very few will consider one in Tomball.

The searches are local and specific: “daycare near me,” “infant care the woodlands,” “preschool spring tx,” “daycare that accepts CCS,” “montessori cypress,” “after school care near [school name].” Parents search on their phones during lunch, read reviews, look at photos, and then look up the center in the state database.

Supply is tight in parts of the region. Advocacy group Children at Risk identified 43 child care deserts in Houston, and 56% of low-income children under six with working parents live in them, per ABC13 in April 2025. The group defines a desert as a ZIP code where young children with working parents outnumber licensed capacity by more than three to one, per KPRC. If your center sits in or near one of those ZIPs, your problem is mostly operations and staffing, not marketing. In the suburbs with heavy competition, like parts of The Woodlands, Katy and Sugar Land, the fight is for the map pack and the tour.

Market the classrooms you can staff

This is the step most centers skip. Before you spend a dollar, list each classroom’s licensed capacity, current enrollment, staffed capacity and expected openings for the next 90 days.

Texas sets child-to-caregiver ratios by age. Per the First Five Years Fund’s ratio table, drawn from the state’s FY2025-2027 child care plan, Texas centers run 1:4 for infants up to 11 months, 1:5 for 12 to 17 months, 1:9 for 18 to 23 months, 1:11 for 2-year-olds, 1:15 for 3-year-olds and 1:18 for 4-year-olds. Check the current Chapter 746 minimum standards for group sizes and exceptions.

The marketing math falls out of that table. One infant teacher can cover four babies; one teacher in the 4s room covers 18 kids. So:

  • If you’re short on infant teachers, stop advertising infant openings. Run a waitlist page instead and put your ad money into the 3s and pre-K rooms.
  • If you just hired, advertise that specific room with a specific start date. “Two infant openings starting October 6” fills faster than “Now enrolling all ages.”
  • If the pre-K room is losing 4-year-olds to free public pre-K, market what the school program doesn’t offer: full-day hours, summer care, before and after care, sibling care in the same building.

Keep a simple waitlist form on the site (child’s birth date, desired start date, schedule needed, phone) and actually call families when a spot opens. A waitlist nobody works is a list of families who enrolled somewhere else.

Google Business Profile for a child care center

The map pack is where most “daycare near me” searches end. Pick the primary category that matches your main program: Day care center, Preschool, Child care agency or Montessori school. Add the others as secondary categories only if they’re true.

  • Hours that match your real drop-off and pick-up window (6:30 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. is common). Parents filter by this.
  • A “Book a tour” appointment link that goes to a real scheduler, not the homepage.
  • Photos of classrooms, the playground, the kitchen, the secure entry and your staff. Never post a child’s face without written parent permission, and keep a permission list by child at the front desk.
  • Services and attributes: infant care, toddler care, pre-K, after-school, summer camp, meals provided, transportation to specific schools, Spanish spoken if true.
  • Weekly posts: an open seat in a specific room, a tour day, a new curriculum unit.

If you run several centers, each needs its own profile and its own page on your site. Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the rest.

Reviews from parents, inside the rules

Parents read reviews more closely for child care than for nearly anything else they buy. They’re looking for specific words: safe, communication, teachers stay, my child loves it.

Under Google’s prohibited content policy, you can’t offer anything for a review (no tuition credit, no free week), can’t ask only happy families, and can’t discourage negative reviews. In April 2026 Google also added language against telling staff to collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content, per PPC Land. Don’t run a teacher contest for reviews that mention their names.

When to ask: after a family’s first month, when the transition tears are over, and at the end of the school year. A simple text from the director works:

“Hi [Name], it’s [Director] at [Center]. We’ve loved having [child] in the Bluebird room this year. If you have a minute, an honest Google review helps other families find us: [link]. Thank you!”

Reply to every review. For critical ones, don’t argue and don’t share details about a child or family. Thank them, say you take it seriously, and invite them to call the director. Our complete guide to Google reviews has more templates.

Your inspection history is public. Own it.

Texas HHS Child Care Regulation runs TXChildCareSearch.org, and Texas HHS tells parents to use it to find regulated providers and review inspection results. Since January 2020 the site has shown five years of an operation’s compliance history, including the number of inspections, deficiencies cited and corrective actions, per Texas HHS. KPRC’s walkthrough of the search notes that the operation details page shows the weights of deficiencies cited in the past three years, and parents can open a narrative of each incident.

Plenty of parents now look this up before the tour. Hoping they won’t is not a plan. What we’d do:

  • Pull your own record every quarter and read it the way a nervous parent would.
  • Prepare a short, honest answer for any citation: what happened, what you changed, and who checks it now. Train the director and assistant director to give it calmly on tours.
  • Put a “Safety and licensing” page on your website with your license number, a link to your state record, your ratios, your staff training and background check policy, and your secure-entry and pick-up procedures.
  • Don’t claim “zero violations” or “perfect inspections” anywhere. Records change, and parents can check.

Texas Rising Star and CCS families

Child Care Services (CCS) scholarships are a big part of enrollment for many Houston centers. Families who qualify can choose any provider that accepts CCS, and once approved they have 14 days to pick one if they haven’t already, per the state’s Child Care Services page.

In this area the program runs through Workforce Solutions Gulf Coast, which serves the 13-county Houston-Galveston region. Families apply through the statewide KinderConnect system, and there is a waitlist. In April 2026, Workforce Solutions Gulf Coast cited an estimated 30,000 eligible children on scholarship waitlists in Harris County alone.

Texas Rising Star is the state’s quality rating for CCS providers. Per Texas Rising Star’s certification page, all regulated CCS providers must participate. New providers start at Entry Level and have 24 months to earn a star rating; Entry Level programs don’t get enhanced payment rates. The star levels are Two-Star, Three-Star and Four-Star, scored across four categories, with teacher-child interactions weighted at 40%.

For marketing, that means:

  • If you accept CCS, say so clearly on your site and Business Profile. “We accept Child Care Services (CCS) scholarships through Workforce Solutions” is a phrase families search for, in English and Spanish.
  • If you’re Three- or Four-Star, put the rating on your homepage, tour page and ads. Workforce Solutions itself points families to Texas Rising Star certification as a quality credential.
  • Build a short “How CCS works at our center” page: how to apply, what documents families need, what happens if they’re on the waitlist, and whether you hold a spot.

Families on the waitlist are still families. Many are working and paying privately while they wait, and they’ll remember the center that explained the process. If you serve a lot of Spanish-speaking families, our guide to Spanish-language marketing in Houston is worth a read.

Facebook and Instagram ads for parents, and what Meta won’t let you do

Meta is where Houston parents spend their phone time, and it’s good for tour days, open-seat announcements and summer camp. Ad targeting has gotten blunter, though.

Meta keeps removing and consolidating detailed targeting options. The latest round took effect January 15, 2026, and ad sets that used removed options stopped delivering, per Social Media Today. Parent-based options still appear under Demographics in many accounts, but check what’s there in Ads Manager before you plan around it. In our experience, a tight radius around the center plus creative that clearly says “infant care in Shenandoah” finds parents better than stacked interests do.

Two policy traps:

  • Personal attributes. Meta ads can’t assert or imply things about the viewer, such as their age, financial situation or family status. “Struggling single mom?” gets rejected. “Full-day child care from 6:30 a.m., CCS accepted” is fine.
  • Hiring ads. Teacher recruiting ads must run under the Employment special ad category, which fixes age at 18 to 65+, includes all genders, requires at least a 15-mile radius and removes lookalike audiences, per Jon Loomer’s guide to special ad categories. Keep hiring and enrollment campaigns separate.

What works: a 20-second phone video walking through a classroom with the teacher explaining the day, a tour-day event with a date and time, and a lead form with two questions (child’s age, desired start date). The enrollment calendar: August, January and summer

Child care enrollment in Houston follows the school calendar more than the weather. Most districts here start in mid-August, which sets off a wave: 4-year-olds leave for public pre-K or kindergarten, older kids move up a room, and new families need full-day care when parents go back to work.

  • February to April: summer camp marketing and fall enrollment for families who plan ahead. Start tour campaigns here, especially for infant rooms.
  • May to July: peak touring for August starts. This is when your tour follow-up has to be perfect.
  • August: fill the gaps left by kids moving to school. Advertise specific open rooms with start dates.
  • September to November: after-school programs and the families whose plans fell through.
  • January: the second wave. Parents return to work after holidays and parental leave ends, and some families switch centers. Plan a tour push for the first two weeks of the year.

Hurricane season is also real here. Have a closure and reopening communication plan ready for families, and update your Business Profile hours the same day you close. Our back-to-school marketing guide covers the August push in more detail.

From tour request to enrolled: the follow-up

Most of the money in daycare marketing is won or lost after the inquiry. Parents tour several centers, and the one that responds first and follows up best usually gets the deposit.

Practical details:

  • Respond to tour requests within the hour during business hours. A parent who requested a tour at lunch has probably requested two more.
  • Offer tour times before 9 a.m. and after 4:30 p.m. Working parents can’t always tour at 10.
  • Have the director, not whoever’s free, run the tour. Show the secure entry, the classroom the child will be in, and introduce the actual teacher.
  • Send a follow-up the same day with tuition, the enrollment packet, the safety page link and a deadline for holding the spot.
  • Call or text at day 3 and day 7. Most centers stop after the tour.

Frequently asked questions

How do I increase enrollment at my daycare?

Start by listing which classrooms have open seats and staff to cover them, then market those rooms specifically. Fix your Google Business Profile, add an online tour scheduler, collect parent reviews after the first month, and build a safety and licensing page that links to your state record. Respond to tour requests within an hour and follow up at day 3 and day 7.

Can parents see my daycare’s inspection history in Texas?

Yes. Texas HHS Child Care Regulation runs TXChildCareSearch.org, where parents can look up any regulated center and see inspections, deficiencies cited and corrective actions, with several years of compliance history and narratives for incidents. Many parents check before touring, so read your own record regularly and prepare honest, calm answers for any citation.

Do I need Texas Rising Star to accept CCS families?

Yes. Texas requires all regulated Child Care Services providers to participate in Texas Rising Star. New providers start at Entry Level and have 24 months to earn a Two-, Three- or Four-Star certification, and Entry Level programs do not receive enhanced payment rates. Families can choose any provider that accepts CCS, so state clearly on your site and profile that you do.

Can I target parents with Facebook ads for my child care center?

Partly. Meta still offers some parent-related demographic options in many accounts, but it keeps removing detailed targeting choices, so check Ads Manager first. A tight radius around your center with very specific creative usually works well. Ad copy cannot imply the viewer’s personal attributes, and teacher hiring ads must run under Meta’s Employment special ad category with its targeting limits.

When is the best time to advertise a daycare in Houston?

Start tour campaigns in February for families planning fall enrollment, push hardest from May through August as the school year approaches, and run a second push in the first weeks of January when parental leave ends and families switch centers. Summer camp marketing should start in February too. Always advertise only rooms you can staff.
