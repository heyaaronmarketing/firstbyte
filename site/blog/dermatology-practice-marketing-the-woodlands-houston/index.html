Monday, 8:05 a.m., at a dermatology office off Research Forest. Thirty-one voicemails from the weekend. Nine are people who found a spot on their back and want it looked at. Six are existing patients needing a refill. Four are asking what a syringe of filler costs. The front desk books the mole checks for late January, because that’s the doctor’s first opening, and three of those callers quietly book somewhere in Spring instead.

Nobody calls that a marketing problem. It is one.

Dermatology practice marketing in The Woodlands and Houston comes down to running two businesses through one front desk. Medical dermatology needs to be findable, in-network and bookable fast. Cosmetic dermatology needs a sales funnel with a consult, a price conversation and follow-up. Both sit under Texas Medical Board advertising rules, HIPAA, and ad platform health policies that are stricter than most practices realize.

This guide is for independent and small-group practices with one to four locations around Montgomery County and north Harris County, plus anyone inside the Loop who’s tired of competing with the big systems on their terms.

Medical and cosmetic dermatology are two different marketing jobs

A patient searching “dermatologist near me that takes Aetna” and a patient searching “lip filler The Woodlands” have almost nothing in common except your logo. Treat them as one audience and you’ll write mushy pages that work for neither.

Medical service pages answer “do you take my insurance and when can I get in.” Cosmetic pages answer “what will it look like, who does it, and what does it cost.” The homepage can serve both, but the paths have to separate within one click.

Your new-patient wait time is a marketing problem

Most practices think of the schedule as an operations issue. From the patient’s side, a seven-week wait is a reason to keep scrolling.

Houston actually looks good on paper. In AMN Healthcare’s 2025 appointment wait-time survey, researchers called 21 Houston dermatology offices in early 2025 and found an average wait of 9 days for a new patient, against 36.5 days across all 15 metros surveyed. The same Houston sample ranged from 1 day to 65 days. That spread is the story. Patients here know someone can see them sooner, and Google will happily show them who.

What we’d do about it:

  • Market the provider with openings. If your physician is booked to February and your PA has slots Thursday, the skin-check ads and the booking page should lead with the PA. More on that below.
  • Show “next available” on the site. Online scheduling that displays real openings converts better than a form that promises a callback. A callback at 4:45 p.m. on Friday is how you lose the patient to a practice in Cypress.
  • Run a cancellation list. Text the waitlist when slots open. It fills holes and makes the long wait feel shorter.
  • Stop paying for demand you can’t serve. If the medical schedule is full for six weeks, pause or trim the medical search campaigns and push cosmetic, where the injector may have room.

Speed matters after the click, too. Our speed-to-lead guide covers the follow-up side, and it applies to web forms just as much as phone calls.

Skin cancer screening seasonality and the Texas sun

Skin checks are the front door of medical dermatology. They’re also seasonal in a way you can plan around.

The numbers give you plenty to say. Per the American Academy of Dermatology, about one in five Americans will develop skin cancer in their lifetime, roughly 9,500 people in the U.S. are diagnosed every day, and the five-year survival rate is 99% for melanoma caught before it reaches the lymph nodes. The AAD projects 234,680 new melanoma cases in 2026.

Houston’s sun does the rest. Space City Weather has pointed out that unprotected skin can burn in half an hour or less around midday in summer and that extreme UV hangs around well into late August. Add pool season in The Woodlands, weekends on Lake Conroe and a lot of outdoor workers, and you have an audience that already knows they should get checked and keeps putting it off.

The calendar we’d use:

  • April: Start building. Write the skin check page, film a short video of what a full-body exam involves (patients worry about this more than they say), and line up an email to past patients.
  • May: Skin Cancer Awareness Month. Melanoma Monday falls on the first Monday in May, which was May 4 in 2026, per AAD’s Skin Cancer Awareness Month page. Raise search budgets on skin check terms and post every week.
  • June through August: Sunburn, sun spots and “new mole” searches. Good time for content about sunscreen and spot checks for kids before camp.
  • October through December: Deductible season. Patients who’ve met their deductible want to get in before January. Say so plainly in emails.
  • January: New deductibles, new insurance cards, lots of “do you take my plan” calls. Update insurance pages before the first week.

One caution on copy. Fear sells skin checks and it also reads cheap. “Most skin cancers are very treatable when caught early. Book a 15-minute check” works better for us than a stock photo of a melanoma.

Texas Medical Board advertising rules and board certification claims

Texas has specific rules about the phrase “board certified,” and dermatology is a specialty where they come up constantly, because so many people do skin treatments.

Per the Texas Medical Board’s board certification advertising rules, a physician can advertise as board certified only when the certifying board is an ABMS member board, a member board of the AOA Bureau of Osteopathic Specialists, the American Board of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, or an organization the TMB has found substantially equivalent. TMLT’s summary of TMB Rule 164 adds that “board eligible” and “board qualified” are prohibited in Texas advertising, and that claims like “most highly qualified” need objective data behind them.

The American Board of Dermatology is the one that matters here. According to the American Board of Dermatology, its diplomates complete a one-year internship plus at least three years of dermatology residency, pass its exams, and keep up continuing certification. It’s one of the 24 ABMS boards. So “board-certified dermatologist” on your site means a specific thing, and patients can check it on the ABD’s own lookup.

A few practical rules we follow on dermatology sites:

  • Name the board: “Board certified by the American Board of Dermatology.” It’s specific and verifiable.
  • Use the degree. Texas Occupations Code §104.003 requires Texas physicians to identify the healing art they practice with designations like “M.D.” or “D.O.” on signs and printed materials. Put it on every provider page and bio.
  • Don’t let “dermatology” blur who does what. If a nurse injector or aesthetician performs a treatment, the page should say so.
  • Avoid superlatives you can’t prove. “Best dermatologist in Houston” is an invitation to a complaint. “Seeing patients in The Woodlands since 2011” is a fact.

Provider pages for PAs and nurse practitioners

Physician assistants and nurse practitioners often carry a big share of new medical visits in Houston dermatology practices. Most websites bury them on a team page with a headshot and two sentences. That’s a waste of your most available providers.

In Texas, PAs and APRNs in a practice like yours prescribe under a written prescriptive authority agreement with a supervising physician, as the Texas Medical Association explains. That structure is worth explaining to patients in plain words, because some are nervous about seeing “not the doctor.” A good provider page turns that nervousness into a booking.

Give each provider a Google Business Profile if they see patients at a regular location and Google’s rules allow it for your setup; our medical practice marketing post covers practitioner listings and how to keep them from competing with the practice listing. Then point skin-check ads at the provider with the soonest openings, and let reviews mention them by name when patients choose to (you can’t script that).

Insurance pages patients can actually use

“Do you take my insurance?” might be the most-asked question in medical dermatology. Most sites answer it with a logo wall that hasn’t been updated since 2022.

Insurance acceptance varies a lot here. In the same AMN survey, 62% of the Houston dermatology offices called accepted Medicare and 5% accepted Medicaid. If you take Medicare, that’s worth a dedicated page, since a lot of retirees in The Woodlands, Montgomery and Kingwood are actively looking for a skin cancer doctor who does.

What a useful insurance section includes:

  • One page per major carrier you’re in network with, naming the specific plans (an HMO and a PPO from the same carrier are different answers).
  • A clear line on cosmetic services: insurance doesn’t cover them, and here’s how pricing and financing work.
  • What patients should bring, and what to do if they’re switching plans in January.
  • A “last updated” date. Patients trust it more, and it reminds your staff to keep it current.

These pages rank surprisingly well for “dermatologist that takes [carrier] Houston” searches, which competitors rarely target. They’re also the pages your front desk can text to callers, which shortens calls.

Then the policies. Google’s Health in personalized advertising policy treats health conditions and invasive procedures, explicitly including cosmetic injections, as a sensitive category. Customer Match lists, your own data segments and lookalike segments aren’t supported for that kind of promotion. In practice, uploading your patient list to target “people like our Botox patients” is off the table. Keywords and location do the targeting.

Prescription product names have their own rule. In the U.S., you can use prescription drug terms in ad text and landing pages for promotion, but keyword-targeting them requires certification, per Google’s restricted drug terms page. Search Engine Roundtable reported the updated policy was enforced starting October 29, 2025. So an ad can name a prescription neurotoxin in the copy (trademark rules permitting), while the keyword list leans on terms like “wrinkle injections” and “forehead lines treatment” unless you hold the certification.

Meta is tighter. Practices classified as health and wellness advertisers can lose the ability to optimize for mid- and lower-funnel standard events, and some lose custom parameters and URL data in the pixel, according to Search Engine Land’s breakdown. Instant forms on Meta and upper-funnel video tend to be the workable path, with the booking happening on your site. Our paid social team and search team set these up so policy problems show up before launch, not after a disapproval.

Budget, as a starting point we’d plan with (illustrative): $2,000 to $4,000 a month on search for a single-location practice, split by what the schedule can absorb, plus $1,000 to $2,500 on Meta if you have a cosmetic program with open injector time. HIPAA and tracking pixels on a dermatology website

Tracking is where dermatology sites quietly get into trouble, usually because someone installed a pixel in 2021 and nobody looked again.

The rules shifted in 2024. HHS’s online tracking guidance still says tracking technologies on user-authenticated pages (patient portals, logged-in booking) generally have access to protected health information, that vendors receiving PHI need a business associate agreement, and that a cookie banner is not a HIPAA authorization. But on June 20, 2024, a federal court in the Northern District of Texas vacated the part of that guidance saying HIPAA is triggered when a tracker links an IP address to a visit on a public, unauthenticated page about a health condition. HHS later withdrew its appeal, per Proskauer.

That ruling helps, and it doesn’t make everything safe. Your booking flow, intake forms and anything behind a login are still the risky parts, and Meta’s own health rules apply no matter what a court said.

Counting conversions without sending health details is doable. Fire a generic “appointment requested” event with no condition or service in it, keep URLs free of terms like “melanoma” or “acne,” and track calls through a provider that will sign a BAA. Our GA4 and Google Ads tracking guide covers the mechanics. Have your compliance counsel sign off on the final setup; we’re marketers, not your lawyers.

Before-and-after photos and patient consent

Cosmetic dermatology sells with photos. So does a lot of medical dermatology, honestly: acne scarring, melasma and psoriasis results are some of the most convincing images a practice can post.

Under HIPAA, using a patient’s information for marketing generally requires their written authorization, per HHS’s marketing guidance. Full-face photos are identifiers, and a distinctive tattoo or birthmark can be too. Treatment consent forms don’t cover marketing use.

The process we’d set up:

  1. A separate marketing authorization that names where photos may appear (website, Instagram, ads, printed materials) and lets the patient choose.
  2. Same lighting, angle and background for before and after, with no filters or retouching on the result.
  3. A note on each image of the treatment, the number of sessions and the time between photos.
  4. A log of who signed what, and a way to pull a photo everywhere if a patient revokes.

Reviews and HIPAA-safe replies

Dermatology reviews are emotional. People write about the scar, the bill, the wait, or the doctor who caught something early. Your reply is read by every future patient, and it can be a HIPAA violation if you get defensive.

This isn’t theoretical. A New Jersey practice paid HHS $30,000 after its replies to negative online reviews disclosed patients’ health information, according to HHS. Confirming that someone is a patient can be enough of a disclosure to cause problems.

A reply pattern that works:

  • Positive review: “Thank you for taking the time to write this. We’re glad you’re happy with your experience at our Woodlands office.” No mention of the treatment, even if they named it.
  • Negative review: “We take every concern seriously and would like to talk. Please call our practice manager at [number].” Nothing about the visit, the diagnosis or the bill.

To get more reviews, send every patient the same link by text after the visit, and don’t gate it. Our Google reviews guide has request scripts you can adapt.

The cosmetic services funnel, from first look to membership

Cosmetic patients in The Woodlands, Sugar Land and Memorial shop around. They follow your Instagram for months, book a consult, ask about price, leave and think about it. The practices that win are the ones that follow up.

Where we see dermatology practices leak:

  • No prices online. You don’t need a full menu, but “starting at” pricing for neurotoxin, filler and peels filters tire-kickers and builds trust.
  • Consults with no next step. Every consult should end with a booked treatment, a quote sent by email, or a scheduled follow-up text.
  • No reason to come back. A membership or a simple treatment plan (“every 12 weeks”) keeps the schedule full between campaigns.
  • The medical patients never hear about cosmetic. Your skin-check patients already trust you. A quiet mention at checkout and in one email a quarter is fine, and nobody needs a hard sell.

A first 90 days for a practice starting from scratch: month one fixes the profile, insurance pages, provider pages and tracking. Month two launches search by service, split by medical and cosmetic, and starts review requests. Month three adds Meta for cosmetic, the email list, and the seasonal calendar. Frequently asked questions

How do dermatologists in The Woodlands and Houston get more patients?

Make it easy to find you, verify you and book you. That means a complete Google Business Profile, separate pages for medical and cosmetic services, insurance pages by carrier, provider pages for PAs and NPs with open schedules, online booking with real availability, and steady review requests. Then add search ads by service, timed around skin cancer season in May and deductible season in the fall.

Can a Texas dermatologist advertise as board certified?

Yes, if the certifying board qualifies under Texas Medical Board rules. That means an ABMS member board such as the American Board of Dermatology, an AOA Bureau of Osteopathic Specialists board, or an organization the TMB has approved as substantially equivalent. Texas doesn’t allow the terms board eligible or board qualified in advertising. Naming the specific board is the safest wording.

Can I use my patient list for Google or Meta ad targeting?

Generally no, for health-related promotion. Google’s Health in personalized advertising policy doesn’t support Customer Match, your own data segments or lookalikes for health conditions or invasive procedures, which includes cosmetic injections. Meta restricts health and wellness advertisers too. Patient lists also raise HIPAA questions. Target with keywords and location instead, and email patients who opted in.

Are tracking pixels allowed on a Houston dermatology website?

Some are, with care. A June 2024 federal court ruling vacated the part of HHS guidance covering public, unauthenticated pages, but tracking on patient portals, logged-in booking and intake forms still raises HIPAA issues, and vendors that receive health information need a business associate agreement. Keep condition names out of events and URLs and have counsel review the setup.

When should a dermatology practice run skin cancer screening ads?

Start building in April and push hardest in May, which is Skin Cancer Awareness Month and includes Melanoma Monday on the first Monday. Keep a steady presence through Houston’s summer, then run a second push from October to December when patients who met their deductible want appointments. In January, focus on insurance updates. Scale every push to the openings you actually have.
