Dog Daycare and Boarding Marketing in Houston: Fill the Slow Weeks

The Wednesday before Thanksgiving, every run is full. There’s a waitlist of 40 names, and the front desk spends the day apologizing to people who called in October and were told to “check back.”

Six weeks later, the second Tuesday in January, a third of those runs are empty and the daycare yard is half full by 10 a.m.

Holiday boarding sells itself. Nearly all the real work in dog daycare and boarding marketing in Houston happens in that second scene, the January Tuesday. You fill weekday daycare and the shoulder months by getting the temperament evaluation booked fast and turning first visits into packages and memberships. Your webcams and daily photos are the best content you’ll ever have. And Google Ads should only reach the neighborhoods and commute paths your clients drive every day.

Below is how we’d run it for a pet resort in Spring, Cypress or The Woodlands. Where we could verify a number, we cite it. Where we’re guessing, we say so.

Holiday boarding fills itself. Weekday daycare doesn’t.

Look at the travel numbers and you’ll see why November and December are never your problem. AAA expected a record 81.8 million Americans to travel for Thanksgiving 2025, including 5.8 million Texans, according to Axios Houston, citing AAA. For the year-end stretch from December 20 to January 1, AAA’s year-end forecast projected 122.4 million travelers.

A big share of those people own dogs, and none of them can take the Lab to their in-laws’ in Midland. Holiday boarding demand outruns supply in most of the Houston area every year. Spending on ads to sell more of it is like advertising umbrellas during a tropical storm.

Holiday boarding needs good operations. Mostly that means:

  • A published date when holiday booking opens, emailed to current clients first.
  • Deposits, a minimum stay for peak dates, and a clear cancellation window.
  • A waitlist that actually gets worked when cancellations come in (and they will, usually the week of).
  • Holiday rates, posted. If you can’t fit everyone, price is the polite way to decide.

The ad money belongs in the valleys.

January and February, for a start. Late August through September, when school is back and vacations stop. Then there are Mondays and Fridays in daycare, which run lighter than midweek at a lot of facilities now that so many Houston offices are on hybrid schedules. Check your own numbers before you believe us on that one, though; a facility near the Energy Corridor and one in Magnolia won’t look the same.

How to fill a dog boarding facility in the slow months

The people boarding a dog in February are mostly not on vacation. Think of the business traveler flying out of Bush Intercontinental on Monday, or the couple headed to a wedding in Austin for a long weekend. Add the family whose floors are being redone and the household that just moved to Montgomery County and hasn’t picked a vet yet.

They book late, and they book from their phones.

  • Make same-week boarding bookable online. If someone has to call during business hours to check availability, you’ve lost the 10 p.m. booker.
  • Price midweek differently. A Sunday-to-Thursday rate gives travelers a reason to choose you without cheapening your peak dates.
  • Sell add-ons: a bath before pickup, an extra playgroup, a nature walk. They raise the ticket in months when volume is low, and they’re a lot easier to walk back later than a discount.
  • Email past boarding clients in early January. A plain “we have openings for your next trip” to people who already trust you will beat any cold ad.
  • If your building has a backup generator, put it on the boarding page. Anyone who sat through Beryl’s outages in July 2024 doesn’t need it explained.

Spring break deserves its own planning. Most Houston-area districts take it in March, but not always the same week, and families in Conroe ISD, Klein, Cy-Fair and Tomball may be on different calendars. Pull the district calendars for your area each fall and treat each break week as a mini-holiday with its own opening date and minimum stay.

The temperament evaluation is where daycare clients convert

Most daycare websites treat the evaluation as a hurdle. We’d call it the most important sales step you have.

Nobody buys a 20-day package from a website. They book an evaluation (or meet-and-greet, or trial day, whatever you call it), watch how the staff handles their dog, and decide right there in the lobby. Friction before that visit quietly costs you clients, and the ten minutes your staff spends with the owner afterward probably matters more than anything on your homepage.

Where facilities usually lose people:

  1. The eval can only be booked by phone. Put it on your booking software with real time slots, including at least one early-morning option for people who work.
  2. Vaccine records hold everything up. Ask for the upload at booking and spell out exactly which vaccines you require. A reminder two days before helps.
  3. Nobody follows up. A dog that passes should go home with a card or text that says “Biscuit did great in the small-dog group. Here’s a photo.” and an offer to book the first package.
  4. Dogs that don’t pass get a form letter. Offer a different group, a training referral, or boarding in a private suite. The owner who feels respected still leaves a good review.

If you track one number, make it evaluations booked to first package purchased. A low rate means more ad spend won’t help. A high one means every evaluation you add is worth real money, and you can work out what you can afford to pay for one. Our post on cost per lead walks through that math.

Daycare packages and memberships: what to sell instead of single days

Single days pay the light bill. Memberships build the business, because a dog that comes every Tuesday and Thursday becomes part of the family’s week.

The usual ladder runs from a single day up through a 10-day pack and a 20-day pack, topped by a monthly membership for two, three or five days a week. Price it so the membership is clearly the best deal per day, and so the 10-pack expires in a reasonable window (dogs that come once every few weeks don’t get the social benefit, and you can’t staff around them).

Memberships also fix your weak days. If Mondays and Fridays are light, a three-day membership that must include Monday or Friday costs you nothing to offer and fills a yard you’re already staffing. Some facilities give members first access to holiday boarding dates. We’d bet on that perk over any discount, because holiday spots are the thing your clients actually worry about.

Please don’t build eight package tiers. Picture your front desk trying to explain them to a customer holding a leash in each hand, and then picture your software reports. Four or five options is plenty.

Webcams, report cards and the content they give you

Webcams used to be a perk. Now they’re close to expected, especially from first-time daycare clients and anyone who’s ever read a scary news story about a boarding facility.

They’re also free marketing. The owner watching their dog play at 11 a.m. from a desk in the Galleria is a client who isn’t going anywhere. Mention the cams on your evaluation page with a screenshot, and say plainly which areas are covered (if the suites aren’t on camera, people will ask, so tell them first).

Then put the footage and your staff’s phone photos to work:

  • Daily report cards with one photo. Owners screenshot them and post them, which makes it the best social proof in the business, and you didn’t have to write a word of it.
  • Short Instagram Reels and TikToks of playgroups, splash-pad days, birthday pupcakes and the dog who refuses to leave at pickup. Ten seconds is enough.
  • “Who’s here today” posts on Facebook and Instagram stories, where regulars tag each other.
  • Once a month, a behind-the-scenes post about cleaning, staff training or how you split playgroups. Boring to you, reassuring to a nervous first-timer.

Get photo consent in your intake forms, and keep staff faces off camera unless they agree. If you want to put a little money behind the best posts, boost them to people within a few miles, not the whole metro. Google Ads for dog daycare: radius, commute routes and settings

Daycare gets chosen on the way to somewhere. People pick a place near home or on the drive to work, and almost never one that’s far from both. Geography is where we see the most wasted spend in these accounts.

Targeting along commuter routes makes sense here, within limits. A facility near SH-249 in Tomball can reasonably target homes in Tomball, Cypress and northwest Spring, because the people driving 249 toward the Beltway pass it every morning. A facility near I-45 and the Grand Parkway might target The Woodlands, Spring and Conroe. What doesn’t work is targeting downtown office ZIPs because some commuters end up there; someone searching from their desk on Louisiana Street wants daycare near home, and the ad is hard to aim.

Settings we’d use:

  • Location: a radius of roughly 5 to 8 miles, or a ZIP list drawn along your actual commute paths. In location options, switch to “Presence”. Google’s default, “Presence or interest”, also shows ads to people elsewhere showing interest in your area. Per Google Ads location options, “Presence” covers people in or regularly in your locations, which conveniently includes people who work nearby.
  • Keywords: “dog daycare near me”, “doggy daycare the woodlands”, “dog boarding spring tx”, “pet resort cypress”, “kennel near me”. Keep daycare and boarding in separate ad groups with separate landing pages.
  • Negatives: “jobs”, “hiring”, “how to start”, “franchise”, “free”, “volunteer”. Daycare searches pull in a lot of job seekers.
  • Schedule: heavier on weekday evenings and Sunday nights, when people plan their week.
  • Budget: for one location, $20 to $40 a day is where we’d start a test (our working range, not a published benchmark). Our Houston cost-per-click guide covers what clicks tend to cost here.

Also check Local Services Ads. Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes “Pet boarding”, though we didn’t see dog daycare listed by name. If it’s offered at your ZIP, we’d give a pay-per-lead test for boarding inquiries a month.

None of the bidding matters much if the landing page is your homepage. Send the click to a page with the yard, the cams, the evaluation steps, your vaccine rules and real prices on it. Or let our search marketing team build it with you.

Google Business Profile categories for a pet resort

You get one primary category, and Google leans on it harder than almost anything else on the profile. The usual candidates are “Dog day care center”, “Pet boarding service” and “Kennel”, with “Pet groomer” and “Dog trainer” as secondaries if you offer them.

Choose the primary by revenue and by what you want to rank for. A facility that makes most of its money from daycare memberships should lead with “Dog day care center”. One that’s mostly boarding should lead with “Pet boarding service”. Don’t flip it seasonally; category changes can trigger a fresh review of your profile and rarely do what owners hope.

The rest of the profile is housekeeping, and most facilities let it slide. Hours should reflect drop-off and pickup windows, with holiday hours entered ahead of every closure. List daycare, boarding, grooming and training as separate services with short descriptions. Upload real photos every month of the yard, the suites, the lobby and the dogs (with permission). And ask every client for a review after a first boarding stay and again after the first month of daycare, with no discount attached, since Google’s policy prohibits incentives.

New facilities keep opening along the Grand Parkway. A complete profile with recent reviews won’t stop them, but it’s the cheapest defense you have.

Do you need a license to open a dog daycare or boarding facility in Texas?

We looked for a statewide Texas license for dog daycare or boarding and couldn’t find one. Texas doesn’t license groomers statewide either, as News 4 San Antonio reported. In practice, the rules come from cities and from your zoning and deed restrictions, so treat this as a starting point and check with your city and county before signing a lease.

Inside Houston city limits, the city’s Commercial Pet Facility Permit applies to any place where dogs or cats over four months old are kept for a commercial purpose (veterinarians and breeders are excluded). The permitting center lists a $200 application fee, a $200 renewal, plan review, and an annual cycle: facilities must be licensed by January 1 each year. The application also asks for a deed restriction and law compliance statement. The Woodlands is unincorporated (governed by The Woodlands Township, mostly in Montgomery County), so a resort there or in Spring or Cypress will be dealing with different authorities than one inside Houston.

For marketing, the missing state license cuts in your favor if you handle it right. Nervous owners have nothing official to check, so they look for other proof. Put your permit (if you have one), insurance, staff training, vaccine policy, playgroup rules and emergency plan on one page. That page will be read by almost every first-time client before they book the evaluation.

A month-by-month marketing plan for a Houston pet resort

  1. January: email past boarding clients about open dates. Run Google search ads for daycare evaluations. Push memberships with a start-of-year offer.
  2. February: publish spring break opening dates for the districts near you. Keep daycare ads running.
  3. March to May: handle spring break like a holiday, then use April and May to sign daycare members before summer.
  4. June to August: summer boarding mostly fills itself. Shift content to heat safety, indoor play and splash days. Watch daycare utilization as kids are home.
  5. Late August to September: back-to-school dip. Your second-best window for daycare ads and new-member offers.
  6. October: open holiday booking to members first, then all clients. Turn boarding ads down, or off.
  7. November to December: operations, not marketing. Work the waitlist, collect reviews after every stay, and plan January.

Most pet resorts we look at do this backwards. Ad money goes out when the phones are already ringing and stops when the runs empty.

Frequently asked questions

How do I market my dog daycare business?

Make the temperament evaluation easy to book online, follow up the same day with a photo and a package offer, and sell memberships instead of single days. Use webcams and daily report cards as social content, keep your Google Business Profile complete with fresh reviews, and run Google search ads only within a few miles of your facility and along your clients' commute.

How do I fill my dog boarding kennel during slow months?

Target the people who board outside holidays: business travelers, long-weekend trips, families moving or renovating. Offer midweek rates and add-ons instead of discounts, make same-week booking possible online, and email past boarding clients in early January and late August. Short bursts of Google search ads work better in these months than in November.

Should a dog boarding facility advertise for the holidays?

Usually not. Thanksgiving and Christmas boarding sells out across the Houston area without ads. Put that energy into operations instead: announce an opening date for holiday reservations, require deposits and minimum stays, offer members first access and work a waitlist for cancellations. Save the ad budget for January, February and the back-to-school dip.

What Google Ads settings work for a dog daycare?

Use a radius of about 5 to 8 miles or a list of ZIP codes along your clients' commute, and switch location options to Presence so you only reach people in or regularly in that area. Split daycare and boarding into separate ad groups, add negatives like jobs and hiring, and send each ad group to its own landing page.

Do you need a license to run a dog daycare in Texas?

We couldn't find a statewide Texas license for dog daycare or boarding, so local rules matter most. Inside Houston city limits, the Commercial Pet Facility Permit from BARC applies to places keeping dogs or cats for a commercial purpose, with a $200 fee and annual renewal by January 1. Check zoning, deed restrictions and city or county rules elsewhere.
