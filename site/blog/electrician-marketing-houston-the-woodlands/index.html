First week of June. The forecast shows something spinning in the Gulf, and by Wednesday afternoon your office has taken nine calls. Six want a standby generator quote “before it gets here.” One is a breaker that keeps tripping in a 1980s house in Kingwood. One is a property manager with a dead parking lot light in Spring. The last one asks whether you install Tesla chargers, and nobody is sure what to tell him about the tax credit.

Three weeks later the storm misses, the generator callers go quiet, and you’re back to waiting on the phone.

Electrician marketing in Houston and The Woodlands comes down to two jobs. The first is owning the everyday service searches (“electrician near me,” “breaker keeps tripping”) through Google Maps, Local Services Ads and reviews, so the phone rings in every season. The second is building demand for the high-ticket project work, mainly panel upgrades, generators, EV chargers and rewiring, before customers are panicking, and showing up first when they are.

This is how we’d set it up for a residential or light commercial shop with two to fifteen trucks, with the actual settings and the Texas rules that apply.

Your TDLR license number is a marketing asset (and a legal requirement)

Start with the rule most shops half-follow. In Texas, the license isn’t just something you hang on the office wall.

Per TDLR’s electrician compliance guide, a licensed electrical contractor has to put its name and license number on both sides of every business vehicle, in contrasting lettering at least two inches tall, with the number shown as “TECL” plus the number. Proposals, invoices and written contracts need your name, address, phone number and license number, and invoices and contracts also carry TDLR’s regulatory statement. Advertising is covered too. Under 16 TAC §73.51, solicitation ads and business cards must include your name and license number, with narrow exceptions such as phone solicitations, where you have to give the number on request.

So put it everywhere a customer looks, on purpose:

  • The site header or footer on every page, in text, not buried in an image.
  • Your Google Business Profile description (“Licensed Texas electrical contractor, TECL 12345”).
  • Your landing pages for Google Ads, next to the phone number.
  • Yard signs, door hangers, truck wraps and the quote PDF.

Homeowners in The Woodlands and Sugar Land have all heard a story about a handyman doing electrical work. A visible TECL number, plus a line like “we pull the permit,” answers that worry before they voice it. It also matters in the City of Houston, where the City of Houston’s generator permit page says electrical permits are only issued to registered master electricians, with plan review and an inspection required. Most unlicensed competitors can’t say “permitted and inspected.” You can.

Google Business Profile for electricians: categories, service areas, photos

For most electrical shops, Google Maps is the biggest free source of service calls. Get the settings right first.

  • Primary category: Electrician. Then add secondary categories that match real work you want more of. Search the category list in your profile editor for the installation and repair options and pick only what you do.
  • Service area: most electricians don’t have customers visit the shop, so per Google’s service-area rules you remove the address and list service areas instead. You get up to 20, defined by city or ZIP code, never a radius, and Google says the whole area shouldn’t stretch past about two hours of driving from your base. For a shop in Conroe, that might be The Woodlands, Spring, Montgomery, Magnolia, Willis, Tomball and the ZIP codes in between.
  • Services: fill in the services list with plain descriptions. “Panel replacement and 200-amp service upgrades, permit and inspection included.”
  • Photos: real panels you’ve replaced (the before shot of a scorched bus bar does more selling than any stock image), generator pads, your trucks with the TECL lettering showing, and your techs in uniform.
  • Hours: if you offer emergency service, say so and mean it. If a 10 p.m. call goes to voicemail, don’t list 24 hours.

Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the rest, including why proximity to the searcher limits how far a single profile can reach.

Reviews that mention the job

Reviews are the tiebreaker in the map pack and on LSA. The policy is simple. Per Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps, you can’t pay or discount for reviews, can’t ask only the happy customers, and can’t discourage negative ones. Send every customer the same link.

The timing and wording are where shops differ. Send the request by text the same day the job closes, from the tech’s name if your software allows it:

“Hi Dana, this is Marcus from [Company]. Thanks for having us out to replace the panel today. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other folks in Spring find a licensed electrician: [link]. Any problems at all, call me directly at [number].”

Customers who describe the job (“replaced our Federal Pacific panel,” “installed a 22kW generator”) give future customers exactly what they’re scanning for. You can’t script what they say, but naming the job in your request makes it more likely. The complete guide to Google reviews has more templates.

Local Services Ads for electricians

Electricians is one of the categories on Google’s Local Services Ads category list, so you can run the pay-per-lead ads that sit above the regular search ads, with the Google Verified badge once you clear Google’s screening. You pay for a call or message rather than a click, and you can dispute leads that weren’t real customers, such as job seekers or wrong numbers.

LSA tends to be strongest for service calls, the “electrician near me” kind of search. It’s weaker for planned projects like generators, because you can’t write ad copy or send people to a generator landing page; the ad is basically your name, rating and phone number.

A few things make LSA work for electrical shops:

  1. Turn on only the job types you want. If you don’t do ceiling fan installs at a profit, switch them off.
  2. Set ad scheduling to hours someone answers. LSA ranking leans on responsiveness, and missed calls cost you twice.
  3. Mark every lead as booked or not in the app. It trains your own reporting, and it keeps you honest about what each lead is worth.
  4. Get reviews coming in steadily. A shop with 200 reviews and a 4.8 will usually outrank one with 25 reviews on the same street.

Generators and hurricane season: market before the storm

No product in Houston electrical work swings harder with the news than standby generators, and the swings have names.

Winter Storm Uri hit Feb. 15 to 18, 2021, in what federal regulators called the largest controlled blackout in U.S. history, per the Texas Tribune. That May, FOX 26 Houston reported one Houston generator company had fielded about 10,000 inquiries since February, with roughly six months from order to install and whole-home units starting around $10,000. Then Hurricane Beryl came ashore on July 8, 2024, and at the peak more than 2.26 million CenterPoint customers lost power, about 80% of its customers, according to Utility Dive.

After Beryl, the bottleneck moved. In September 2024, ABC13 reported that CenterPoint, which reviews and approves automatic transfer switches before a generator can be connected, had a backlog of applications. CenterPoint told customers to allow up to a month, and documents the station reviewed showed average waits near eight weeks. One family in the story had a generator sitting installed and unusable.

What that means for your marketing:

  • Sell generators in the off-season. Run your strongest generator campaigns from January through May, with messages like “Get installed before June 1.” Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30, and the people who wait for a named storm are the ones who end up on a waitlist.
  • Show the real timeline. Put the steps on your generator page, including utility approval and the city permit. Customers who know it takes weeks are calmer, and calmer customers leave better reviews.
  • Have a storm mode ready. Build an ad group and landing page now that says you’re taking generator consultations and gives the current lead time. Switch it on the afternoon a storm is named. Turn it off when the inquiries stop converting.
  • Market maintenance. Every generator you install is a yearly service visit, and the spring “test it before the season” reminder is one of the easiest emails you’ll send.

Freezes count too. A February cold snap pulls the same searches as a hurricane, smaller and shorter, so keep the generator campaign live with a modest budget all year rather than off entirely.

EV chargers: update your pages for the end of the federal credit

Plenty of electrician websites still say “get 30% back with the federal tax credit” on their EV charger page. Check yours.

The federal 30C credit covered 30% of the cost of a home charger, up to $1,000 per charging port, and only in eligible low-income or non-urban census tracts. Per the IRS page on the credit, it applies to property placed in service through June 30, 2026. A charger installed today doesn’t qualify, and a page that implies otherwise is the kind of thing that turns into a bad review.

The demand doesn’t depend on the credit, though. People who buy an EV in Katy or Cypress still need a 240-volt circuit in the garage, and many need a panel upgrade to support it. That combined job is what we’d market:

  • A dedicated EV page per charger type you install, with a “text us photos of your panel and garage” quote option. Photo quotes cut a site visit out of a small job.
  • Keyword targeting on charger brand plus “install” or “installer.”
  • A clear note when a panel upgrade may be needed, so the bigger quote doesn’t feel like an upsell.
  • Permit language. The City of Houston has its own permit type for EV charging outlets, and saying you handle it beats leaving the customer to wonder.

New-construction builders in Bridgeland and Sienna are worth a referral conversation too. Their buyers ask who to call.

Service-area pages that rank in Spring, Katy and Sugar Land

One profile won’t rank everywhere you drive. Location pages on your website help fill the gaps, as long as each one is actually about that place.

A thin page that swaps the city name into the same 300 words doesn’t help anyone. A useful one includes:

  • The neighborhoods you work in (Grogan’s Mill and Panther Creek in The Woodlands, Cinco Ranch in Katy, First Colony in Sugar Land) and the kind of housing stock there.
  • Photos and short write-ups of recent jobs in that area, with the customer’s permission.
  • Reviews from customers in that city.
  • Permit notes. The City of Houston, unincorporated Harris and Montgomery County, and suburban cities each handle electrical permits their own way, and naming the right office shows you’ve worked there.
  • Your typical arrival window from your shop.

Older homes inside the Loop and in established suburbs like Kingwood and Clear Lake bring rewiring, panel replacement and insurance-driven inspection work. Newer master-planned communities bring EV chargers, generator pads and ceiling fan and lighting jobs in homes where the builder ran minimum circuits. Write each page for what you actually do there. Financing and membership plans close the big tickets

A $1,200 repair is a decision. A $14,000 generator or a $3,500 panel upgrade is a household budget conversation, and the shop that offers monthly payments often wins it even at a higher price.

On financing, our advice is mostly about presentation:

  • Show a “from $X per month” figure on the generator and panel pages, from whatever lender you use, with the lender’s required disclosures.
  • Have techs mention financing at the estimate, not after the customer flinches.
  • Put the pre-qualification link in the follow-up text that goes out with the quote.

Membership plans are the other underused tool. HVAC and plumbing companies have run them for years (it’s a model we’ve seen up close working with Weiss Air Conditioning & Plumbing). For an electrician, a plan might bundle a yearly safety inspection, generator maintenance, priority scheduling and a discount on repairs. The marketing value isn’t the fee. It’s that members call you first for the next job instead of searching, and every inspection visit creates a list of future work.

None of it works if calls go to voicemail. Route ad and LSA calls to a person, answer forms within five minutes, and listen to ten recorded calls a month; our speed-to-lead guide has the follow-up sequence. Then track cost per booked job by campaign, not cost per lead. A generator lead that costs $150 and turns into a $14,000 install is cheap. A $40 lead for a single outlet swap that eats a two-hour drive from Conroe to Pearland isn’t. Set your goals by job type once you have three months of real data.

A first 90 days, roughly:

  1. Month one: fix the profile, add TECL everywhere, build the panel, generator and EV pages, set up call tracking, start review requests.
  2. Month two: launch LSA for service calls and search campaigns for panels and generators, with the negative list from above.
  3. Month three: cut what isn’t booking, add two or three service-area pages, and set up the storm-mode campaign so it’s ready to switch on.

Frequently asked questions

How do electricians get more customers?

Start with a complete Google Business Profile using Electrician as the primary category, accurate service areas and real job photos, then ask every customer for a review by text the same day. Add Local Services Ads for service calls and Google search ads for panel upgrades, generators and EV chargers, each sending traffic to its own page. Answer calls fast, because the first shop to pick up usually gets the job.

Do Texas electricians have to show their license number in ads?

Yes. TDLR rules require electrical contractors to include their name and license number on advertising and business cards, with limited exceptions such as phone solicitations, where the number must be provided on request. Vehicles need the name and TECL number on both sides in letters at least two inches tall, and proposals, invoices and contracts must show the license number. Displaying it prominently also builds trust.

Are Local Services Ads worth it for electricians?

Often, for service and repair calls. Electricians is a listed Local Services Ads category, you pay per lead instead of per click, and you can dispute leads that aren't real customers. LSA is less useful for planned projects like generators because you can't write custom ads or choose a landing page. Many shops run LSA for service calls and regular Google Ads for project work.

When should I advertise standby generator installation in Houston?

Run your strongest generator campaigns from January through May, before hurricane season starts June 1. After big outages like Uri and Beryl, demand spiked and installs backed up for weeks or months, including utility transfer switch approvals. Keep a smaller campaign running year-round, have a storm-mode ad group ready to switch on when a storm is named, and show customers the real installation timeline.

Can I still advertise the federal tax credit for EV charger installation?

Not for new installs. The federal 30C credit covered 30% of the cost up to $1,000 per charging port in eligible census tracts, but it applies only to property placed in service through June 30, 2026. Remove or update any website copy or ads that promise the credit. EV owners still need 240-volt circuits and sometimes panel upgrades, so the service is still worth marketing.
