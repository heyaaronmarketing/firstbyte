She signed the lease on a salon suite off Research Forest Drive in March. The room is beautiful: steamer, LED panel, a good bed, ring light clipped to the shelf. Her Instagram has 1,900 followers, most of them other estheticians. The book is about 40% full, and the girl two doors down keeps asking if she wants to “collab.”

We hear some version of that from solo estheticians in The Woodlands and Houston constantly.

The esthetician marketing ideas we’d actually use aren’t clever. Advertise only what your Texas license covers. Get a Google Business Profile that works for a suite, which takes more care than it should. Post the work, and leave the close-ups of problem skin off your feed. Then build pricing around a membership and rebook every client before she leaves the chair, since one-off facials won’t fill a solo book on their own.

Instagram helps. Google books.

A lot of national advice for “how to market yourself as an esthetician” was written for other states or for med spas. Some of it can get a Texas esthetician in trouble, so we’ll start there.

What a Texas esthetician can and can’t advertise

Estheticians in Texas are licensed by TDLR, and the scope is narrower than a lot of Instagram menus suggest. We went to the agency’s own pages rather than the forums.

TDLR’s medical spa page says estheticians may perform “light” or “superficial” peels that don’t pierce the dermis, and microdermabrasion that removes only dead cells from the epidermis. Medium and deep peels are medical procedures regulated by the Texas Medical Board. Deeper microdermabrasion that reaches the dermis has to be administered or delegated by a physician. The same page lists “medical-grade facial treatments” among the services that fall under medical treatment and must be provided under a physician’s order and delegation. If you’re a solo esthetician, we’d drop the phrase “medical-grade” from your menu and your ads entirely.

TDLR’s “Who regulates what” list sends microneedling, chemical peeling, Botox and lasers beyond hair removal to the Texas Medical Board or the Department of State Health Services. Dermaplaning is the one that surprises people: the list puts dermaplaning with a non-scalpel razor under a Class A Barber license, and the agency’s esthetician scope of practice page lists shaving a person’s face among things an esthetician license doesn’t cover. If dermaplaning is on your menu, call TDLR before you build ads around it.

The Legislature has been tightening this, too. Senate Bill 378 passed both chambers in 2025 with a September 1, 2025 effective date. It bars barbering and cosmetology license holders from making an incision into the dermis, including to inject something, or using a device that requires a practitioner’s order, unless they’re separately authorized. It was written in response to “Botox parties.”

Google Business Profile for a salon suite esthetician

Suite-based estheticians hit the same handful of Business Profile problems over and over.

Your suite is a real location, so show the address. TDLR’s medical spa page says estheticians may only perform esthetic services at a TDLR-licensed establishment. That means a facial business can’t be a pure service-area business that hides its address and drives to clients. Your suite should hold a mini-establishment license; TDLR’s mini-establishment FAQ describes it as a room or suite leased inside a licensed establishment, and the license has to be displayed inside the suite.

The suite number goes in address line 2. Google’s Business Profile guidelines say so, and that businesses showing an address should keep permanent signage at it. In a suite building, that means your name on the door and on the lobby directory. A suite with no sign is the kind of profile that gets asked for video verification.

Hours should be the hours you’re actually in the room. Google expects a location to be staffed and able to receive customers during its stated hours, so if you work Tuesday through Saturday, don’t list Monday because it looks fuller.

Pick categories carefully. “Facial spa” or “Skin care clinic” usually fits best as primary, with “Waxing hair removal service” or “Eyelash salon” as secondary categories if those are real parts of your book. Skip “Medical spa” unless a physician is involved; it invites the wrong searches and the wrong scrutiny.

Expect friction with the booking button. Square’s Reserve with Google help page says booth and room renters don’t qualify for Reserve with Google. Other platforms have their own rules. If the integrated button isn’t available, add your booking page as an appointment link instead.

One more: don’t create a second profile for each specialty (“Jane’s Brow Studio” and “Jane’s Acne Facials” at the same suite). Google’s guidelines say a practitioner shouldn’t have multiple profiles to cover specializations, and it’s an easy way to lose both.

How to get more facial clients from Instagram and TikTok

Instagram is where clients check you out. It is rarely where they first find you, and once you accept that, what you post changes.

Most esthetician accounts are built for other estheticians. Product flat lays, tool close-ups, industry memes. Those posts get likes from peers in Phoenix and zero bookings in Spring. The posts that book are the ones a stranger in The Woodlands can picture herself in.

  • The process, start to finish. A 25-second reel of a real facial: cleanse, steam, extractions (gently), mask, the client’s face at the end. Hands and room, not just glowing skin.
  • You talking. Answer one question per video: “Can I get a facial if I’m on tretinoin?” “Why your skin breaks out after a Houston summer.” Face to camera, natural light, no script beyond three bullet points.
  • The suite itself: parking, the walk in, the bed. First-timers are nervous about walking into a building full of doors, and a 10-second clip fixes that.
  • Real clients, with written consent. A short clip of a regular saying why she comes back. Keep the signed consent with her client card.

TikTok is a bigger reach engine and a weaker booking engine for a local suite. We’d post the same reels there and not stress about it.

Paid ads are a different set of rules. Meta’s personal health and appearance standard bars health and appearance ads that imply or try to create negative self-perception, and its own examples mark a close-up image of wrinkles as non-compliant. That rules out the zoomed-in acne or texture “before” shots that do well organically. Run ads on calm, finished-skin shots, the room, and you on camera. Organic posts don’t go through ad review, but keep your claims inside your scope there too. We cover platform differences in Facebook vs Instagram vs TikTok.

Esthetician pricing and memberships that fill a solo book

Content and ads only bring in first visits. Whether a suite survives its second year gets decided on the price sheet.

Start with capacity. Say you do five 75-minute facials a day, four days a week. That’s 20 a week, roughly 85 a month. If most clients come every four to six weeks, a full book is somewhere around 100 to 130 regulars. You don’t need thousands of followers. You need about a hundred people who rebook.

Price against the market you’re in. When we checked in October 2026, the Massage Envy near Market Street listed a 60-minute Hydrafacial at $290 for non-members and $210 as a member rate. That franchise is your real price anchor in The Woodlands, and most suite estheticians we see underprice it badly. For context, the Bureau of Labor Statistics puts median pay for skincare specialists at $45,330 and says 29% are self-employed. That median includes employees at chains and spas. A suite owner who prices well should beat it, and one who underprices often won’t.

Then sell a membership. A simple version: one signature facial a month on auto-draft, 10% off retail, a free add-on each quarter, unused facials roll over once. If 40 members pay $135 a month, that’s $5,400 of predictable revenue before a single new client books. (The chart above is illustrative; plug in your own rates.)

Retail matters more than estheticians like to admit, partly because selling feels awkward in a room that small. But a client on a home routine sees results faster and comes back sooner. Recommend two products. Seven is a lecture.

Houston weather is a skin care content calendar

Houston gives you a year of content for free, as long as you stay factual and inside your scope.

Summer is long here, and clients sweat through sunscreen. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends a broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, reapplied about every two hours or after swimming or sweating, and notes that no sunscreen is sweatproof. You can get a reel and an email out of that one paragraph, and it pairs naturally with the sunscreens on your shelf.

  • March to May: prom, graduation and wedding season. Sell facial series that end two to three days before the event.
  • June to August: sweat, breakouts under masks and helmets, pool and lake weekends at Lake Conroe. Short, cheap express treatments fill weekday gaps.
  • September to October: back-to-routine. Good month for membership launches.
  • November to December: holiday party facials and gift cards. Sell gift cards on your site, and see the gift card section of our massage client guide for the federal expiration rule.
  • January: resolutions and gift card redemptions. Have a first-visit offer that converts into the membership.

Keep claims about acne, rosacea and pigment modest. “Helps with” and “can improve the look of” are honest. “Cures,” “erases” and “clinical results” aren’t, and those claims tend to draw scrutiny from Meta’s ad review as well as TDLR.

Google Ads for estheticians: small budget, tight radius

Search ads are the closest thing to a reliable new-client faucet for a skin studio. People type “facial the woodlands” or “facial near me” when they’re ready to book.

For a solo esthetician, keep it small:

  • Keywords: facial plus town (“facial spring tx,” “facial the woodlands”), plus named services you’re known for, like “acne facial,” “brazilian wax the woodlands” or “lash extensions conroe.”
  • Negatives: jobs, school, course, license, DIY, at home, mask, products, Amazon, and anything out of scope like Botox, filler or microneedling.
  • Radius: about 15 to 20 minutes of real drive time. From a suite near the Grand Parkway, that might cover The Woodlands, Spring, Shenandoah, Magnolia and Tomball. It won’t cover Sugar Land.
  • Budget: $15 to $35 a day is a sensible starting range for one suite. Run it during the hours and days you have openings.
  • Landing page: a service page with prices, photos of your suite, your license info and a booking button. Not your Instagram.

We’d run these before spending a dollar on Meta. Search catches people who already want a facial; Meta has to talk them into it. Our search marketing page shows how we set them up.

Rebooking, referrals and reviews

The first facial is an audition. Rebook before she gets up: “Your skin should be ready for the next step in four weeks. Does Thursday the 14th at 10 work?” A suggested date gets booked far more often than “Text me when you want to come back.”

Then give her an easy reason to send a friend. A $20 credit for the referrer and the new client, tracked in your booking software, is plenty. Partnerships work too, with the lash artist down the hall, a brow artist, a local photographer before engagement shoots, or a massage therapist who can refer face-focused clients (an esthetician in Texas can massage the face, neck, shoulders and arms, but full-body work needs a massage license).

For reviews, ask by text the same evening, from your own name, with a direct link. Reviews that mention your name and the service (“Kelsey’s acne facial”) help you show up for those searches. See our guide to Google reviews for how to ask without breaking Google’s rules.

A 60-day marketing plan for a new esthetician suite

If you just signed a lease, this is the order we’d do things in:

  1. Week 1: mini-establishment license displayed, menu rewritten to match your scope, license info on the website and booking page.
  2. Week 2: Business Profile with suite number, signage photographed, categories set, services and prices listed, booking link added.
  3. Weeks 2 to 4: text every past client from your old salon (only the ones who gave you their number), with an opening-month offer that converts into a membership.
  4. Weeks 3 to 8: two reels a week from your own suite, one process video and one talking-head answer.
  5. Week 4: Google Ads on three or four service-plus-town keywords, tight radius, negatives in place.
  6. Ongoing: rebook every client in the chair, ask for every review the same day, check bookings by source monthly.

That’s plenty for one person. TikTok ads, influencer swaps and a newsletter can all wait for month three, if you still want them by then.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more facial clients as a new esthetician?

Start with a complete Google Business Profile for your suite, accurate services and prices, and a booking link. Text past clients with an opening offer, post short videos of real facials in your own room, and run a small Google Ads campaign on facial plus your town. Rebook every first-time client before she leaves, since second visits are what fill a solo book.

Can estheticians in Texas advertise microneedling or medical-grade facials?

Generally no, not under an esthetician license alone. TDLR directs microneedling and medium or deep peels to the Texas Medical Board, and its medical spa page lists medical-grade facial treatments among services that require a physician's order and delegation. Estheticians can advertise facials, superficial peels and epidermal microdermabrasion. Check TDLR's current guidance before adding services to your menu or ads.

Can a salon suite esthetician have a Google Business Profile?

Yes. A suite where you see clients is a real location, so list the street address with the suite number in address line 2, keep permanent signage on the door and in the lobby directory, and set hours when you're actually there. Don't create extra profiles for each specialty, and don't hide the address, since Texas requires esthetic services to happen at a licensed establishment.

Is Instagram or Google better for estheticians?

Use both, for different jobs. Google Search and the map pack catch people ready to book a facial in your town, so they usually bring more new clients per dollar. Instagram is where those people check your work, personality and suite before booking. Post consistently, but put your first ad dollars into Google search.

Should estheticians offer memberships?

For most solo estheticians, yes. A monthly membership with one facial, a retail discount and a small perk turns irregular visits into predictable revenue and keeps clients on a schedule that shows results. Keep the terms simple, allow limited rollover, and price it so the monthly facial is a modest discount from your regular rate rather than a deep cut.
