The FTC Fake Review Rule: What Houston and The Woodlands Businesses Can and Can’t Do

The FTC fake review rule for Houston and The Woodlands businesses: what’s banned, penalties, incentives, review gating, staff reviews and Google’s own rules.

“Can I give people $10 off their next visit for leaving a Google review?”

It’s the review question we hear most from owners in Houston and The Woodlands, and the honest answer is annoying. Under federal law, maybe. Under Google’s rules, no. Under Yelp’s, you shouldn’t even be asking.

The short answer: the FTC’s Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, in effect since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, buying positive or negative reviews, undisclosed reviews from staff and owners, fake “independent” review sites, review suppression and fake followers. Knowing violations can cost up to $53,088 each. Asking every real customer for an honest review is still fine.

This guide goes through the rule section by section with local examples, then covers what the FTC has actually enforced, where Google’s and Yelp’s rules are stricter, and how to run a review program that holds up everywhere. It’s general information, not legal advice.

What does the FTC fake review rule ban?

The rule (16 CFR Part 465) has six main prohibitions. The FTC’s Q&A on the rule walks through each. Here they are in plain language, with the kind of example we’d actually worry about at a local business.

SectionWhat it bansLocal example that crosses the line
465.2 Fake reviewsWriting, buying or selling reviews from people who don’t exist or didn’t use the business, or that misstate their experiencePaying a “review service” for 40 five-star reviews for a Conroe roofer
465.4 Buying sentimentIncentives conditioned on a positive (or negative) reviewA Katy restaurant offering free dessert “for a 5-star review”
465.5 Insider reviewsReviews by owners, managers or employees, or their solicited relatives, without a clear disclosureA Spring med spa’s front desk team reviewing the spa from personal accounts
465.6 Fake “independent” sitesClaiming a site you control offers independent reviewsA “Best Plumbers in The Woodlands” site secretly owned by one plumber
465.7 Review suppressionUnfounded legal threats, intimidation or false accusations to remove reviews; hiding negatives while implying you show them allThreatening a customer with a defamation suit over a truthful 2-star review
465.8 Fake social influenceBuying or selling fake followers, views or engagementBuying 10,000 Instagram followers for a new boutique on Market Street

The FTC’s August 2024 announcement called out AI-generated fake reviews by name. If a tool writes reviews from customers who never wrote them, that’s a fake review no matter how real the customer was.

Who can be liable is broader than owners expect. Per the FTC’s Q&A, review brokers, marketing agencies, PR firms and reputation management companies can be on the hook too. Ordinary customers can’t be liable for what they write.

How big are the penalties for fake reviews?

The rule’s main teeth is civil penalties, which the FTC couldn’t usually seek for a first offense before. The current maximum is $53,088 per violation, the figure the FTC cited in its December 2025 warning letters, per Benesch’s summary of the letters. The FTC didn’t adjust penalty amounts for 2026 because the government shutdown delayed the inflation data, so 2025 levels still apply, per a September 2026 Federal Register notice.

“Per violation” is the scary part. Forty bought reviews could be argued as forty violations.

Penalties apply to knowing violations. For buying reviews, the FTC uses a “knew or should have known” standard and lists red flags in its Q&A: reviews posted very fast after a purchase, a sudden burst, or reviews about the wrong product. There’s no private right of action under the rule, so a competitor can’t sue you under it, but they can report you to the FTC, to Google, and to the Texas Attorney General, who enforces the state’s Deceptive Trade Practices Act.

Has the FTC actually enforced the rule?

Yes, and the cases are getting closer to Main Street.

In December 2025, the FTC sent warning letters to 10 companies about possible violations, its first public use of the rule. Recipients had five days to confirm in writing what they’d fixed.

In April 2026, the FTC’s TruHeight case alleged a supplement company posted thousands of fake five-star reviews written by employees and gave away free or discounted product in exchange for five-star reviews. The proposed order required a $750,000 payment against a larger, partly suspended judgment, and bans buying reviews conditioned on any sentiment.

The one that should make home service owners sit up came in May 2026. On an FTC referral, a Justice Department complaint accused an Illinois company doing business as Premium Home Service of creating more than 15,000 fake Google Business Profiles for plumbing, heating and electrical work, using local-looking addresses and phone numbers that routed to overseas call centers, and posting fake reviews on them. It cites the reviews rule alongside the FTC Act. Those are allegations, not findings, but Houston contractors have been competing with exactly this kind of lead-gen listing for years.

Texas has its own history. The Texas Attorney General sued a reputation management company in Harris County district court in 2017, alleging it used lawsuits with fictitious parties to get online reviews taken down, under the Deceptive Trade Practices Act. Same idea as the federal rule’s suppression section, years earlier.

Can I offer a discount or gift card for a review?

This is where federal law and the platforms split, and it’s the source of most confusion.

Under the FTC rule, incentives aren’t banned unless they’re tied, openly or by implication, to a particular sentiment. “$10 off for any review, good or bad” can be legal federally. Generalized requests sent to all your customers are exempt from the insider rules even if an incentive is offered, per the FTC’s Q&A.

Under Google’s rules, it’s flatly out. Google’s Maps content policy says merchants may not offer payment, discounts, or free goods or services in exchange for a review, an edit, or removing a negative one. Incentivized reviews get removed, and a pattern can get your profile restricted.

Under Yelp’s rules, you shouldn’t ask for reviews at all, incentive or not, per BrightLocal’s platform-by-platform summary. Facebook, Tripadvisor and Angi also prohibit incentives.

For a local business, Google is the platform that pays the bills, so Google’s rule is the one to follow. No discounts, no drawings, no “review us and get a free car wash.” If you want to thank customers, thank all of them the same way, whether they review or not.

Is review gating illegal?

Review gating is when a survey asks “How did we do?” and only sends happy customers to Google while unhappy ones go to a private feedback form. Plenty of review software still offers it as a setting.

The FTC rule doesn’t name gating specifically, but the FTC says the practice could violate the FTC Act. Google is explicit: merchants may not “selectively solicit positive reviews from customers,” per its policy. So the answer for practical purposes is don’t.

The fix is easy. Send every customer the same review link. If you also want private feedback, put a second, separate line in the message (“Something not right? Reply here or call Dana at the office”). Both options go to everyone.

Can my employees, friends or family review my business?

Under the FTC rule, insiders can post only with a clear and conspicuous disclosure of the relationship. Family members can review your business if they disclose they’re family. The FTC doesn’t expect you to vet every reviewer, but red flags should prompt a question.

Google is stricter. Its policy removes content based on a conflict of interest, including current or former employment or a contractual relationship. Practically, that means no staff reviews of your own business on Google, disclosure or not.

Google tightened the staff side in April 2026. The policy now bars merchants from asking staff to collect a set number of reviews, or reviews with specific content, “including content that identifies a staff member,” per PPC Land. That hits a habit we see all over Houston: techs and stylists told to get five reviews a week that mention them by name. It also says merchants shouldn’t pressure customers to review while on the premises, so the “can you do it right now while I’m standing here” move at checkout is out too.

Asking customers is fine. Setting staff quotas or scripting what customers say is the line.

How do I handle negative reviews without breaking the rule?

Respond publicly, calmly and briefly. The FTC’s Q&A says businesses can respond to negative reviews, contact the customer to fix the problem, and ask satisfied customers to update a review. What they can’t do is use intimidation, unfounded legal threats or knowingly false accusations to get reviews removed. Paying someone to take down a truthful negative review may be unfair under the FTC Act.

Fake negative reviews are a separate problem. If someone floods your profile with one-star reviews and then demands payment to stop, Google has a dedicated report form for review extortion, per Search Engine Land. Use it, don’t pay, and keep screenshots. Google says it now detects more of these scams before they publish, per Google’s April 2026 Maps safety update.

For reviews that break Google’s policy, flag them through your Business Profile. For a deeper look at why legitimate reviews vanish and how to get them back, see why Google reviews disappear.

What Google does to profiles that cheat

The FTC is slow. Google isn’t. Per Google’s April 2026 Maps safety update, Google blocked or removed more than 292 million policy-violating reviews in 2025, took down over 13 million fake Business Profiles and restricted more than 782,000 accounts.

When Google decides a profile bought or faked reviews, its Business Profile restrictions page says the profile can be blocked from getting new reviews for a set period, have existing reviews unpublished, and show a warning telling shoppers fake reviews were removed. For a dentist in Sugar Land or an HVAC company in Cypress, that warning banner costs more than any FTC fine is likely to.

Customers notice too. BrightLocal’s 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, and 97% think businesses caught faking them should face consequences.

Does the rule apply to my marketing agency or review software?

Yes. Agencies and reputation management firms can be liable under the rule, per the FTC’s Q&A, and you’re not off the hook because a vendor did it for you. Before you sign with anyone promising reviews, ask:

  • Where do the reviews come from? (The only right answer is your real customers.)
  • Does the software gate by rating, or send everyone the same link?
  • Does it write, suggest or “polish” review text for customers?
  • Do you guarantee a number of reviews or a star rating? (If yes, walk.)
  • Will you post reviews from accounts you control?

The same logic covers creators and influencers. Paid or gifted posts are endorsements and need disclosure; we cover that in FTC rules for influencer partnerships.

Your own website counts too. The FTC’s Q&A is clear that putting testimonials in your advertising isn’t “hosting” reviews, so the testimonials page, the quotes on your landing pages and the reviews in your Facebook ads all have to be real customers describing real experiences. Copy them word for word, get permission, and don’t stitch two customers into one glowing quote. Our guide to reviews and testimonials on your site covers how to display them well, and the Google reviews guide covers the asking side in more depth.

A review program that’s legal everywhere

The program that satisfies the FTC, Google and common sense is boring, and it works because it’s steady. BrightLocal’s survey found 74% of consumers only care about reviews from the last three months, so a slow, constant flow beats a burst.

Sample text message, sent the day the job closes: “Hi Maria, thanks for choosing us for your AC repair today. If you have a minute, an honest review helps other Woodlands families find us: [link]. Anything not right? Just reply here.” Same message to every customer. No incentive, no tech name prompt.

Then reply to every review within a couple of business days. BrightLocal found 89% of consumers expect owners to respond, and half are put off by templated replies, so write them like a person.

Frequently asked questions

Is it illegal to ask customers for Google reviews in Texas?

No. Asking real customers for honest reviews is legal under the FTC rule and allowed by Google, as long as you ask everyone, don’t offer incentives, and don’t filter out unhappy customers. Texas doesn’t add a ban on asking. What gets businesses in trouble is fake reviews, paid reviews, staff reviews, review gating and threatening people over negative reviews. Yelp is the exception: it asks businesses not to request reviews at all.

Can I get fined for fake Google reviews my old marketing company posted?

Possibly. The FTC rule covers businesses that buy or procure fake reviews, and the FTC says agencies and reputation firms can be liable too, so both sides can be exposed. Penalties apply to knowing violations, including when you should have known. If you suspect a past vendor posted fake reviews, stop the practice, document what happened and talk to an attorney about cleanup before Google restricts your profile.

Can my employees leave Google reviews for my business?

Not on Google. Google removes reviews based on a conflict of interest, including current or former employment. The FTC rule allows insider reviews only with a clear disclosure, but that doesn’t override Google’s policy. Since April 2026, Google also bars asking staff to collect a set number of reviews or reviews that name them. Have employees ask customers to review instead, using the same link for everyone.

What is the penalty for fake reviews under the FTC rule in 2026?

Up to $53,088 per violation for knowing violations. The FTC did not make its usual inflation adjustment for 2026 because the government shutdown delayed the data it relies on, so 2025 amounts still apply. Each fake or bought review can be counted as a separate violation. Courts decide the actual amount, and the FTC can also seek refunds for customers and orders that change how a business operates.

Are review management tools that filter unhappy customers allowed?

Filtering, or review gating, is prohibited by Google, which bars merchants from selectively soliciting positive reviews. The FTC rule doesn’t name gating, but the FTC says it could violate the FTC Act. Many tools still offer the setting, so check yours. Send every customer the same review link, and offer private feedback as a separate option shown to everyone, not as a detour for unhappy customers.
