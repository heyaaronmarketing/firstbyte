Monday, 9 a.m., the regional franchisee call. Five owners on the line: Katy, Cypress, Pearland, Kingwood and Sugar Land. Katy is up 60 members this month and doesn’t know why. Pearland is down and wants to know why the brand fund paid for a national campaign instead of more ads in Pearland. Kingwood asks whether the leads in the portal are his or the Humble location’s. Nobody can answer cleanly, because each studio’s numbers come from a different place.

That call is fitness franchise marketing in a nutshell. The ads are rarely the hard part. Who pays for what, who controls which account, and whether every location measures the same things the same way: that’s where it gets hard.

This guide is for franchisor marketing teams and multi-unit franchisees running gyms, studios and boutique fitness concepts. We’ll cover the brand fund and local spend split, co-op advertising, location pages and Google profiles at scale, Meta ads that localize for every location, tracking, budgets by location maturity, and how to report to franchisees so the Monday call goes better.

Brand fund vs. local marketing: who pays for what

Most fitness franchise systems split marketing money into two or three buckets:

  • The brand fund (also called the national advertising fund, marketing fund or NAF). Franchisees pay a set percentage or flat fee. The franchisor spends it on things that help every location: brand creative, national media, the website, the app, the CRM, ad templates.
  • Local marketing, a required minimum each franchisee spends in their own market, on approved channels.
  • Co-ops, where franchisees in a market pool local money for media that one location couldn’t afford alone.

Planet Fitness is the clearest public example because it files a 10-K. Per Planet Fitness’s 2025 annual report (10-K), franchisees and corporate-owned clubs generally contribute about 2% of membership dues to the national advertising fund, and franchisees in the U.S. and Canada must spend 7% of monthly dues on local marketing. For 2026, the filing says franchisees voted to raise the national fund to 3% and lower local marketing to 6%. That’s a system with 2,896 clubs as of the end of 2025 deciding that a bit more central spend beats a bit more local spend.

Other fitness systems use flat fees. a 2024 International Franchise Association legal symposium paper that reviewed dozens of franchise disclosure documents cited Anytime Fitness requiring a flat $600, $800 or $1,000 a month in local spend depending on market size, and Stretch Lab requiring at least $1,500 a month. Those figures came from the FDDs the authors reviewed at the time, so check the current documents for any system you’re looking at.

Our opinion: central money should fund what has economies of scale (creative, technology, brand awareness media, the website), and local money should fund anything that depends on a specific trade area (search, local Meta ads, events, partnerships). Things go sideways when the brand fund tries to buy local results with national media, or when every franchisee builds their own creative because the brand’s templates don’t fit their market.

What the franchise disclosure document says about marketing

If you work on the franchisor side, the FTC Franchise Rule shapes what you can do with the fund, because franchisees read Item 11 of the franchise disclosure document before they sign.

Under 16 CFR 436.5(k), Item 11 has to describe the advertising program, including what media the franchisor may use, whether coverage is local, regional or national, who produces the ads, and when franchisees can use their own materials. For any advertising fund, it has to disclose who contributes and how much, whether franchisor-owned outlets contribute and on the same basis, who administers it, if and when it’s audited, whether financial statements are available to franchisees, and how the money was used in the last fiscal year, including the percentages spent on production, media placement and administration. Local or regional co-ops get their own disclosures: whether participation is required, how contributions are set, who administers the co-op and whether it produces financial statements.

Administrative costs vary widely. The same IFA paper found fund administrative fees in its sample ranging from 0.5% of annual fund spending at Taco Bell to 18% at Snap Fitness, and estimated that more than 90% of the FDDs it reviewed gave franchisees some way to see how the fund was spent.

What this means for marketers:

  • Report fund spending in the same categories Item 11 uses (production, media, administration, other). Franchisees will compare your quarterly report to the FDD.
  • Keep franchise-sales marketing out of the fund unless the FDD allows it. Item 11 also asks for the share of fund money used mainly to sell new franchises.
  • Watch franchise-development ads for earnings claims. Under Item 19 of the Franchise Rule (16 CFR 436.5(s)), financial performance information has to have a reasonable basis and be in the FDD; numbers that differ from Item 19 can only be given in narrow, documented cases. “Owners average $1.2M in revenue” in a recruitment ad is a legal question, not a copywriting one.

We’re marketers, not franchise lawyers. Have counsel review anything that touches the fund or franchise sales.

Co-op advertising for franchisees in the same market

Co-ops make sense when the right media covers more than one trade area. In Houston, that’s most of the big media. A streaming TV buy, a radio flight or a billboard on I-45 reaches people from Conroe to Clear Lake, so five franchisees each buying it alone means five overlapping, too-small buys.

Good fits for a Houston co-op:

  • Streaming TV targeted to the Houston DMA or a list of ZIP codes around all member locations. Our CTV, radio and billboard team handles these.
  • Radio and audio streaming around January and back-to-school, when gym demand moves.
  • Billboards and digital out-of-home on commuter routes, with copy that points to the nearest-location finder.
  • Sponsorships: a high school football stadium sign, a 5K in The Woodlands, a Houston Marathon expo booth.

Rules that keep a co-op from turning into a fight:

  1. Split costs by a formula everyone agreed to in writing: equal shares, share of members, or share of revenue. Member share is usually fairest.
  2. Don’t run search through the co-op. Search is location-specific and should stay in each location’s own budget.
  3. One media plan, one report. Show reach and frequency by location area, plus brand search volume and website visits per location page before and after each flight.
  4. Write down who decides, who breaks ties, and how a new location joins mid-year.

Location pages and Google Business Profile at scale

We cover the mechanics in the multi-location SEO guide. The fitness-specific parts:

  • Every location page shows that location’s real class schedule, hours, pricing (or a starting price), amenities, staff and photos. A template with only the address swapped is close to useless for search and doesn’t convert.
  • Presale locations get a page the day the lease is signed, with the founding offer and a waitlist form. That page will own the brand-plus-town search when the banner goes up. Our gym presale guide covers the rest of a launch.
  • One Google Business Profile per open location, owned by the brand’s organization account with the franchisee as a manager, so nothing is lost when a location changes hands.
  • Review requests run from the CRM per location, and the response templates sound like the local team. A reply signed by the Cypress manager beats “Thank you for your feedback! – The Brand Team.” See our Google reviews guide.
  • Hours, holiday closures and class changes update centrally, so a hurricane closure or a holiday schedule doesn’t live in 14 different hands.

Meta local ads with dynamic location info

Meta is usually the biggest local spend line for a fitness franchise, and the hardest one to run across many locations. There are three common setups.

1. One campaign, one ad set per location. Each ad set has its own pin and radius, budget and creative that names the location. Simple and transparent, and every location gets the spend it paid for. The downside is work: 30 locations means 30 ad sets to build, refresh and report on.

2. One campaign with dynamic location ads. Meta can fill in details from the nearest location automatically. Per Meta’s Marketing API guide to store traffic ads, a brand with its locations set up under a parent Facebook Page can use placeholders such as {{page.location.city}}, {{page.location.street_address}}, {{page.hours.today}} and {{page.phone_number}} in ad text, and Meta serves each ad from the store closest to the viewer, with radius limits of 0.7 to 50 miles. “Your new gym is on {{page.location.street_address}}” scales to hundreds of locations with one ad. The catch: that setup depends on a clean Facebook location structure, which many brands don’t have, and budget control by location is weaker.

3. A local-ads platform. Several vendors let franchisees pick from brand-approved templates, set their own budget and launch ads in their own name, while the franchisor keeps control of creative. Good for systems with many small owners. Check who owns the ad accounts and data before signing.

One warning on option 1. If you use Advantage campaign budget (campaign budget optimization) across location ad sets, Meta moves money to whichever locations get the cheapest results, which might not be the ones that paid in or the ones that need members. Use ad set spend limits under Advantage campaign budget, or set budgets at the ad set level, so Pearland’s money stays in Pearland.

Whichever setup you use, creative should look local even when it’s templated: real footage from that location, the coach’s name, the shopping center everyone knows. In our experience, “Now open next to the H-E-B” outperforms a polished national brand spot in local feeds. Our paid social team builds these for multi-location brands.

Consistent tracking for every location

The Monday call problem is a data problem. If each location counts leads differently, no comparison means anything.

The minimum standard we put in place:

  • A location ID (a short code like HOU-KTY-01) used everywhere: CRM, UTM tags, ad set names, call tracking, the club management software.
  • One UTM convention: utm_source=meta, utm_medium=paid_social, utm_campaign=2027-01_newyear_HOU-KTY-01. Write it down, and reject links that don’t follow it.
  • One definition per stage. A lead, a booked tour, a show, a trial, a join and a cancel each mean exactly one thing, system-wide.
  • Call tracking numbers per location and per channel, with calls recorded where legal.
  • Lead source written into the member record, so a join in the club management software can be traced back to the ad. Without this, you’ll never know your real cost per member.
  • Offline conversions (tours and joins) sent back to Google Ads and Meta, so the platforms optimize for members instead of form fills.

Our guide to tracking forms and calls in GA4 and Google Ads covers the plumbing.

Budget allocation by location maturity

A two-year-old location with 1,800 members and a new studio in presale shouldn’t get the same budget, even if the franchise agreement requires the same minimum from both. Here’s how we’d think about it. The percentages are illustrative, not a rule.

StageGoalSpend level (illustrative)Where it goes
Presale (90 days before opening)Founding members, waitlistHighest, often a separate launch budgetMeta lead and conversion campaigns, presale office, launch media
Ramp (months 1-12)Reach break-even membershipAbove the system minimumMeta, Google search, reviews, referral offers
Mature (year 2+)Replace churn, grow slowlyAt the minimum, peaks in JanuarySearch, retargeting, referrals, retention
TurnaroundStop the declineTemporarily above minimum, with a fix firstOffer, reviews and location page fixes before more media

Two notes. First, January is the biggest month for gyms almost everywhere, so most locations should spend unevenly across the year and a flat monthly minimum works against them. If the agreement allows it, measure the minimum annually. Second, “turnaround” locations usually have a problem that more ads won’t fix: bad reviews, a confusing offer, a weak front desk. Fix those first, or the extra spend just buys more people who don’t join. Our single-location gym marketing guide covers the offer and retention side.

Reporting to franchisees

Franchisees don’t need more dashboards. They need one page a month that answers four questions: what did I spend, what did I get, how does it compare, and what changes next month.

What makes reporting work:

  • Same layout for every location, with the system median next to each number so owners can see where they stand.
  • Members first, leads second. Lead counts make bad months look fine.
  • Local spend and brand fund reported separately, with the brand fund in Item 11 categories.
  • One recommendation per location, specific: “Kingwood’s lead-to-tour rate is half the system median. The leads look fine, so the issue is follow-up time. Average first call is 9 hours.”
  • A quarterly review with each multi-unit owner, walking the numbers in person or on video.

Mistakes we see in fitness franchise marketing

  • Franchisees running their own ad accounts with no shared standards. The brand can’t see results, and owners get sold by whichever agency called last.
  • The brand owning everything and sharing nothing. Franchisees pay for ads they can’t see and stop trusting the fund.
  • Campaign budget optimization across locations that quietly starves the new or struggling ones.
  • One national offer everywhere. A price-led offer that works in a value market can underprice a premium location in The Woodlands or River Oaks.
  • No lead source in the member record, so cost per join is a guess.
  • Template location pages with nothing local on them.
  • Judging locations by cost per lead instead of cost per join and net member growth.

A first 90 days for a franchisor marketing team

Days 1-30: agree on the location ID, UTM convention and stage definitions. Audit who owns every Meta ad account, Google Ads account and Google Business Profile, and move ownership to the brand’s business accounts with franchisees as users. Pick the Meta setup (ad set per location, dynamic location ads or a local-ads platform).

Days 31-60: get lead source flowing into the club management software and offline conversions flowing back to the ad platforms for at least a few pilot locations. Rebuild the location page template so it pulls each location’s schedule, pricing and photos. Set up the Houston co-op agreement if there’s more than one owner in the market.

Days 61-90: send the first one-page monthly report to every franchisee, run the first quarterly reviews with multi-unit owners, and set next year’s budget by location stage, with January planned early.

Frequently asked questions

How much do fitness franchisees pay into a marketing fund?

It varies by system and is disclosed in Item 11 of the franchise disclosure document. Planet Fitness, for example, generally collected about 2% of membership dues for its national fund and required 7% of monthly dues for local marketing, and franchisees voted to change that to 3% and 6% for 2026. Other fitness systems use flat monthly local spending minimums instead of percentages.

What is the difference between a brand fund and local marketing?

The brand fund is money franchisees pay to the franchisor for marketing that benefits every location, such as creative, national media, the website and technology. Local marketing is spending each franchisee must do in their own trade area, usually on approved channels like Meta ads, Google search and events. Co-ops sit in between, pooling local money for shared media in one market.

How should a fitness franchise run Facebook ads for many locations?

Most systems use one of three setups: one ad set per location with its own radius and budget, one campaign using Meta's dynamic location fields so each ad shows the nearest location's details, or a local-ads platform with brand-approved templates. If you use campaign budget optimization across locations, set ad set spend limits so budget stays with the locations that paid for it.

What should a franchisor report to franchisees about marketing?

Send one page per location each month showing local spend, leads, tours, joins, cost per join and net member growth, with the system median beside each number and one specific recommendation. Report brand fund spending separately, using the same production, media and administration categories as Item 11 of the FDD, so franchisees can compare it to what they were told.

Does the FTC Franchise Rule affect franchise marketing?

Yes, in two main ways. Item 11 of the disclosure document must describe the advertising fund, including contributions, administration, audits and how money was spent on production, media and administration. And franchise recruitment marketing has to be careful with earnings claims, since financial performance representations need a reasonable basis and must be included in Item 19. Have franchise counsel review fund and recruitment materials.
