Friday, 4:50 p.m., an apartment complex off Kuykendahl in Spring. The window opens at 5. The property manager promised “at least 60 residents,” and the generator is already humming. Your Instagram story with today’s spot went up at 4:45. Meanwhile, somebody in Building C googles your truck’s name, and Google says you’re closed, listed at an address in Tomball where you parked once in March.

By 6:15 you’ve served 22 people.

Most food truck marketing problems look like that evening. The food is fine. The information about where you are and when is scattered across three apps, and none of them agree.

Food truck marketing in Houston comes down to four habits: post your location the same way every day, keep your Google listing honest, book enough guaranteed private events (offices, apartments, breweries) that the slow days don’t sink the month, and pick festivals with a calculator instead of a gut feeling. Below is how we’d set each one up for a truck working Houston, The Woodlands, Cypress or Katy, starting with the rule change every Texas truck had to deal with in 2026.

The 2026 rule change: one Texas license, but Houston still decides where you park

Until mid-2026, a truck that worked Houston, Harris County and Montgomery County could be juggling a separate permit for each one. House Bill 2844 changed that. Per DSHS’s mobile food vendor page, every mobile food vendor in Texas must hold a license from the Department of State Health Services starting July 1, 2026, with a pre-licensing inspection before it’s issued. Fees run by type: $309 for Type I (prepackaged food, no pre-licensing inspection fee), $618 plus a $400 pre-licensing inspection for Type II, and $876 plus $500 for Type III, which covers trucks that cook.

The city side didn’t disappear. The Houston Health Department’s mobile food unit page says City of Houston medallions stop being valid after July 1, 2026, and that operators should get the DSHS license and then schedule the city inspection. The same page still describes daily servicing at an approved central preparation facility and a signed restroom letter from a business near where you operate. As the Texas Tribune reported, local fire codes and zoning stay local, so cities still control where and when trucks can set up.

Fire is its own permit inside city limits. The Houston Fire Marshal’s food truck basics requires a Fire Marshal’s permit for every food truck, at least one 3A:40BC extinguisher, a Type K extinguisher if you fry, and a hood and suppression system for grease-producing cooking. Trucks running propane must stay at least 10 feet from buildings and 25 feet from any other propane truck.

Planners at offices and apartment complexes are nervous about liability, and you can calm them down before they have to ask.

The Woodlands and Montgomery County: events, parks and private lots

The Woodlands is a special case, because the Township isn’t a city. Community Impact reported in 2023 that the Township “does not have the authority to enact ordinances involving the operation of food trucks” except on Township-owned property, where park events usually need a special-use permit. The same story counted more than 400 active mobile food permits in Montgomery County and described food truck parks opening off Sawdust Road and FM 1488, plus venues like Kirby Ice House that rotate trucks instead of running a kitchen.

If you’re working an event in a Township park, the Township’s special event policies say an insurance certificate is required for every food and beverage vendor, with event coverage of at least $1 million single limit and the Township named as additional insured. Organizers pay a $1,000 permit fee for commercial festivals ($500 for nonprofits) and have a 30-day pre-application deadline for events with food. Some facilities also have exclusive concession contracts, so the organizer needs the concessionaire’s consent before you sell anything.

For your marketing, that means two things. Keep a current certificate of insurance ready to email in five minutes, because it’s the first thing a Woodlands event organizer will ask for. And don’t announce a Township park appearance until the organizer confirms the permit; we’ve watched trucks promote a Saturday in Northshore Park that never got approved.

Shenandoah, Oak Ridge North and Conroe each have their own rules. HOAs in Creekside Park, Harmony and the newer Conroe master-planned communities often allow trucks only through an HOA or lifestyle director booking. Those lifestyle directors are worth knowing by name.

Posting your location: the daily habit that decides how many people show up

A restaurant has an address. You have a feed. If your followers can’t tell in three seconds where you are tonight, they’ll eat somewhere that has an address.

What we’d set up for any Houston truck:

Keep the format identical every time: the same template graphic, the same order (spot name, street, hours, one menu highlight), and the location sticker on every Instagram story. Pin a weekly schedule post to the top of your Instagram grid and Facebook page every Monday, then update the story daily. When plans change because of rain or a cancelled event, post the change before you post anything else.

Facebook matters more for food trucks than most owners think. Neighborhood groups in Cypress, Kingwood, Atascocita and The Woodlands are where residents ask “any food trucks around tonight?” Read each group’s rules before posting. Many allow vendor posts one day a week, and the trucks that follow that rule get recommended by members instead of reported.

Build a text list as well. A QR code at the window that says “text TACO to get our weekly spots” turns a one-time customer into somebody you can reach Monday morning. Texting has rules: get clear opt-in for marketing messages and keep the record. Texas also brought marketing texts under its telephone solicitation law in 2025, so read our SMS marketing rules for Texas businesses before you send the first one.

Google Business Profile for a truck that moves

Google’s guidelines don’t mention food trucks directly. Whitespark’s look at food truck eligibility (March 2025) found no line about them in the current rules. In practice, most trucks set up the profile like a service-area business: the address that matches your business documents, hidden from the public, with service areas listed instead.

Per Google’s service-area help page, you can list up to 20 service areas, and the overall area shouldn’t extend more than about two hours of driving from where you’re based. For a truck based in Spring, a realistic list is Spring, The Woodlands, Tomball, Cypress, Klein, Humble, Kingwood, Conroe and north Houston. Listing Galveston and Austin won’t help anyone find you.

What to fill in, in order of how much it helps:

  • Category. Type “food truck” into the category field and see what Google offers, then compare it with the cuisine category that fits (“Taco restaurant,” “Barbecue restaurant”). Add “Caterer” as a secondary category if you do private events.
  • Hours. Don’t set fake “open 11 to 9 every day” hours to look busy. Set the hours for your recurring spot if you have one, and use Google posts for the week’s schedule.
  • Menu and photos. Upload the menu and a photo of the truck itself, so people at a festival can match the wrap to the listing.
  • Booking link. Point the website button at a catering and events page with a quote form. That’s where the money is.

If you park at the same food truck park or brewery five days a week, you’re closer to a fixed-location business, and some trucks list that address. Ask whether you can receive mail there and whether the property owner is fine with it before you try. A suspended profile is a long fix; our reinstatement guide explains why. Corporate lunches and apartment complex events: the weekday money

Street service is fun and unpredictable. A booked office lunch with a guaranteed minimum pays the commissary bill. Most trucks we talk to want more of the second kind and don’t know how to ask for it.

Houston has plenty of buyers. Office parks along Research Forest and Lake Robbins in The Woodlands, the Energy Corridor along I-10, hospital campuses, distribution centers out by the Grand Parkway, and school districts with teacher appreciation weeks all book trucks. So do apartment complexes. Property managers run resident events monthly and have a budget line for them.

Make the yes easy:

Then send it to people who can say yes. For apartments, that’s the community manager or the regional marketing manager for a management company with ten properties in Spring and Cypress. For offices, it’s the office manager or HR coordinator. A short email with your one-pager, three dates you have open and a photo of a line at your window will outperform any ad you could buy for that audience.

Price it simply. Most trucks use one of two models: a guaranteed minimum (the host covers the gap if sales fall short) or a prepaid per-person package. Put both on your events page with “starting at” numbers. Write “[your price]” in your draft and fill it in; planners skip trucks that say “call for pricing.” Our catering marketing guide covers the corporate side in more depth if you’re growing into full catering.

Brewery partnerships: the easiest recurring spot in Houston

Plenty of Houston-area taprooms don’t run a kitchen, and their customers get hungry around the second pint. That’s a weekly spot with built-in traffic, shelter from the sun, restrooms and a host who wants you to succeed.

How to make it work for both sides:

  • Agree on a recurring day (every Thursday beats “sometime next month”) and put it on both calendars.
  • Post together. A collab post on Instagram shows up on both accounts, which doubles reach for free.
  • Ask the brewery to list you in their event listing and their Untappd or website calendar.
  • Build a pairing special around their new release. You’re selling food; they handle anything with alcohol in it.
  • Track your sales by brewery night, and drop the ones that don’t work after six weeks.

Breweries have their own advertising rules from TABC, so let them write anything about beer prices or drink specials. Our brewery and taproom marketing post explains the brewery side of that partnership.

Festival booking: run the math before you pay the fee

Festival season around Houston runs hardest in spring and fall, when the weather cooperates. The vendor application looks the same every time: an application fee, a booth fee or a percentage of sales, sometimes both, and a requirement to stay open for the whole event.

Before you apply, estimate four numbers. Attendance, how many other food vendors there will be, how many people you can actually serve per hour, and the hours you’ll be open. Throughput caps your upside no matter how big the crowd is.

The illustrative chart above compares three kinds of bookings on net take for one day after fees and food cost. Big festivals can be great, but they’re also where trucks lose the most money on a rainy day. A private booking with a minimum often nets more with no risk.

Ask organizers for last year’s attendance and vendor count, and ask a truck that worked it. Use festivals for what they’re best at: putting your truck in front of thousands of people who’ll follow you afterward. That only works if your Instagram handle and text-list QR code are bigger on the truck than your logo.

Reviews: ask at the window, not in a coupon

A truck with 300 Google reviews at 4.7 stars gets booked by office managers who’ve never tasted the food. Reviews are your sales team for private events.

The rules are the same as for any business. Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps prohibits offering incentives for reviews (no free drink for a five-star review) and prohibits selectively asking only happy customers. Ask everyone, the same way.

What works at a truck:

  1. A QR code sticker on the pickup window that goes straight to your Google review form.
  2. One line when you hand over the order: “If you liked it, a Google review helps us get booked for events.”
  3. After every private event, a text or email to the host with the review link and a thank-you.
  4. Reply to every review within a couple of days. Planners read the replies to see how you handle a cold burger.

Houston weather: heat, rain and hurricane season

Weather is the one variable every Houston truck owner complains about, and most of them don’t plan their marketing around it.

Summer heat pushes lunch traffic down and dinner traffic later. Shift your street schedule to evenings in July and August and promote shade, misting fans or indoor seating at your brewery partners. Afternoon thunderstorms in summer and fall will kill a service in 20 minutes, so keep a “rain plan” post template ready and use it the moment the radar turns green.

Write weather into your private booking terms: what happens if it rains (move under cover, reschedule or cancel with notice) and what the minimum guarantee looks like on a stormy day. Putting it on your events page saves you from awkward conversations later.

Hurricane season runs June through November. Some trucks book work feeding crews and volunteers after storms, but that depends on fuel, power and whether your commissary is open. Have a plan and a post ready, and don’t promise anything you can’t deliver when the power’s out across Harris County.

Paid ads: small budgets, tight radius, two jobs

Most trucks don’t need much ad spend. When they do, it’s for two jobs: getting new followers near a recurring spot and getting private event inquiries.

Meta ads around your regular spots. A radius of a few miles around the brewery or truck park you’re at every week, a short video of the food being made, and a message like “Every Thursday at [brewery], 5 to 9.” Budget something like $5 to $15 a day for a few weeks, and judge it on follower growth and Thursday sales. The Facebook and Instagram ads guide for local businesses covers setup.

Google Ads for event bookings. Search terms like “food truck catering Houston,” “food truck for party The Woodlands,” “taco truck catering Katy” and “food truck for corporate event.” Send them to your events page with the quote form. Add negatives on day one: for sale, used, rental, jobs, hiring, permit, license, “how to start,” commissary and wrap. A few hundred dollars a month is a reasonable test in our experience. Frequently asked questions

Do Houston food trucks need a state permit now?

Yes. Under House Bill 2844, every mobile food vendor in Texas needs a license from the Department of State Health Services starting July 1, 2026. Fees depend on the type of food, from $309 for prepackaged food up to $876 plus a $500 pre-licensing inspection for trucks that cook. Houston’s Health Department still schedules a city inspection, and the Houston Fire Marshal still requires its own permit inside city limits.

Can a food truck have a Google Business Profile?

Usually, yes. Google’s guidelines don’t address food trucks directly, so most trucks set up a service-area profile with the business address hidden and up to 20 service areas listed, within about two hours’ drive of their base. Add your menu, photos of the truck, a catering or events link, and keep hours honest. If you park at one permanent spot daily, ask about listing that address first.

How do food trucks get booked for corporate events in Houston?

Build a simple events page with packages, minimums, your license and insurance, then email office managers, HR coordinators and apartment community managers directly. Include photos of a line at your window, three open dates and starting prices. Office parks in The Woodlands, the Energy Corridor and hospital campuses all book trucks. Follow up after each event with a review link and an offer to rebook.

Are festivals worth it for a food truck?

Sometimes. Before applying, estimate attendance, the number of other food vendors, how many people you can serve per hour and the hours you’ll be open, then subtract the booth fee, any percentage of sales and food cost. Throughput limits your upside. Big festivals are best for gaining followers; private bookings with a guaranteed minimum often net more with far less weather risk.

What should a food truck post on Instagram every day?

Post the same thing in the same format every day: where you are, the street or cross streets, your hours and one menu highlight, with a location sticker on the story. Pin a weekly schedule post every Monday, and post any change before anything else. Short videos of food being made and lines at the window work better than polished graphics.
