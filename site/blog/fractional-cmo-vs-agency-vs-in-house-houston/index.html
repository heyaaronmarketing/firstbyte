It’s Sunday night, you’re approving payroll, and the Google Ads invoice is bigger than last month. Nobody can say if it worked. So you start pricing help: a marketing manager, an agency, or the “fractional CMO” a friend mentioned over lunch at Hughes Landing.

The short answer to fractional CMO vs agency vs an in-house hire is that you’re buying different things. A fractional CMO rents you senior judgment for some hours a week, while an agency is mostly hands, the people who build and run campaigns. Only a full-time hire gives you one person’s whole attention. In the Houston metro, marketing managers averaged $154,220 a year in the most recent metro-level BLS wage data for the Houston-The Woodlands-Sugar Land metro we could open, before benefits. Fractional CMOs commonly quote $3,000 to $15,000 a month. Agencies vary with the work.

Our opinion, for most owners in The Woodlands, Spring, Conroe and greater Houston doing under about $5 million a year: an agency for execution plus one person inside the company who owns the numbers beats both a pricey full-time hire and a part-time strategist with nobody to do the work. Above that, the math changes. Below is how to tell which side of the line you’re on.

Fractional CMO vs agency vs in-house hire: what you’re actually buying

The three options get compared as if they were one product at three prices. They aren’t, and most bad marketing hires come from buying the wrong thing well.

The fractional CMO

A fractional chief marketing officer is a senior marketer who works for you part time, usually a set number of hours a week, on a monthly retainer. Expect a plan, a budget split across channels, a sane reporting setup, and someone who can interview candidates and fire a vendor. Don’t expect them to build your Meta campaigns at 10 p.m. Most won’t touch the ad accounts at all; they manage the people who do.

What an agency brings

An agency gives you a team: someone who lives inside Google Ads, someone who designs, someone who writes, someone who knows why your conversion tracking double-counts calls. You get breadth that no single hire has. The trade-off is attention. Your account is one of many, and the person you met in the pitch is rarely the person pulling the levers. (That’s true of us too, to a degree. Ask who will actually run your account.)

Where an in-house hire still wins

A full-time marketing person knows your techs by name, hears the customer complaints, and can shoot a reel at the job site on a Tuesday afternoon. Nobody outside the building can match that. The weakness is range. One person is rarely strong at paid search, Meta, SEO, design, analytics and email at once, and the ones who are cost a lot more than the job posting says.

Before you compare prices, name what’s actually missing. Is it a plan, people to do the work, or somebody in the building who thinks about marketing every day? Most owners already know.

What a marketing manager costs in Houston (salary is only the start)

Owners almost always underestimate this one.

The BLS wage data for the Houston-The Woodlands-Sugar Land metro (May 2023 estimates, the latest metro-level page we could verify) shows 7,890 marketing managers working in the Houston-The Woodlands-Sugar Land area, with a mean wage of $74.15 an hour and a mean salary of $154,220. The median hourly wage was $65.85, which works out to roughly $137,000 a year. Nationally, the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook puts the median for marketing managers at $166,790 as of May 2025, so Houston has likely moved up since 2023.

“But I don’t need a manager, I need a marketing person.” Fair. The same Houston data shows market research analysts and marketing specialists (the category most “marketing coordinator” roles fall into) at a mean of $71,550 and a median of about $62,650. That person can post, schedule, update the website and coordinate vendors. They usually can’t set strategy or rescue a Performance Max campaign that’s spending on junk placements.

Then add what isn’t on the offer letter. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 found benefits made up 30.0 percent of what private employers spend on compensation: $14.07 an hour in benefits against $32.82 in wages. Senior salaried roles often run a little lower as a percentage, so we use 30 percent on top of salary as a planning number. On a $137,000 manager that’s about $178,000 a year in real cost, before a laptop, software, recruiting fees or the bonus they’ll ask for in year two.

And the Houston market makes this harder than it looks. Plenty of strong marketers here work for energy, healthcare and engineering firms that pay corporate salaries. A local HVAC company or a Kingwood dental group is competing with those offers, and with the commute. A great candidate in Katy won’t drive the Grand Parkway to The Woodlands every morning for long.

How much does a fractional CMO cost?

Fractional pricing is less standardized than salaries, and almost all of the published numbers come from firms that sell fractional executives. Grain of salt. This is what we could verify.

  • MarketerHire’s pricing guide lists $3,000 to $15,000 a month, with most fractional CMOs working 10 to 20 hours a week per client. Their tiers: strategy only at 5 to 10 hours a week runs about $3,000 to $6,000 a month; strategy plus oversight of execution at 15 to 20 hours runs about $7,000 to $12,000.
  • Go Fractional puts the typical retainer at $4,000 to $20,000 a month, with an average of $12,000 a month ($144,000 a year), and says its people average around 20 hours a week.
  • Webtonic’s roundup of fractional rate data cites a median hourly rate for fractional marketing leadership of $175, with the middle half of rates between $130 and $220, and a typical commitment of about nine hours a week.

Those numbers don’t perfectly agree (Go Fractional’s own page lists an average hourly rate of $325), which tells you how wide the market is. A former Fortune 500 CMO and a marketer who ran one regional brand will both call themselves fractional CMOs.

What the hours buy matters more than the rate. Five hours a week gets you an advisor, someone who runs a monthly planning session, glances at the dashboard and picks up when something breaks. Fifteen to twenty hours a week is a real leader who runs your weekly marketing meeting and holds vendors accountable. If you have no one to execute and no budget for an agency, paying for the advisor tier mostly buys you a very well-informed to-do list.

Houston note: local fractional CMOs exist, and so do national firms that will match you with someone remote. For a home services company in Montgomery County, we’d weight local knowledge (storm seasons, which suburbs actually buy, what a Conroe homeowner expects from a contractor) above a résumé full of SaaS launches.

What agencies cost, and where the money goes

Agency pricing has two parts that get blurred together in proposals: the management fee you pay the agency, and the media budget that goes to Google, Meta or a TV network. We broke down typical channel costs in our digital marketing cost guide, so we won’t repeat it here. For a local business, managing one or two channels often lands in the low thousands a month, and full service climbs from there.

What owners don’t see in the proposal:

  • Who actually does the work. A senior strategist sells the deal, and a coordinator two years out of school may run it. Ask for names.
  • Setup fees and minimum terms. Signing 12 months on a channel you’ve never tested means betting on the agency before you know whether the channel works.
  • Account ownership. Your Google Ads account, Meta Business Manager, Google Business Profile and analytics should be in your name, with the agency added as a user. If they own it, leaving costs you your history.
  • Media markups. Some agencies take a percentage of ad spend on top of the fee, which is fine if it’s disclosed. If the invoice from Google never reaches you, ask why.
  • Turnover on their side. Agencies lose people too, and every handoff costs you about a month of someone relearning your business.

Our list of questions to ask before hiring a marketing agency covers how to pressure-test a proposal. We’d add one more for this decision: ask the agency what they would not do for you. A good one will say, plainly, that they don’t replace someone inside the business who answers the phone well and follows up on leads.

Hidden costs nobody puts in the proposal

Every option has costs that don’t show up on the first invoice.

Start with your own time. A new hire needs onboarding, weekly one-on-ones and a boss who understands marketing well enough to judge their work. If that boss is you, budget three to five hours a week for the first few months. A fractional CMO needs less of your time; an agency needs a monthly review and fast answers when they ask for photos or approvals.

Software is the next surprise. An agency usually brings its own SEO tools, call tracking and reporting dashboards. An in-house person needs those subscriptions in your name (SEO tool, scheduler, design software, maybe a CRM upgrade), often a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars a month.

Turnover is the expensive one. Gallup estimates that replacing an employee can cost one-half to two times their annual salary. For a $137,000 marketing manager that’s a range of roughly $68,000 to $274,000, counting recruiting, ramp time and work that stalls while the seat is empty. Agencies and fractional CMOs leave too, though replacing one usually means a few weeks of transition rather than a six-month search.

Whoever you pick needs 60 to 90 days to learn your customers, your seasons (hello, hurricane season and August heat) and your numbers. Expect a slow first quarter whichever way you go.

And ask about conflicts. Some fractional CMOs are owned by, or refer work to, an agency. That can be fine, but you want to hear it from them up front. A fractional lead who recommends their own shop for every channel isn’t really independent oversight.

Combinations that work (and one that usually doesn’t)

The best setups we see around The Woodlands and Houston are hybrids.

Fractional lead plus agency execution

The fractional CMO sets the plan and budget, sits in the monthly agency review, and asks the uncomfortable questions (“why did cost per lead go up 40 percent in September?”). The agency runs the campaigns. This works well for companies in the $5 million to $20 million range with several service lines, say a home services company doing HVAC, plumbing and electrical across Montgomery and north Harris counties.

In-house coordinator plus agency

The coordinator handles anything that needs to be local and fast, from job-site photos and review requests to the Waterway Square event booth. The agency handles paid media, SEO and web work. It’s the setup we recommend most often for growing local businesses, at far less than a manager costs.

In-house manager plus specialist agencies

At larger companies, a full-time marketing leader manages two or three specialist firms (paid media, SEO, creative) instead of one full-service agency. More coordination, sharper specialists.

The one that usually doesn’t work

Hiring one junior “marketing person” and expecting them to own strategy, ads, SEO, the website, social and analytics, with no senior help. They’re set up to fail, they usually leave within a year or two, and the owner concludes “marketing doesn’t work for us.” If you can only afford one junior hire, pair them with an agency or a few hours of a fractional lead.

Whatever the mix, someone needs patience with the slow channels. If SEO is part of the plan, our month-by-month SEO timeline shows what progress should look like before you judge anyone on it.

How to make the call this month

You don’t need a consultant to make this decision. You need about two hours and your last 12 months of numbers.

  1. Write down what marketing cost last year, all in. Media, agency fees, software, even the hours your office manager spent posting on Facebook.
  2. Count your leads and booked jobs by source, even roughly. If you can’t, tracking is the first problem to solve, before hiring anyone.
  3. Name the gap from the first section and match it. A missing plan points to a fractional lead and missing hands to an agency; if nobody cares about marketing day to day, that’s a coordinator.
  4. Price the loaded cost from the table above, not the salary or the retainer alone.
  5. Test before you commit. A 90-day agency engagement or a three-month fractional trial beats a 12-month contract or a hire you can’t easily unwind.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a fractional CMO cost in Houston?

Published ranges run about $3,000 to $15,000 a month, with averages near $12,000 for roughly 20 hours a week. Advisory roles at five to ten hours a week sit around $3,000 to $6,000. Houston rates follow national ranges because many fractional CMOs work remotely, so the bigger driver is hours per week and the person’s experience, not the zip code.

Is a fractional CMO worth it for a small business?

Usually only once you have several channels, a real budget and someone to do the work. Below about $5 million in revenue, most local businesses get more from an agency plus an internal owner of the numbers. A fractional CMO pays off when you have vendors or staff who need direction and nobody senior setting goals or judging results.

Should I hire a marketing manager or an agency?

Compare loaded cost to what you spend on media. A Houston marketing manager costs around $178,000 a year with benefits, more than many local businesses spend on ads. If your media budget is modest, an agency for execution plus a coordinator or office manager who follows up on leads is typically the better use of money.

What does a fractional CMO actually do?

They build the marketing plan, split the budget across channels, set up reporting, manage agencies and freelancers, and help hire. Most do not run ad campaigns or design content themselves. Think of them as a part-time head of marketing who makes decisions and holds people accountable, working a set number of hours each week.

Can a fractional CMO work with my existing agency?

Yes, and it's one of the pairings we see work best. The fractional CMO sets goals and reviews the agency’s work each month, while the agency handles execution. Ask whether the fractional CMO has any financial ties to agencies they recommend, so their oversight stays independent.
