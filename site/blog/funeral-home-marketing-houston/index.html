Funeral Home Marketing in Houston: Pre-Need, Prices and Earning Trust Online

Funeral home marketing for Houston: pre-need campaigns, FTC and Texas price rules, reviews handled with care, obituary SEO and Spanish-speaking families.

Tuesday, 2:15 a.m. The on-call director at a family-owned funeral home in Kingwood takes a call from a hospice nurse. The family chose this home because their grandmother’s service was here in 2011, and they remember how they were treated.

At 3 a.m., one of the grandchildren is on her phone in the hospice parking lot, searching “cremation cost Houston” and reading reviews of three other places, because nobody in the family is sure what the grandmother wanted.

Both of those moments are funeral home marketing. The first is reputation built over decades. The second is a family comparing you online, at the worst hour of their year, on a phone.

For a Houston funeral home, the marketing that pays is mostly about the time before anyone is in crisis: pre-need planning that brings families to you while there’s room to think, a website that publishes prices and answers questions plainly, and Google reviews earned and answered with care. Obituary pages are the most-visited part of most funeral home sites, so they deserve real SEO attention. And Spanish-speaking families, a big part of Houston, should be served in their language. Below is how we’d approach it, with the federal and Texas rules that apply.

At-need vs. pre-need: where marketing actually moves the numbers

At-need calls are decided by reputation, proximity, faith community and family history. Advertising can keep you visible, but a crass at-need ad does more harm than good, and most families in that moment call whoever they already trust.

Pre-need is different. Someone planning ahead has time to compare, attend a seminar, read your website and meet a counselor. That makes it marketable in the normal sense, and a pre-need contract today is usually your at-need family later.

The market keeps shifting under both. NFDA projected the U.S. cremation rate at 63.4% for 2025 and the burial rate at 31.6%, per NFDA’s 2025 Cremation & Burial Report, and expects cremation to reach 82.3% by 2045, per NFDA’s statistics page. NFDA’s 2023 price study put the national median for a funeral with viewing and burial at $8,300, and $6,280 for a funeral with viewing and cremation, per NFDA’s 2023 price list study.

That shift is a marketing problem. Families who choose cremation often assume it should be cheap and simple, then search for the lowest price online. Homes that explain the full range, from direct cremation to cremation with a memorial service and a gathering, keep those families. Homes that hide the options lose them to the cheapest listing on the map.

Prices online: the FTC Funeral Rule and Texas requirements

The FTC Funeral Rule (16 CFR part 453) requires funeral providers to give consumers itemized price information, show price lists at specific points, provide prices by phone and give a statement of the goods and services selected, as summarized in the FTC’s May 2026 Federal Register notice. Texas has its own versions. Texas Occupations Code §651.405 defines the retail price list as a printed or typewritten list and spells out what it must include, and 22 TAC §203.46 requires accurate price answers by phone and printed lists for people who ask in person.

Neither the federal rule nor those Texas provisions requires posting prices on your website today. That may change. In October 2022 the FTC voted to issue an advance notice of proposed rulemaking that asked, among other things, whether funeral homes should have to post prices online, per NFDA’s report on the FTC vote. NFDA opposed a mandate. As of a September 2026 industry summary, online price posting still hadn’t been finalized into an enforceable requirement, and we haven’t seen a final rule since.

Our view: post your General Price List online anyway, along with plain-English package summaries. Families are already comparing on their phones. A home that shows prices looks confident and honest, and a home that hides them looks like it has something to hide, even when it doesn’t.

A good pricing page has the downloadable GPL with its effective date, three or four common arrangements with a total (“direct cremation,” “cremation with memorial service,” “traditional funeral with burial”) and a sentence on what drives the difference. Add a phone number answered 24 hours a day. If you do post prices, keep them current; an outdated price list online is worse than none.

Texas regulators and pre-need rules that shape your ads

Funeral homes and directors in Texas are licensed by the Texas Funeral Service Commission, which also requires establishments to give prospective customers its “Facts About Funerals” brochure when funeral services are discussed. The Commission warns consumers that some providers advertise in Texas without a Texas license, and it tells consumers to check the license database before paying. Put your license number and a link to that lookup on your website; it costs nothing and answers a question careful families are asking.

Pre-need is regulated separately. Per the Texas Department of Banking’s prepaid funeral site, any funeral home or cemetery selling prepaid funeral goods or services in Texas must hold its own trust-funded permit from the Department of Banking or sell through a third party with a trust-funded or insurance-funded permit. The Commission’s consumer page notes that the Department of Banking oversees trust-funded contracts and the Texas Department of Insurance oversees insurance-funded ones.

For marketing, that means a few things:

  • Say plainly how your pre-need plans are funded and who holds the permit. Families ask.
  • Have your permit holder or insurer review pre-need ad copy, mailers and seminar handouts before they go out. They often have their own rules.
  • Don’t promise “locked-in prices” or “guaranteed” anything unless your contract terms actually say so, in those words.

Marketing pre-need without being morbid

Pre-need marketing works when it treats planning as a kindness to the family, not a sales pitch.

What we see work for Houston-area homes:

  • Planning seminars. A free 45-minute session at a church hall, library branch or senior living community in The Woodlands, Sugar Land, Cypress or Pearland, with a practical title like “What your family needs to know: wills, VA benefits and funeral planning.” Invite an estate attorney or a VA benefits counselor to co-present.
  • A planning guide download. A printable workbook that covers wishes, contacts, documents, music and readings. Offer it on your website and in Facebook ads. People who download it are your best pre-need leads.
  • Adult children. A lot of pre-need decisions start with a 50-something daughter worried about her parents. Ads and content can speak to her (“Have you talked with your parents about their wishes?”).
  • Veterans. Explain burial benefits and the Houston National Cemetery process clearly, and you’ll become the home veterans’ families recommend.
  • Direct mail still works with older households. Keep it plain, warm and specific, with an invitation to a seminar rather than a hard offer.

Facebook is where to spend most of the paid budget for pre-need. Pew Research Center’s 2025 survey found 71% of U.S. adults use Facebook, and a majority of every age group uses it. Video works: a two-minute talk from your owner or pre-need counselor about why planning helps families will outperform any stock image. Use instant forms or a landing page to book seminar seats and planning appointments. Our Facebook and Instagram ads guide covers setup.

Avoid fear-based copy. “Don’t leave your family with a $9,000 bill” might get clicks, but it doesn’t fit a business that runs on trust, and it ages badly in a community where people talk.

Google Business Profile and reviews, handled with care

Families searching at 3 a.m. look at the map first. Your Business Profile needs the right primary category (Funeral home), secondary categories for services you really offer such as cremation, real photos of the chapel, lobby and parking, accurate 24-hour phone information and a clear description of services. Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the rest. Google’s Local Services Ads also include a “Funeral home” category, per Google’s Local Services Ads category list, which some homes use for pre-need and cremation inquiries.

Reviews need a gentler process than most businesses use. Timing matters. Asking at the arrangement conference or right after the service is too soon. Two to four weeks later, with a handwritten card from the director and a printed QR code or short link, is about right. Many families are glad to write something once they’ve had time.

The rules are the same as everywhere: no incentives, no discouraging negative reviews and no asking only the families you think are happy, per Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps. Ask everyone the same way.

Respond to every review, and keep the reply brief and personal. For a hard review, don’t argue about details of a family’s service in public:

“We’re very sorry your family’s experience fell short of what you expected during such a difficult time. I’d like to talk with you personally. Please call me directly at [number]. [Name], Funeral Director.”

Obituary pages: your biggest SEO asset

Obituaries bring more visitors to a funeral home website than any other page, in every site we’ve looked at. People search the name of the person who died, find the obituary, read service details, sign the guestbook and send flowers. Each of those visitors is a local person who may one day need you.

A few things matter:

  • Host obituaries on your own domain. If your obituaries live on a third-party platform’s subdomain, you may be giving away your best traffic. Ask your website provider exactly where they live and who gets the search credit.
  • Write a clear page title with the full name, city and dates (“Maria Elena Garza, 1941–2026, Pasadena, TX”). That matches what people type.
  • Service details at the top: date, time, address with a map link, livestream link, and whether flowers or donations are preferred.
  • Speed and mobile. Most obituary visits are on phones. Compress photos and keep the page fast.
  • Gentle next steps. A small, quiet “Planning ahead?” link at the bottom of each obituary is enough. Don’t put pop-ups on an obituary.

Watch for scams. As Malwarebytes described in 2024, scammers comment on funeral home Facebook posts with fake livestream links that lead to phishing pages, often using photos copied from obituaries. Post the official livestream link on the obituary page itself, say in the Facebook post that it’s the only official link, moderate comments, and tell families what to expect. Some homes now post that a service will not be streamed at all, which shuts the scam down.

Serving Spanish-speaking families

Many Houston families speak Spanish at home, and funeral traditions in many Mexican, Central American and Catholic families come with specific expectations: a rosary or vigil, a funeral Mass, sometimes a novena, and for some families transport of the body to another country for burial.

Marketing to those families well isn’t about running a translated ad. It means:

  • Bilingual staff who can handle an arrangement conference start to finish, named on the website.
  • Spanish pages written by a native speaker, not machine-translated: services, pricing, pre-need, and repatriation if you offer it.
  • Spanish-language Google Business Profile posts, Facebook content and review replies when the review is in Spanish.
  • Relationships with Catholic parishes and Spanish-language congregations, and with the funeral homes or consulates you work with abroad.

Community trust and measuring pre-need appointments

Funeral homes win by being known. The homes we see thriving across Houston, Humble, Tomball and Conroe still show up: hospice and nursing home relationships with real education for staff, church and veterans group events, grief support groups open to anyone, holiday remembrance services in December. None of that is an ad, and all of it is marketing.

For the parts you pay for, track them properly. Use separate call tracking numbers for Google, Facebook, direct mail and seminars (our call tracking guide covers setup), record every pre-need lead in one CRM with its source, and count:

  • Planning guide downloads and seminar registrations
  • Pre-need appointments booked and held
  • Pre-need contracts written, by source
  • At-need calls from families who say they found you online

Pre-need contracts are your clearest return on marketing. If a Facebook seminar campaign fills two sessions a month and each session produces three appointments, you can see what each contract cost and decide whether to scale it.

A first 90 days we’d recommend: post your GPL and package pages, fix your Business Profile and start the review card process in month one. Launch a planning guide and one seminar campaign in month two. In month three, rebuild obituary page titles and service details, and add Spanish pages if you serve those families.

Frequently asked questions

How do funeral homes get more families?

Most at-need families choose a funeral home based on reputation, location, faith community and past experience, so the long game is trust. The marketing that moves numbers is pre-need: planning seminars, a downloadable planning guide, Facebook video ads aimed at older adults and their grown children, and a website that answers questions and shows prices. Strong Google reviews and well-built obituary pages keep you visible when families search.

Do funeral homes have to post prices online?

Not currently under federal rules or the Texas price list provisions. The FTC Funeral Rule requires itemized price lists in person and price information by phone, and Texas law requires printed price lists. The FTC opened a review in 2022 that asked whether online posting should be required, but it had not been finalized as of late 2026. Many homes post their General Price List online anyway because families compare on their phones.

Who regulates pre-need funeral contracts in Texas?

The Texas Department of Banking oversees trust-funded prepaid funeral contracts, and the Texas Department of Insurance oversees insurance-funded ones. A funeral home or cemetery selling prepaid contracts must hold its own trust-funded permit or sell through a third party with a trust-funded or insurance-funded permit. Funeral homes and directors themselves are licensed by the Texas Funeral Service Commission.

How should a funeral home ask for Google reviews?

Gently and consistently. Wait two to four weeks after the service, then send a handwritten card from the director with a short review link or QR code. Ask every family the same way, because Google prohibits incentives, discouraging negative reviews and asking only happy customers. Reply to every review briefly and personally, and invite anyone with a complaint to call the director directly rather than discussing details publicly.

Are obituary pages good for funeral home SEO?

Yes. Obituaries are usually the most visited pages on a funeral home website, because people search for the name of someone who died. Host them on your own domain, use the full name, city and years in the page title, put service details and the official livestream link at the top, and keep pages fast on phones. Moderate social comments to block fake livestream scam links.
