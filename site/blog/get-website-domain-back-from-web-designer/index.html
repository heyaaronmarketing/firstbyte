How to Get Your Website and Domain Back From a Web Designer

How to get your website and domain back from a web designer: check who the registrant is, transfer the domain under ICANN rules, and recover your accounts.

A plumbing company owner in Spring calls us on a Tuesday. His website has been down since Saturday. The designer who built it in 2017 stopped answering texts last year, and now there’s a “hosting account suspended” page where his homepage used to be. His email is down too, because it ran through the same domain.

He pays the invoices. He assumed he owned all of it. Then we looked up the domain and found it registered to the designer’s own business.

To get your website and domain back from a web designer, first look up who is listed as the domain’s registrant, because that person controls it, whoever paid. If it’s you, get into the registrar account (or have the registrar help you) and transfer the domain with its authorization code. If it’s the designer, ask in writing for a change of registrant or a transfer, and use the registrar and ICANN’s complaint process if they won’t cooperate. Then collect hosting, CMS and Google account access one at a time, and decide whether the site is worth recovering or rebuilding.

We’re marketers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. Where money or a contract dispute is involved, talk to a Texas business attorney.

Work top to bottom. The domain matters most, because whoever controls it controls where your website and your email point.

Registrant vs. account holder: who actually owns your domain

This is the distinction that catches most owners off guard. Two different people can be in charge of a domain.

  • The registrant (ICANN calls this the registered name holder) is the person or company listed in the domain’s registration record. The registrant has the rights in the domain, including the right to transfer it.
  • The account holder is whoever logs in to the registrar account where the domain sits. That person can change settings and, in practice, can often change the registrant record too.

In the best case, you’re both. In a common case, you’re the registrant but the domain sits in the designer’s registrar account alongside forty other client domains. In the worst case, the designer is listed as the registrant and holds the account. Paying the renewal invoices doesn’t change who’s listed.

ICANN’s guidance to domain holders is to keep registration contact details accurate and current, because those details are what protect you against unauthorized transfers. A record full of the designer’s email addresses works against you.

Look up your domain with ICANN Lookup

The old WHOIS system was retired for generic top-level domains like .com, .net and .org. As of January 28, 2025, the Registration Data Access Protocol (RDAP) is the definitive source for that registration data, per ICANN’s RDAP announcement. The easy way to use it is ICANN Lookup, ICANN’s own free lookup tool.

Type your domain in and read four things:

  1. Registrar. The company the domain is registered through. This is who you’ll be dealing with.
  2. Dates: creation, last update and expiration. If it expires next month, you have a deadline.
  3. Status codes. “clientTransferProhibited” means the registrar lock is on. That’s normal, and it’s turned off before a transfer. “redemptionPeriod” or “pendingDelete” means the domain has already expired and you need to act today.
  4. Nameservers. Where DNS is managed. If they point to the designer’s host, your website and email depend on that host.

The registrant’s name will often be redacted for privacy. You may see only an organization name and state, or nothing. If it’s hidden, the registrar can confirm if you’re the registrant once you prove who you are, and your old renewal receipts or setup emails from the designer often show the account it was registered under.

Country-code domains like .us or .co.uk follow their own registry rules, so check that registry’s process if yours isn’t a .com, .net or similar.

If you’re the registrant but don’t have the login

This is the easier situation. You have the rights. You just need the keys.

Try account recovery at the registrar first, using any email address you might have been set up with. If that fails, call the registrar’s support line, say you are the registrant and need access, and ask what identity verification they require. It usually means a government ID and something tying you to the business, like formation documents or a recent invoice under your company name. Some registrars will move the domain into a new account in your name once they verify you.

If the domain sits inside the designer’s account, you can also ask the designer to “push” it to an account you open at the same registrar. That keeps the domain at the same registrar and is usually instant. Under the current ICANN rules, a change to the registrant name, organization or email triggers a 60-day lock on transfers to another registrar, though some registrars let you opt out ahead of time, per ICANN’s registrant transfer FAQ. Ask before the change is made.

If the designer is listed as the registrant

Now you need their cooperation, or a dispute process. Start with the cooperative route, because it’s faster and cheaper than anything else on this page.

Ask in writing. Email is fine. Keep it short and factual, without threats:

“Hi [name], we’re making some changes to how we manage our website and need to take over the domain [yourdomain.com]. Please either change the registrant to [your legal business name, your email], or remove the transfer lock and send the authorization code so we can transfer it to our own registrar account. We’d also like the hosting login and an administrator account on the site. Can you get this done by [date a week out]? Happy to pay any outstanding balance once we confirm the amount. Thanks.”

Most designers do it. If they claim an unpaid balance, ask for an itemized invoice. If it’s legitimate, paying it is usually cheaper than fighting.

If they refuse or vanish, gather your evidence before going further: invoices you paid for the domain or the site, the contract or proposal, emails where they described the domain as yours, and your business formation documents. Contact the registrar’s support or legal team with that evidence and ask what their process is for a disputed registrant.

If the registrar won’t help and the designer won’t either, you’re in contract-dispute territory. That’s the point to call an attorney. Texas justice courts hear small claims up to $20,000, per the Texas State Law Library, which covers most web design disputes, though a court order about a domain is a different matter than a money judgment, and an attorney can tell you which you need.

How a domain transfer works under ICANN’s current rules

Once you’re the registrant, moving the domain to a registrar account you control follows ICANN’s Transfer Policy. These are the rules in ICANN’s registrant transfer FAQ:

  • Your registrar must give you the authorization code (also called an auth code, EPP code or transfer code) within five calendar days of your request.
  • A registrar may deny a transfer for evidence of fraud, a reasonable dispute over who authorized it, unpaid fees for a previous registration period, a written objection from the registrant, a lock you haven’t removed, or within 60 days of the initial registration or a previous transfer.
  • A registrar must deny a transfer during a UDRP, URS or transfer dispute proceeding, under a court order, or during a 60-day change-of-registrant lock.
  • A registrar can’t refuse because you haven’t paid its transfer fee, and it can’t refuse because the domain expired or wasn’t renewed, unless a previous period is unpaid. A domain in its Redemption Grace Period has to be restored first, which can carry a redemption fee.

In our experience, a transfer between two cooperative registrars wraps up in a few days to a week. Before you start, copy down every DNS record (especially MX records for email, and any TXT records used for verification) so you can recreate them if the DNS host changes. Losing email for three days because nobody saved the MX records is the most common self-inflicted wound we see in these moves.

ICANN’s new transfer rules: adopted, not yet in effect

You’ll see blog posts saying the 60-day lock is gone. Not yet. In the ICANN Board’s June 7, 2026 resolution, ICANN’s Board adopted the Transfer Policy Review recommendations and directed ICANN staff to implement them, subject to prioritization, with an Implementation Review Team drafting the final policy language. The resolution gives no effective date, and as of this writing (November 2026) the current policy above still applies.

The recommendations, per the GNSO Council’s report to the Board, would replace the 60-day locks after registration and after a transfer with 720-hour (30-day) locks, state the five days to issue an auth code as 120 hours, make an auth code valid for 336 hours (14 days) once set, and drop the 60-day transfer lock after a change of registrant. Check your registrar’s help pages for the date they adopt the new rules before you plan around them.

Filing a complaint with ICANN

ICANN regulates registrars, not web designers. Its compliance team can act when a registrar breaks the Transfer Policy: refusing to give you an auth code within five days, denying a transfer for a reason the policy doesn’t allow, or ignoring your request entirely. For that, ICANN’s FAQ points you to ICANN’s transfer complaint form.

Before you file, have the domain name, both registrars’ names, the dates you asked for the code and what the registrar told you, ideally in writing.

What ICANN won’t do is decide whether you or your designer should be the registrant. That’s a dispute between the two of you, governed by your agreement, and it goes to the registrar’s own process, negotiation or a court.

Hosting, the CMS and the website files

Getting the domain back lets you point it anywhere. Getting the site itself back is a separate question.

  • WordPress on hosting you don’t control. Ask for an administrator account, then make a full backup with a plugin like UpdraftPlus or your new host’s migration tool before anything else changes. Ask for the hosting login or a copy of the files and database if the designer will provide one.
  • Wix, Squarespace or another hosted builder. The site lives in someone’s account on that platform. Most let the account owner transfer a site to another account. Generally you can’t pick the design up and move it to a different platform, so “getting it back” means getting the account, or rebuilding.
  • Premium themes and plugins. If the designer used their own agency licenses, those licenses may not transfer to you. Budget for your own.
  • Email. If your email runs through the designer’s hosting or their Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 account, plan that move carefully. Export mailboxes before cutting anything off.

If the hosting lapsed and the files are gone, the Wayback Machine may still have copies of your pages, which can at least save your copy and images for a rebuild.

Who owns the design under copyright

Owners often assume that paying for a website means owning it. Copyright law doesn’t work that way by default.

Under Chapter 2 of the Copyright Act, copyright belongs at first to the author of the work. The exception is a “work made for hire.” Per U.S. Copyright Office Circular 30, that covers work an employee creates within the scope of their job, or work that’s specially commissioned in one of nine listed categories (such as a contribution to a collective work, a compilation or part of an audiovisual work) when both sides sign a written agreement saying so. A freelance web designer isn’t your employee, and plenty of website contracts have no such clause.

Copyright can still be transferred, but section 204(a) of the Act says a transfer of ownership is valid only in writing, signed by the owner of the rights. So if your contract assigns the site, the code and the graphics to you, you’re in good shape. If you never signed anything, or the contract is silent, ownership of the design and code may be less clear than you assumed. Stock photos, fonts and themes carry their own license terms on top of that.

Two practical points. Your own content (your text, your photos, your logo if you made it or paid for it with an assignment) is a separate question from the designer’s code and layout. And for most small businesses, the domain, the content and the Google accounts are worth far more than a five-year-old theme. An attorney can read your contract and tell you where you stand.

Google Business Profile, Analytics and Search Console

These are often the most valuable accounts to win back, and Google has a process for each.

Google Business Profile. If someone else manages your profile, go to business.google.com/add, search for your business and choose Request Access when you see that someone else may manage it. Per Google’s request-ownership help page, the current owner gets an email and has 3 days to respond. If they don’t, you may be able to claim the profile from the link in your confirmation email. If they deny it, you can appeal. Once you’re in, make yourself the primary owner and remove anyone who shouldn’t be there. If the profile has gone sideways along the way, our suspension and reinstatement guide covers that.

Search Console. You don’t need the designer’s permission. Verify ownership yourself, for example with a DNS record once you control the domain. Then, per Search Console’s owners and permissions page, remove the old owner from Settings, then Users and permissions, and also delete their verification token (their HTML file, meta tag or DNS record). Removing the user alone leaves the token, and the token lets them verify again.

GA4. Ask the designer to give you the Administrator role on the property under Admin, then Property access management. If the property sits inside the designer’s own Analytics account, it can be moved to an account you own; per Google’s move-a-property help page, whoever moves it needs the Administrator and Editor roles on both the source and destination accounts. If they won’t cooperate, you can create a new property, but the history stays behind.

Google Ads has its own rules about who owns the account and the billing. We cover those in who owns your Google Ads account.

What to put in writing next time

Every one of these fights is easier to prevent than to win. Whoever you hire next, including us, get these in the agreement:

Make the agreement say plainly that the domain, the hosting account and every Google and Meta account are in your company’s name, and that the agency or designer gets user access, not ownership. When to rebuild instead of recovering

Sometimes the old site isn’t worth the fight. Our rule of thumb: always recover the domain, the Google Business Profile and your content. The design and code are negotiable.

  • Recover if the site still ranks well, converts well, runs on a mainstream platform and you can get an administrator login and a backup without a long dispute.
  • Rebuild if the site is on a builder tied to the designer’s account, runs an outdated theme nobody can update, loads slowly on phones, or the designer wants more than a new site would cost to hand it over.

If you rebuild, the ranking risk is real, because the new site will almost certainly have different URLs. Frequently asked questions

Who owns my domain if my web designer registered it?

Whoever is listed as the registrant in the domain’s registration record controls it, regardless of who paid. If your designer registered it under their own name or business, they hold the rights until they change the registrant or transfer it to you. Look the domain up at ICANN Lookup, then contact the registrar. If the designer won’t cooperate, gather your invoices and contract and talk to an attorney.

How do I get my domain transfer authorization code?

Sign in to your registrar account and look for an option to remove the transfer lock and get the transfer or EPP code. If you can’t find one, contact the registrar. Under ICANN’s registrant FAQ, your registrar must provide the code within five calendar days of your request. If it refuses or ignores you without a valid reason, you can file a transfer complaint with ICANN Compliance.

Can I transfer my domain within 60 days?

Under ICANN’s current Transfer Policy, a registrar may deny a transfer within 60 days of the initial registration or a previous transfer, and a change to the registrant’s name, organization or email triggers a 60-day transfer lock unless your registrar offered an opt-out. ICANN’s Board adopted changes in June 2026 that would shorten these locks, but they hadn’t taken effect as of November 2026.

Does my web designer own the copyright to my website?

Possibly. Under U.S. copyright law, the creator owns the work by default unless it qualifies as a work made for hire or the rights were transferred in a signed writing. An independent designer’s work usually isn’t work for hire without a written agreement in a qualifying category. Your own text, photos and logo are separate questions. Have an attorney review your contract; this isn’t legal advice.

How do I get my Google Business Profile back from a former agency?

Ask the agency to make you primary owner first. If they won’t, go to business.google.com/add, find your business and select Request Access. Google emails the current owner, who has 3 days to respond. If they don’t respond, you may be able to claim the profile from your confirmation email. If they deny the request, you can appeal through Google Business Profile support.
