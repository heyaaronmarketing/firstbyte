The question came from the volunteer coordinator at a small Spring nonprofit: “Google turned off our free ads. Can you turn them back on?”

The account had run for three years. The board member who set it up had moved to Austin, nobody had logged in since, the click-through rate had been under 5% since spring, and the only conversion it counted was a homepage view. None of that is unusual. We see some version of it every few months.

The short answer: Google Ad Grants gives eligible 501(c)(3) nonprofits up to $10,000 a month in Google Search text ads. To get it, register with Google for Nonprofits, pass validation through Goodstack, bring your website up to Google’s policy and request activation. To keep it, hold a 5% account click-through rate, record at least one real conversion every month and follow the keyword rules.

Below is the whole process in order, with the menu paths and the rules as Google currently writes them. It’s written for Houston, The Woodlands and Montgomery County nonprofits, but the steps are the same anywhere in the U.S.

Is Google Ad Grants worth it for a small Houston nonprofit?

Usually, yes. With a caveat about time.

The grant is a cap, not a deposit. Google’s Google’s budgets and bidding page says it plainly: the $10,000 is “a limit, not a guarantee of spend,” with a daily ceiling of $329. Most small accounts we look at spend a fraction of it, because there are only so many people in north Houston searching “volunteer at an animal shelter near me” on a given Tuesday. That’s fine. A food pantry in Conroe that spends $1,800 of grant money a month and gets 40 volunteer sign-ups is doing well.

The cost is attention. Somebody has to log in, read the search terms, pause bad keywords and check that conversions are firing. Figure two to four hours a month once it’s set up, more in the first 60 days. If nobody on staff or on the board can own that, the account will drift toward suspension the way the Spring one did.

And it’s Search only. No display banners, no YouTube. You’re reaching people who type a need into Google, which for most nonprofits is exactly who you want.

Step 1: Confirm your nonprofit is eligible

Per Google for Nonprofits eligibility guidelines, U.S. organizations must be registered with the IRS as exempt under Section 501(c)(3). Group exemptions count if you can prove affiliation with a central 501(c)(3). Fiscally sponsored projects without their own 501(c)(3) status are not currently eligible, which catches a lot of young Houston community groups that operate under a sponsor’s umbrella.

Three kinds of organizations are excluded outright:

  • Government entities and organizations (a city parks department, a county library).
  • Hospitals and health care organizations. Their charitable arms or foundations may be eligible.
  • Schools, academic institutions and universities. Their philanthropic arms may be eligible, so a school district’s education foundation can apply even though the district can’t.

That foundation exception matters in the Houston area, where most big hospital systems and many ISDs have a separate foundation with its own EIN. If you work for one, apply as the foundation, with the foundation’s website.

Step 2: Register with Google for Nonprofits and get through Goodstack

Go to Google for Nonprofits, sign in with a Google account tied to your organization’s domain email (a Gmail address works but invites more questions), and submit your organization’s legal name, EIN and address.

Google’s validation partner is Goodstack. If you remember TechSoup handling this, that’s out of date: per TechSoup’s own notice, TechSoup stopped performing new Google for Nonprofits validations in August 2023.

According to Google’s Goodstack verification page, Google sends your legal name, registration number and address to Goodstack, which checks them against official registries in the background. Most applications are reviewed within 3 to 5 business days. If Goodstack can’t match you through public databases, it emails asking for documents such as bylaws, a government registration certificate or your IRS determination letter. If you applied from a personal email address, expect a request for a signed letter from a board member or executive confirming your role.

Two things slow Houston applicants down, in our experience. The legal name doesn’t match the IRS record (“Hope Center of Montgomery County” on the form, “Hope Ctr Inc” at the IRS), and the confirmation email lands in spam. Watch for mail from verifications@mail.goodstack.org.

Step 3: Fix your website before you apply

This is where most rejections come from, and it’s the step people skip because the site “already exists.”

The Ad Grants website policy requires, among other things, that your nonprofit owns the domain your ads point to, that the entire site is served over HTTPS, and that the site has “a significant amount of original content.” It also says not to display AdSense ads, not to host excessive third-party ads, and that donation links must work and go to a secure page dedicated to donations.

A Facebook page doesn’t count as your website, and neither does a donation portal page. Neither does a one-page site with a logo, a PayPal button and three paragraphs. If your site is thin, add real pages before you apply: one per program, a volunteer page with actual shifts and requirements, an events page, an about page with your service area. Those pages become your ad landing pages later anyway.

Speed counts too. The policy asks that pages load quickly across devices, and plenty of nonprofit sites run on a page builder with 40 plugins and a 9 MB hero photo of the gala. Our post on how fast a website should load covers the fixes, and our web team builds sites that pass this review.

Step 4: Request Ad Grants activation, and don’t open your own Ads account

Once Google for Nonprofits approves you, follow Google’s Ad Grants activation guide:

  1. Confirm the site is on HTTPS and meets the website policy.
  2. Sign in to Google for Nonprofits, find Google Ad Grants and click Get started. Enter the website you’ll advertise.
  3. Watch the welcome video (Google says about 5 minutes) and tick the box confirming you did.
  4. Click Submit activation request.

Google’s guide is blunt on one point: “Do not create a Google Ads account at any time during the Google Ad Grants activation process.” An Ad Grants account is provisioned for you. People who make their own account first end up with a regular paid account that wants a credit card, then a support ticket to untangle it.

Review “may take several business days.” After activation you can invite other users. Add at least two people from the organization as admins, plus your agency if you use one, so the account never depends on one volunteer’s login again.

Step 5: Set up conversion tracking that actually counts

Conversion tracking is a requirement now. Before you write a single ad, get this part right.

The Ad Grants account management policy says accounts must have at least one qualifying conversion type and “accrue at least 1 conversion per month.” Accounts created on or after April 22, 2019 must also use conversion-based Smart Bidding on all campaigns: Maximize conversions, Maximize conversion value, Target CPA or Target ROAS. Smart Bidding needs conversion data to work, so a broken tag means ads that barely serve.

What counts, per the Ad Grants conversion tracking guide: donations, newsletter sign-ups, volunteer sign-ups, support line calls, purchases at a charity shop, app downloads. Track a form by its thank-you page where possible, and fall back to tracking the button click only if there’s no confirmation page. Phone calls need a minimum 30-second call length.

What doesn’t count as a primary conversion: time on site, homepage visits and similar soft signals. You can add them, but they belong in the “Other” category and shouldn’t be marked as conversions. Google also says that if your conversion rate looks very high, you should confirm it reflects a meaningful action. A 60% conversion rate on “visited the donate page” is the kind of thing that gets an account flagged.

Our setup is Google Tag Manager with GA4, a thank-you page for each form, and a donation platform that fires a purchase event with the gift amount. The form and call tracking guide walks through the tag setup step by step.

Step 7: Build campaigns and keywords that pass the rules

The keyword rules trip up more Houston accounts than anything else. From the Ad Grants policy compliance guide:

  • Keywords with a quality score of 1 or 2 must be paused or removed. Keywords showing “—” aren’t scored yet and aren’t monitored.
  • Single-word keywords aren’t permitted, with exceptions for your own brand terms, recognized medical conditions and a short list on the single keyword exceptions page: charity, charities, donate, donation, ngo, ngos, nonprofit, nonprofits, volunteer and volunteering.
  • Overly generic keywords that don’t show intent must be paused or removed. Google’s own examples are “free videos,” “e-books” and “today’s news.”
  • Each campaign needs at least 2 ad groups, and the account needs at least 2 sitelink assets.

Geo-targeting is in the main policy: show ads where people would actually use your services. A Montgomery County food pantry shouldn’t be serving ads in Galveston. If you have a local program and a national advocacy arm, split them into separate campaigns by geography. In Campaign settings > Locations, use “Presence: people in or regularly in your included locations,” and target the cities or counties you serve rather than “United States.”

Build each ad group tight: one theme, five to fifteen keywords, phrase and exact match, and a landing page that matches. “Volunteer with seniors The Woodlands” goes to the senior companion volunteer page, never the homepage. That tightness is also how you keep quality scores above 2. The quality score guide explains the three components if you want the mechanics.

Add negatives from day one. Volunteer campaigns pick up job seekers (“jobs,” “salary,” “hiring,” “paid”) unless you actually have paid roles. Food assistance campaigns pick up searches for SNAP office hours and grocery coupons. Donation campaigns attract “tax deduction calculator” and “how to start a nonprofit.” Read the search terms report every week for the first month.

Step 8: Write ads that clear the 5% CTR line

The rule: “maintain a 5% click-through rate (CTR) each month,” measured across the whole account. Miss it two consecutive months and the account is temporarily deactivated, per the Ad Grants account management policy. Accounts running only Smart campaigns are exempt, though we rarely recommend that route.

Five percent is hard with sloppy keywords and easy with tight ones. What moves it in accounts we’ve run:

  • Put the place and the action in headline one. “Volunteer in The Woodlands This Saturday” beats “Make a Difference Today” every time.
  • Use concrete details: shift length, age minimum, parking, whether groups are welcome. “2-Hour Shifts, Ages 12+ With a Parent.”
  • Fill every asset slot: four or more sitelinks, callouts, structured snippets, a call asset during office hours. Bigger ads get clicked more.
  • Pause any keyword that has a few hundred impressions and a CTR under 2%. It’s dragging the account average down, and a paused keyword costs you nothing.
  • Cut brand-adjacent fluff like “charity” or “nonprofit Houston” on their own, which pull huge impressions and low clicks.

Why do Google Ad Grants accounts get suspended?

Google says accounts that violate program policies are “subject to automatic suspension without notification.” So the first sign is often just that the ads stopped and the dashboard shows the account as paused or suspended. Here are the causes we see, roughly in order of frequency:

Reinstatement means fixing the problem and submitting a reactivation request from the account. Fix everything at once. Requests that fix the CTR but leave a single-word keyword live just get bounced. When the grant isn’t enough

Ad Grants is a great base. It has limits. It’s Search only and text only, and the policy restrictions mean some valuable searches (single-word causes, broad awareness terms) are off the table.

Plenty of Houston nonprofits pair the grant with a small paid Google Ads account for gala ticket sales or a capital campaign, plus Meta ads to reach volunteers who wouldn’t think to search. Keep the two Google accounts separate and keep the grant account clean. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get Google Ad Grants approved?

Plan on two to three weeks if your paperwork and website are in order. Goodstack validation for Google for Nonprofits usually takes 3 to 5 business days, and the Ad Grants activation review takes several more business days after you submit. Delays usually come from a legal name that doesn’t match IRS records, a document request sitting in a spam folder, or a website that fails the HTTPS or content requirements.

Can a Houston church or school get Google Ad Grants?

Schools, academic institutions and universities are not eligible, but their philanthropic arms, such as an ISD education foundation with its own 501(c)(3), may be. Churches aren’t named as an excluded category, but every applicant must be recognized by the IRS as a 501(c)(3) and pass Goodstack validation. Many churches never file for a determination letter, so check your status before applying.

What happens if my Ad Grants CTR drops below 5%?

One month under 5% is a warning sign, not a suspension. If the account-level click-through rate stays below 5% for two consecutive months, Google temporarily deactivates the account. To recover, pause low-performing keywords, tighten ad groups, add more sitelinks and callouts, and submit a reactivation request. Accounts that only run Smart campaigns are exempt from the CTR rule.

Do I need a 501(c)(3) to get free Google ads in Texas?

Yes. In the United States, Google for Nonprofits requires registration with the IRS as exempt under Section 501(c)(3), or a group exemption with proven affiliation to a 501(c)(3) parent. Fiscally sponsored projects without their own status are currently not eligible. Government entities, hospitals and health care organizations, and schools are excluded, though their charitable foundations may qualify.

Can someone else manage our Google Ad Grants account?

Yes. After activation you can invite additional users, including staff, board members or an agency, to manage the account. We recommend at least two admins from the organization so access never depends on one person. Google offers no payment for management, so an agency’s fee comes from your budget, and the account should stay owned by the nonprofit.
