Google Ads Quality Score: How to Lower Your Cost per Click

How Google Ads Quality Score works: expected CTR, ad relevance and landing page experience, how Ad Rank sets your CPC, and the fixes that lower what you pay.

Second week of July in The Woodlands. It’s 98 degrees, every AC company in Montgomery County has raised its Google Ads budget, and an HVAC owner is looking at his keyword report. “AC repair The Woodlands” shows a Quality Score of 3. His average cost per click on it has crept past what he was paying for whole jobs’ worth of leads a few summers ago.

His agency says Quality Score “doesn’t really matter anymore.” A forum thread says it’s the only thing that matters. Both are partly wrong.

Quality Score is Google’s 1-to-10 report card on how your ads and landing pages compare with other advertisers on the same searches. The score itself isn’t used in the auction. The quality it summarizes is, and Google says plainly that higher quality ads typically cost less per click. So you don’t chase the number. You fix what it’s pointing at, and the price comes down.

This guide covers how Google actually reports Quality Score, how Ad Rank and your cost per click are set, and the specific changes to ad groups, ads and landing pages that move it. If you want the price side first, our Houston cost-per-click benchmarks show what local businesses typically pay.

What Quality Score is, in Google’s own words

From Google’s Quality Score help page: Quality Score “is a diagnostic tool meant to give you a sense of how well your ad quality compares to other advertisers.” It’s measured from 1 to 10 and reported at the keyword level, for Search campaigns. It isn’t reported for an ad group or a campaign, and Performance Max doesn’t have it at all.

It has three components, each rated Above average, Average or Below average:

  • Expected clickthrough rate: how likely your ad is to be clicked when it shows.
  • Ad relevance: how closely your ad matches the intent behind the search.
  • Landing page experience: how relevant and useful your page is to the people who click.

The comparison is against other advertisers whose ads showed for the exact same search over the last 90 days, based on historical impressions for exact searches. That has a few consequences people miss. Changing a keyword’s match type doesn’t change its Quality Score. A broad match keyword can show a score with no impressions of its own if a matching exact keyword in the same ad group has data. And if there aren’t enough exact-match searches, you’ll see a “—” instead of a number. That’s common on long-tail local terms like “tankless water heater install Magnolia,” and it isn’t a problem.

Google also says Quality Score is “not a key performance indicator” and shouldn’t be aggregated with other data. Treat it like the warning lights on a dashboard. A light tells you where to look.

How to see Quality Score and its components

The columns are hidden by default. Per Google’s Quality Score help page:

  1. Go to Keywords under the Campaigns menu.
  2. Click the Columns icon at the top right of the table.
  3. Under “Modify columns for keywords,” open the Quality score section.
  4. Add Quality Score, Landing Page Exp., Exp. CTR and Ad Relevance.
  5. Click Apply.

The current columns show today’s snapshot. The “(hist.)” versions (Quality Score (hist.), Exp. CTR (hist.) and so on) show what the score was during the date range you pick, and you can segment by day to see when a change kicked in. That’s how you check whether last month’s landing page rebuild actually moved anything.

When we review an account, we sort keywords by cost, not by Quality Score. A QS of 4 on a keyword that spent $60 last quarter isn’t worth an afternoon. A QS of 4 on the keyword eating 30% of your budget is the whole project.

Reported Quality Score vs. the quality the auction actually uses

This is the part that confuses everyone, so it’s worth being precise.

The 1-to-10 number is a summary built from history. The auction doesn’t read it. What the auction uses is a fresh, real-time estimate of ad quality for that particular search. Google’s Google’s ad quality page says ad quality “varies with the context of every search,” and the Quality Score page lists things the score may not capture: the searcher’s device, location and time of day, and your assets.

So the same keyword can have very different auction-time quality at 2 p.m. on a desktop in the Energy Corridor and at 11 p.m. on a phone in Conroe. Your QS of 6 is an average across all of that. It’s useful for finding weak spots, and useless as a target.

Ad Rank, and how your cost per click is actually set

Where your ad shows, and whether it shows at all, comes from Ad Rank. Per Google’s Ad Rank page, Ad Rank uses six factors:

  1. Your bid (the most you’re willing to pay for a click).
  2. The quality of your ad and landing page.
  3. Ad Rank thresholds, the minimum an ad must clear to show.
  4. How competitive the auction is.
  5. The context of the search: terms, location, device, time and other signals.
  6. The expected impact of your assets and other ad formats, like sitelinks and call assets.

Ad Rank is calculated every time someone searches, and twice per auction: once for eligibility and once for ranking.

Then the price. Per Google’s actual CPC definition, you “only pay what’s minimally required to clear the Ad Rank thresholds,” which in practice means beating the Ad Rank of the advertiser just below you. With nobody below you, you pay the reserve price. On manual bidding you’re never charged more than your max CPC, though Enhanced CPC and bid adjustments can push past it. Google no longer publishes an exact formula.

That’s why quality lowers cost. If your quality is higher, you need less bid to reach the same Ad Rank, and the amount you need to beat the next advertiser is smaller. Google says “higher quality ads typically cost less per click than lower quality ads,” and that low-quality ads can push your actual CPC close to your max even with little competition (Google’s ad quality page).

Here’s a deliberately simplified model to show the mechanics. It treats Ad Rank as bid times a quality factor and ignores thresholds, assets and context, so read it as a teaching example and nothing more.

Expected CTR: write ads people pick

Expected CTR is Google’s prediction of how often your ad gets clicked when it shows, compared with others on the same search. You raise it by making the ad the obvious choice for the searcher.

What moves it in local service accounts we’ve run:

  • The search term in the headline. Someone typing “emergency plumber Katy” should see “Emergency Plumber in Katy” or close to it. That isn’t a trick; it’s confirmation they found what they asked for.
  • A concrete reason to click. “Same-day AC repair, upfront pricing, 4.9 stars from 600+ reviews” beats “Quality Service You Can Trust.” Google’s Google’s guide to optimizing ads and landing pages recommends specific calls to action and differentiators like guarantees and offers.
  • Assets. Sitelinks, callouts, call assets, location and structured snippets make the ad bigger and more useful, and their expected impact is one of the six Ad Rank factors.
  • Negative keywords. Every impression on “ac repair jobs” or “how to recharge car ac” that doesn’t get clicked drags your CTR down. Clean the search terms report weekly at first.
  • Honest location and schedule settings. Showing ads to people outside your service area, or at 2 a.m. when you can’t answer, earns impressions nobody clicks.

Ad relevance: tighten the ad groups

Ad relevance is the easiest component to fix and the one most often rated Below average in small accounts. The cause is almost always one big ad group with 60 keywords and two ads trying to speak to all of them.

You don’t need single-keyword ad groups. That habit made sense years ago; with close variants and broad match, it mostly creates clutter. What you need is ad groups tight enough that one set of headlines honestly answers every keyword in them. For a Houston plumbing company, that might mean separate ad groups for water heater repair, water heater replacement, tankless installs, slab leak detection, drain cleaning and emergency service, each with its own ads and its own landing page. “Slab leak” is very much a Houston search, by the way; the clay soil here moves foundations and pipes with it.

A quick test: read each keyword in the ad group, then read your Headline 1. If a searcher typing that keyword would think “close, but not what I asked for,” the keyword belongs somewhere else.

A responsive search ad strategy that helps instead of hurting

Responsive search ads let you give Google up to 15 headlines of 30 characters each and up to 4 descriptions of 90 characters, and Google mixes them, per Google’s responsive search ads page. Up to three headlines can show at once, and only Headline 1 and Description 1 reliably appear.

How we write them for a local service ad group:

  • Three or four headlines that contain the keyword theme (“Water Heater Repair Spring TX,” “Same-Day Water Heater Repair”).
  • Three or four that state proof: review count and rating, years in business, licensed and insured, your Texas license number if your trade has one.
  • Three or four with the offer or call to action: “Upfront Price Before We Start,” “Call Now, Tech Today.”
  • A couple about service area (“Serving The Woodlands to Tomball”).
  • Descriptions that read fine in any order and each make one clear point.

Go easy on pinning. Google says pinning isn’t recommended for most advertisers and limits which assets can show; if you must pin, pin two or three options to a position, not one. The common exception is a legal or brand line that has to appear. Google also reports that advertisers who moved Ad Strength from Poor to Excellent saw 15% more clicks and conversions on average, from its own internal data. Treat Ad Strength as a nudge toward variety; the search terms report is still the better guide to what to write.

Landing page experience: speed and message match

Landing page experience is the component most tied to whether a click becomes a customer, and it’s where most Houston small-business accounts lose. The usual sin is sending every ad to the homepage.

Google’s Google’s guide to optimizing ads and landing pages lists the basics: a page that closely matches the ad and keywords, mirrors the ad’s call to action, is mobile-friendly and fast, keeps contact details easy to find, keeps navigation simple, avoids clutter and pop-ups, and is clear about what you do.

In practice, message match means the page headline repeats the promise of the ad. If the ad says “Same-Day AC Repair in Cypress,” the page says that above the fold, with a phone number you can tap and a short form. Speed matters most on phones; heavy sliders, autoplay video and five tracking scripts on a cheap shared host are what we see slowing local sites down. What Quality Score doesn’t affect, and what doesn’t affect it

A lot of bad advice comes from treating QS as more than it is. A few things Google states outright:

  • It isn’t an auction input. The 1-10 number itself doesn’t set your price (Google’s Quality Score help page).
  • Bids don’t change ad quality. Bids affect Ad Rank, but not Google’s assessment of your ad’s quality (Google’s ad quality page).
  • Account structure, ad frequency and reported conversions don’t affect it either (same page). Restructuring into more campaigns doesn’t raise QS by itself; better-matched ads and pages do.
  • Match type changes don’t change it, since it’s based on exact searches.
  • It isn’t a KPI. A keyword with QS 5 that books jobs at a good cost is a better keyword than a QS 9 that books none.

And it doesn’t exist for Performance Max, Display or Demand Gen. If most of your spend runs through Performance Max, QS tells you nothing about it; see Performance Max vs. Search for how to judge those campaigns.

A 30-day plan to raise Quality Score on the keywords that cost you most

Here’s how we’d run it.

Days 1-3. Add the Quality Score columns. Sort keywords by cost over the last 90 days. Mark the top 20 and note which component is Below average on each.

Days 4-10. Split ad groups where ad relevance is weak. Write new responsive search ads for each split group with the keyword theme in several headlines. Add negatives from the search terms report.

Days 11-20. Build or fix a dedicated landing page for each major service, matched to the ad. Check mobile speed. Make the phone number tappable and the form short.

Days 21-30. Let data come in. Compare Quality Score (hist.) and average CPC on those 20 keywords to the prior period, and watch conversion rate and cost per lead alongside them.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good Quality Score in Google Ads?

Google reports Quality Score from 1 to 10 for each Search keyword. Scores of 7 and up usually mean your ads and landing pages compare well with competitors on the same searches, and a score of 4 or lower on a high-spend keyword is worth fixing. Google calls Quality Score a diagnostic, not a KPI, so judge keywords by cost per lead and booked jobs first.

Does Quality Score affect cost per click?

Indirectly, yes. The 1 to 10 number isn’t used in the auction, but the ad quality it summarizes is part of Ad Rank, which decides your position and what you pay. Google says higher quality ads typically cost less per click than lower quality ads. Better expected CTR, ad relevance and landing page experience let you reach the same position with a lower bid.

How is cost per click calculated in Google Ads?

Google says you pay only what’s minimally required to clear the Ad Rank thresholds and beat the Ad Rank of the advertiser just below you. If nobody is below you, you pay the reserve price. With manual bidding you won’t be charged more than your max CPC, though Enhanced CPC and bid adjustments can exceed it. Google no longer publishes an exact formula.

How long does it take for Quality Score to improve?

Quality Score is based on exact-match search history over the last 90 days, so changes to ads and landing pages show up gradually as new impressions build. On busy keywords you may see movement within a few weeks. On low-volume local terms it can take longer, and some will show a dash because there isn’t enough data. Check the historical columns segmented by day.

Why does my keyword show a dash instead of a Quality Score?

A dash means there haven’t been enough exact-match searches for Google to calculate a score. It’s common on long-tail local keywords like a specific service plus a small town name. It doesn’t hurt your ads. Google suggests finding higher-volume keywords in Keyword Planner or broadening match types if you want the keyword to enter more auctions and collect data.
