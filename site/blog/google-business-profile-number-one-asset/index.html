“Do I even need a website if everybody calls from the Google thing?” We hear some version of that from owners in Spring and The Woodlands more than you’d think. It’s a fair question. For a lot of local businesses the profile produces more calls than the site. You still need the website (we’ll get to why), but the instinct about which one people see first is right.

Short answer: for a local business in Houston or The Woodlands, the Google Business Profile is usually the most valuable marketing asset you own because it’s where people compare you, read reviews, call and get directions, often without visiting your site. It’s free. Most profiles are half finished, which is your opening.

This is the field-by-field version: what Google says decides rankings, the settings most Houston profiles get wrong, what changed between 2024 and 2026, and how to tell whether the profile is working.

Is a Google Business Profile more important than a website?

For the first impression, usually yes. For closing the sale, they work as a pair.

The profile is where comparison happens now. In BrightLocal’s 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, published in February 2026 from a panel of 1,002 U.S. adults, 97% of consumers said they read reviews for local businesses and Google was still the top review source at 71%, down from 83% a year earlier. Generative AI tools jumped to 45% as a source for local recommendations. Those AI answers lean heavily on the same reviews and profile data, so a weak profile costs you in two places.

The website still matters because the profile can’t hold everything. Pricing detail, your full service list, financing, before-and-after galleries, the reason to pick you over the four other dentists in the map pack. The profile earns the click or call; the site handles the customer who wants more proof first. If you’ve been relying on a free Google-built site, note that Google shut those down in March 2024 and started redirecting them to profiles, per Search Engine Land.

What Google says decides local rankings

Google is unusually direct about this. In Google’s own explanation of local ranking, local results are based on three things:

  • Relevance: “how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for.”
  • Distance: how far the business is from the searcher, or from the location in the search.
  • Prominence: “how well-known a business is,” including links, articles and review count and rating.

The same page says “There’s no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google.” Ads can appear above the map pack, but they don’t move your organic map position. Anyone selling you guaranteed map rankings is selling something Google says doesn’t exist.

Distance is the one you can’t change, and it explains most of the frustration we hear. A plumber off Woodlands Parkway will usually rank well in Shenandoah and Oak Ridge North and badly in Katy, no matter how good the profile is.

That’s why the profile and the local landing pages on your website work together. The profile wins near your address. The pages, reviews from farther-out customers, and ads cover the towns where distance works against you. Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack goes further on prominence.

The field-by-field setup most Houston profiles get wrong

Open your profile from Google Search (search your business name while signed in) or the Maps app, and go through this table. The limits come from Google’s Edit your Business Profile page and Google’s guidelines for representing your business.

Business nameMust match your real-world name; no taglines, locations or extra keywords (Google’s example: “Midas” is fine, “Midas Auto Service Experts” isn’t)Use the name on your sign. Keyword-stuffed names invite edits and suspensions.
Primary categoryOne; choose the most specific that completes “this business IS a”Pick the category for the work you most want. “Emergency veterinarian” and “Veterinarian” aren’t interchangeable.

One judgment call: call tracking. Google’s guidelines don’t address it directly. Our practice is to put a tracking number as primary and the main business line as an additional number so the listing stays consistent with the rest of the web. Do it once and leave it; swapping primary numbers every few months is asking for trouble.

The name field is where we see Houston businesses take the most risk. “Best Roofing Company Houston TX Roof Repair” might rank for a while. Then a competitor suggests an edit, or Google catches it, and the profile gets suspended right before storm season. Read our reinstatement guide if that’s already happened.

Two fields get skipped that shouldn’t. Attributes (wheelchair-accessible entrance, free parking, women-owned or veteran-owned where they apply, online appointments) are small, but they’re exactly what an AI answer pulls when someone asks Maps for “a pediatric dentist in Cypress with Saturday hours.” And the appointment or booking link should go to a page where someone can actually book, not your homepage. If you use a scheduler, link straight to it.

What changed on Google Business Profiles from 2024 to 2026

A lot of advice online is stale. Here’s what’s gone and what’s new.

  • Free Business Profile websites were shut down in March 2024 and redirected to the profile, per Search Engine Land.
  • Chat and call history ended on July 31, 2024, per Google’s notice on chat and call history. If a vendor’s report still shows “chats,” ask where they come from.
  • Public Q&A was pulled back through 2025 and replaced by an AI “Ask” feature in Maps that answers from your own information and reviews, as Search Engine Roundtable reported in December 2025. Answers you’d previously posted may still inform Google, but seeding your own Q&A, once standard advice, isn’t the lever it was. Your description, services and reviews do that work now.
  • Review requests got stricter. Google’s Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps already banned incentives, review gating and discouraging negative reviews. In April 2026 it added language against making staff collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content, per PPC Land. Stop the “get 10 reviews that mention your name” contests.

Reviews: the part of the profile people read first

Most of what makes a profile convert is the review section. The 2026 numbers from BrightLocal’s 2026 survey are harder on businesses than they used to be.

Thirty-one percent of consumers will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17% the year before. Forty-seven percent won’t use a business with fewer than 20 reviews. Seventy-four percent only care about reviews from the last three months, which means a profile with 300 reviews and none since spring looks closed. And 89% expect owners to respond, with 81% expecting a reply within a week.

A review ask that works and stays inside Google’s rules:

  1. Send the same request to every customer, by text, within a few hours of the job or visit.
  2. Keep it short: “Thanks for choosing us today. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other families in Spring find us: [link]. Either way, we appreciate you.”
  3. Mention it once in person at checkout. No discount, no pressure, no “only if it’s five stars.”
  4. Reply to every review within a week. For bad ones, acknowledge the specific problem, skip the defensiveness and give a name and number to call.

One Houston-specific habit worth copying: answer a review in the language it was written in. A Spanish or Vietnamese review in Bellaire or Alief that gets a reply in English only reads as a form letter. If nobody on staff writes that language well, keep the reply short and have someone fluent check it.

Photos, posts and storm hours: keeping a Houston profile alive

Customers notice dead profiles. The newest photo is from 2022, the last update was a Fourth of July post, and the hours say open on a day the office was flooded.

A realistic cadence for a small team:

  • Photos: four to ten new ones a month. Real jobs, real staff, the storefront from the street so first-timers can find the turn off FM 1488 or Kuykendahl.
  • Updates (posts): one or two a month. A seasonal offer, a new service, a recent project. Short, with a photo and a button.
  • Services: review each quarter. Add what you started offering, remove what you stopped.

Houston adds one job most of the country doesn’t have: weather hours. When Hurricane Beryl came through in July 2024, the Texas Tribune counted 2.26 million power outages across the region, and plenty of businesses that were closed for days still showed “Open” on Google. Every customer who drove over and found a dark building had a bad experience you couldn’t see. Set special hours from your phone the moment you know, using Google’s special hours help page, and mark “temporarily closed” if you need to. Freeze days in January get the same treatment. Our hurricane season playbook has the rest.

How do I know if my Google Business Profile is working?

Open the Performance tab. Per Google’s performance help page, you’ll see views (unique people, counted at most once per person per day), the search terms that surfaced you, calls (taps on the call button), direction requests, website clicks, and bookings or messages where they apply.

Those are interactions, not customers. To connect them to revenue:

  • Calls: a tap isn’t a conversation. Call tracking on the profile’s number tells you how many connected, how long they lasted and which became appointments.
  • Website clicks: tag the profile’s link with UTM parameters (for example utm_source=google, utm_medium=organic, utm_campaign=gbp) so GA4 reports profile traffic separately. Our GA4 tracking guide walks through setup.
  • Search terms: check monthly. If “emergency plumber” never appears but “plumbing supply” does, your categories or services need work.
  • Direction requests: for storefronts, compare to walk-ins. A spike in requests with flat walk-ins can mean a bad pin location.

Look at trends over three months, not week to week. Weather, holidays and school calendars move Houston local search more than most owners expect.

Suspensions, scam calls and the address question

Three problems eat more GBP time than everything else combined, at least in the accounts we see.

Suspensions. The usual triggers are a keyword-stuffed name, a virtual office or coworking address without signage, and repeated edits to the address or name. Google says changing your address or name after verification may mean verifying again. If you’re moving, read how to change your address without a suspension first, and see video verification for what Google asks you to film.

Scam calls. “This is Google, your listing will be deleted.” It isn’t Google. The profile is free, and Google doesn’t cold-call threatening to remove it. Our scam calls post has the script for hanging up.

The address question. A Spring mailing address inside The Woodlands, or a Conroe address on the Woodlands side of the county line, changes which searches you show for. Use the address that’s actually on your building, keep it identical everywhere online, and let landing pages handle the towns you serve but aren’t located in.

A 20-minute weekly Google Business Profile routine

Nobody needs to live in the dashboard. Twenty minutes on Monday morning covers it.

Once a quarter, add a longer pass: recheck categories against your top competitors in the map pack, update services, refresh the description, and compare three months of calls and direction requests with the same period last year.

Frequently asked questions

How do I rank higher on Google Maps in The Woodlands or Houston?

Google ranks local results on relevance, distance and prominence, and says you can’t pay for better placement. Choose the most specific primary category, fill in services and the description, keep hours accurate, and earn a steady stream of recent reviews you reply to. Distance from the searcher is fixed, so use local landing pages and ads for towns farther from your address.

How many Google reviews does a local business need?

There’s no official number, but BrightLocal’s 2026 survey found 47% of consumers won’t use a business with fewer than 20 reviews and 74% only care about reviews from the last three months. Aim for at least 20 to start, then a steady flow every month. Recent reviews matter more than a large old total, and replies to them matter too.

Can I use a tracking number on my Google Business Profile?

Google’s guidelines don’t address call tracking directly. They require numbers under your direct control and allow one primary plus up to two additional numbers. A common approach is a local tracking number as primary with your main line as an additional number, so the listing still matches your number elsewhere online. Set it once and avoid frequent changes.

What happened to Q&A on Google Business Profiles?

Google pulled back public questions and answers on Business Profiles during 2025 and replaced them with an AI Ask feature in Google Maps that answers from a business’s own information and reviews. Previously published answers may still inform Google. In practice, a complete description, detailed services, accurate attributes and recent reviews now do the job seeded Q&A used to do.

How often should I post on my Google Business Profile?

One or two updates a month is enough for most small businesses, plus four to ten new photos. Posts keep the profile looking active and give customers current offers or news, but they matter less for ranking than categories, reviews and distance. Spend the extra time replying to reviews and updating special hours around holidays and Houston storms.
