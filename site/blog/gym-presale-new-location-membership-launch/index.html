Thursday, 6:15 p.m., in a leased trailer at the edge of a parking lot off FM 2978. The building behind it is still studs and conduit. Your presale manager has two tours booked, a third walk-in who saw the banner from the road, and 38 new leads from yesterday’s Facebook ads that nobody has called yet.

Opening day is supposedly 74 days away. The contractor said “supposedly” too.

This is what presale marketing actually looks like. A gym presale is the stretch, usually about 90 days, when you sell founding memberships before the doors open, so the club starts with dues already coming in and a floor that isn’t empty. Done well, it pays for a good chunk of the launch. Done badly, you get a list of 4,000 “leads,” 140 members, and a refund problem when the opening slips.

We’ve run club launches for Equinox, so this comes from doing it rather than reading about it. If you want the general gym marketing picture (intro offers, Google profile, retention), our gym and fitness studio marketing guide covers it. This post is only about the presale.

What a presale has to accomplish

A presale has three jobs, and most clubs only plan for the first one.

  1. Sign members before opening, at a price that still makes sense once the founding rate is locked in for years.
  2. Build a waitlist of people who didn’t buy yet, so opening week has a second wave of buyers.
  3. Teach the neighborhood the club exists, so you show up in searches, in conversations at the pickup line and on Nextdoor the week you open.

The demand is there. The Health & Fitness Association counted a record 81 million U.S. fitness facility members in 2025, up 5.2% from 2024, or 26.1% of Americans age six and up, per the Health & Fitness Association’s 2026 consumer report. The same report found the share of members who never use their membership fell to 4.6%. People are joining and showing up.

So is the competition. Community Impact listed nine new fitness locations in The Woodlands area going into 2025, from a pickleball club in Magnolia to a Pilates studio on Woodlands Parkway. A new club in Spring, Cypress or Katy is never the only new thing in the feed.

The 90/60/30-day presale timeline

Ninety days is the window our sports and fitness work is built on. Shorter and you open half full. Much longer and people forget they bought, or construction makes a liar of you.

Days 120 to 90: setup. Register with the state if you need to (see the Texas section), finalize the founding offer, build the landing page and CRM pipeline, and shoot creative. Put the banner on the building the day you’re allowed to.

Days 90 to 60: waitlist and first sales. Ads go live around the site. The goal for the first two weeks is a waitlist big enough to sell from. Open the presale office or trailer as soon as it’s safe and permitted. The first founding-rate tier sells here.

Days 60 to 30: the grind. Leads get cheaper, but closing gets harder because the easiest buyers already bought. Follow-up discipline decides the number here. Run your first price-increase deadline and a member-get-member push.

Days 30 to 0: urgency and media. Final founding tier, hard-hat tours if allowed, a founders’ preview night, and bigger launch media like the CTV and digital out-of-home bursts we ran for Equinox (our streaming TV post covers buying it locally).

Founding-member offers that sell without wrecking your pricing

The founding offer is the product you’re actually advertising for 90 days, so design it before anyone writes an ad.

What a good founding offer usually has:

  • A rate lock below standard dues, for as long as the membership stays active. This is the thing people buy.
  • Tiers that close. The first 200 members get the lowest rate, the next 300 get a slightly higher one, then standard pricing on opening day. Real caps, publicly counted down, honored exactly.
  • A reduced or waived enrollment fee instead of a lower monthly rate, if your margins are tight. A waived fee costs you once. A low rate costs you every month.
  • Something only founders get: a founder bag, first booking rights for classes, a preview night.
  • Billing that starts on opening day (or a small deposit now), which removes the “why am I paying for a gym I can’t use” objection.

What we’d avoid: lifetime rates with no conditions, discounts so deep that standard pricing looks like a ripoff, and “limited spots” claims that aren’t limited. Members at a new club talk.

The presale office or trailer

A real place to sit down and sign closes more than any landing page. Most of the clubs we’ve seen launch well have a presale office: a trailer on site, a vacant suite in the same center, or a pop-up in a nearby retail space.

What makes it work:

  • Visible from the road, with signage that says “Founding memberships, now open” and the hours. Drive-by traffic on Woodlands Parkway or the Grand Parkway frontage is free marketing.
  • Renderings and samples. Floor plans, equipment specs, locker room finishes. People are buying something they can’t see.
  • Hours that match when people come. Evenings and Saturday mornings. A trailer that closes at 5 p.m. misses the commuters coming home from downtown or the Energy Corridor.
  • Booked appointments, not just walk-ins. Every lead gets offered a tour of the presale office. That appointment is the main metric (see the follow-up section).

If your presale runs through a Houston summer, budget for real AC and a covered entrance, or rent a suite. A sweaty consult is a short consult.

Meta lead ads vs. landing pages for a presale

Most presale leads come from Facebook and Instagram, because nobody searches for a gym that doesn’t exist yet. The question is where the click goes.

Instant forms (lead ads) are cheap and fast. People submit without leaving Instagram, so you get a lot of leads for the money, and plenty of them forgot they filled anything out. A few settings help a lot, and Jon Loomer’s guide to instant forms walks through them: choose the Higher intent form type, which adds a review step before submitting; add one or two custom questions (“When are you looking to start?” and “Which location?”); and use conditional logic to send people who say they’re not local to a close-form screen instead of your CRM. For deeper problems, our post on fixing bad Facebook leads covers more.

Landing pages cost more per lead and sell better. The page can show the founding tiers, the countdown, renderings, a booking calendar for the presale office, and a checkout to buy the founding membership online. Optimize the campaign for the purchase or the booked tour, not the form fill, once the pixel and Conversions API are tracking those events.

Our default for a single club: instant forms to build the waitlist in the first month, a landing page campaign optimized for purchases or bookings from day 30 on, and retargeting from both.

One more option worth knowing about. Meta’s “conversion leads” optimization lets you feed lead stages from your CRM back to Meta so it finds people who actually buy, not just people who fill out forms. Per Meta’s Conversions API for CRM documentation, it works only with instant forms, Meta wants at least 200 leads a month, you should upload data at least daily, and the stage you optimize for should happen within 28 days of the lead with a conversion rate between 1% and 40%. A busy presale clears all of those. “Booked a presale tour” is a good stage to send.

Geo radius and targeting around the site

Geography does most of the targeting work in a presale. People join gyms close to home or work, so the radius decides who sees you.

In Meta Ads Manager, you set it at the ad set level under Audience, then Locations: drop a pin on the site and choose a radius. Meta allows a pin radius between 1 and 50 miles, and the old “living in” versus “traveling in” choices have been folded into one setting, people living in or recently in the location, per Big Eye Agency’s 2026 guide to Meta geo targeting. That “recently in” part means you’ll reach some people passing through, which is one more reason to qualify on the form.

Radius rules of thumb from accounts we’ve run (our experience, not platform guidance):

  • Dense, inside-the-Loop locations: 2 to 4 miles. People won’t cross the city for a gym.
  • Suburban sites in The Woodlands, Spring, Cypress, Katy or Pearland: 5 to 8 miles, then shape it. A pin near I-45 can reach neighborhoods across the freeway that won’t make the drive at 6 a.m.
  • Destination clubs with a pool, courts or a big kids’ club: up to 10 to 12 miles, with a separate, tighter ad set for the closest neighborhoods so they get more frequency.

On Google Ads, use radius targeting around the site and set the location option to Presence (people in or regularly in your targeted locations) instead of the default Presence or Interest, which also reaches people merely interested in the area, per Google Ads Help. Search volume is small until the banner goes up. Bid on your brand plus the area (“[brand] The Woodlands”), “new gym [town]” and category terms like “24 hour gym Spring TX,” with the founding rate in the ad. Add negatives for jobs, careers, “hiring” and “day pass” early.

Follow-up and appointment setting

This is where presales are won or lost. A lead called within five minutes and offered a tour is worth far more than one emailed two days later. Our speed-to-lead guide has the details, and the presale version is simple:

  1. Instant text when the lead comes in (with consent captured on the form), from a real name, offering two tour times.
  2. A call within 15 minutes during staffed hours. The only goal is a booked appointment at the presale office.
  3. Reminder text the day before and two hours before the tour.
  4. No-show follow-up the same day, with a new time.
  5. Weekly nurture for the “not yet” crowd: construction updates, tier countdowns, a class schedule preview.

A first text that tends to work:

“Hi Maria, it’s Josh with [Club] Spring. Thanks for asking about founding memberships. We have tours at the presale office tomorrow at 6:00 or Saturday at 10:30. Want me to hold one? Founders 1 has 41 spots left.”

Specific, short, a real deadline.

Texting has legal strings in Texas. Since September 1, 2025, SB 140 brings text messages under the state’s telephone solicitation law, which means many businesses texting Texans to sell something have to register with the Secretary of State (a $10,000 security and a $200 fee per location), and consumers can sue under the DTPA for violations, per Morgan Lewis’s summary of SB 140. There are exemptions, including some for current or former customers and businesses that mostly sell from brick-and-mortar locations, but a presale lead hasn’t bought anything yet and your club isn’t open. Ask your lawyer before you launch the text sequence, and see our Texas SMS rules post for the basics.

Texas health spa rules that shape a presale

Texas regulates gyms that sell memberships as “health spas,” and presales get special attention. This isn’t legal advice, but your marketing has to fit inside these rules.

Per the Texas Secretary of State’s health spa FAQ, a business selling memberships for an exercise program generally needs a health spa certificate of registration from the Secretary of State before selling memberships ($100 a year), with security of $20,000 to $50,000 per location depending on prepaid memberships, unless it qualifies for an exemption. One exemption covers operators that don’t offer contracts longer than 31 days, don’t take prepayment for longer than 31 days and don’t charge initiation fees, among other conditions. Founding offers with enrollment fees or annual prepay usually fall outside that exemption, so check before you design the offer.

The presale-specific parts, also per the Secretary of State: if you accept prepayments before opening, you have to open an escrow account at an FDIC-insured institution within 14 days of the first prepayment, file a statement about it, and keep it until 30 days after the club opens.

Two contract rules matter to marketers. Section 702.305 of the Occupations Code requires a conspicuous notice telling presale buyers they get a full refund if the spa doesn’t open before the 181st day after they prepaid (with different terms if the operator has a nearby location they can use). And under Section 702.307, members can cancel for a full refund by midnight of the third business day after signing.

So what changes in the marketing:

  • Count members net of the three-day cancellations before you celebrate a weekend.
  • Don’t start taking money too early. If you sell on day 120 and construction slips four months, you’re past the 181-day line.
  • Ads promise what the contract says. “Rate locked for as long as you’re a member” has to match the actual terms.

When construction runs late

It will. Permits, inspections, a late HVAC unit, a week of August storms. The question is how you handle it.

  • Never put a hard opening date in ads until the certificate of occupancy is in sight. Say “Opening this spring” or “Opening early 2027” until you’re sure, then switch to the date.
  • Tell founders first, by email and text, before it shows up on Facebook. Include the new window and what you’re doing for them: an extra free month, a guest pass, an extended founding-rate deadline for their friends.
  • Pull back spend, don’t stop it. Cut prospecting to roughly a maintenance level and keep retargeting and nurture running. Restarting a campaign from zero costs more than idling one.
  • Watch the 181-day refund window for your earliest buyers and plan the communication before anyone asks.

Delays are also content. A phone video of the floor going in builds more trust than a render.

Measuring cost per presale member

Cost per lead is the number everybody looks at and the one that matters least. The presale number is cost per net presale member.

Total presale marketing cost should include media, creative, the landing page, CRM and texting tools, and the agency. Presale office rent and staff are your call; just be consistent between launches.

For reference, the Equinox launches our team ran combined geo-targeted paid social and search starting 90 days before opening, a founding-member offer with dedicated landing pages and a waitlist, CRM, SMS and email nurture, and launch-week CTV and digital out-of-home. The Equinox case study reports $4.8M in presale membership revenue, 11,400 founding-member leads, 94% capacity on opening day and cost per lead 62% below benchmark. Your club isn’t Equinox and your numbers won’t match, but the structure transfers.

The ramp after opening day

Opening day is the start of the second campaign.

Weeks one and two: the waitlist gets an opening-week offer and an invitation to tour the finished club. Swap renderings for real member footage the first weekend, and push Google search on brand and “gym near me” terms now that your Business Profile is live. Our grand opening plan covers the event side.

Weeks two through eight: ask founders for Google reviews while the excitement is fresh (our Google reviews guide has the ask), launch a member-get-member offer, and start onboarding: a first-week check-in, a free session with a coach, a class challenge. Founders who don’t build a habit in the first month are your first cancellations.

Months three through six: shift from presale urgency to always-on demand, at a cost per member you can sustain.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should a gym start a presale?

About 90 days before opening is the window we plan around, with setup work starting a month earlier. That gives time to build a waitlist, sell founding tiers and run launch media. Starting much earlier risks buyers losing interest and, in Texas, getting close to the 181-day refund line if construction slips. Avoid publishing a hard opening date until the certificate of occupancy is close.

What is a good founding member offer for a new gym?

Most good founding offers lock a lower monthly rate for as long as the membership stays active, sold in tiers with real caps that close on schedule. Waiving the enrollment fee is a cheaper alternative to a lower rate. Add something only founders get, like a preview night or first class booking. Avoid fake scarcity and lifetime rates with no conditions, since members compare notes.

Should gym presale ads use Facebook lead forms or a landing page?

Use both. Instant forms build a waitlist cheaply in the first month, especially with the Higher intent form type and a qualifying question or two. A landing page with founding tiers, renderings, tour booking and online checkout converts better and lets you optimize for purchases or booked tours. Retarget both audiences, and judge each by cost per net presale member, not cost per lead.

Do Texas gyms need to register before selling presale memberships?

Generally yes. Texas treats gyms selling memberships as health spas, which need a Secretary of State registration and security of $20,000 to $50,000 per location unless exempt. Prepayments before opening require an escrow account opened within 14 days. Contracts need a refund notice tied to the 181st day after prepayment, and members can cancel within three business days. Have a lawyer review your contract.

How do you calculate cost per presale member?

Divide total presale marketing cost by net presale members. Include media, creative, landing pages, CRM and texting tools and agency fees, and decide once whether presale office rent and staff belong in it. Net members means members sold minus three-day cancellations and refunds. Track lead-to-tour rate, show rate and close rate alongside it so you can see where the funnel is leaking.
