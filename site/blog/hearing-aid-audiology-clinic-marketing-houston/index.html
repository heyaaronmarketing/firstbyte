Monday morning at a clinic off Kingwood Drive. The first walk-in of the day is a retired refinery engineer holding a box of over-the-counter hearing aids he bought at a pharmacy. He can’t get the app to pair. His daughter booked the appointment online from Katy, and she’s on speakerphone asking whether you take his Medicare Advantage plan.

Five years ago that man would have been your patient from the start. Now he’s a patient who tried something else first, and how you treat him decides whether he buys a $4,000 pair from you next year or keeps fiddling with the box.

Hearing aid marketing in Houston now means three things. You show up where older adults and their adult children search and scroll, you explain Medicare and Medicare Advantage benefits better than anyone else in town, and you make the free screening or OTC help visit an honest front door instead of a bait-and-switch. All of it has to happen inside Texas licensing rules that are more specific about advertising than most owners realize.

What OTC hearing aids changed, and what they didn’t

The FDA’s over-the-counter category took effect October 17, 2022. Per the FDA’s final rule, OTC devices are for people 18 and older with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss, and a state can’t require a dispenser of OTC hearing aids to hold a special license. Texas followed with rule changes effective January 1, 2024, exempting certain OTC activities like sales from the fitter and audiologist chapters, per TDLR’s over-the-counter practice standard. Then in September 2024 the FDA authorized the first OTC hearing aid software, for Apple’s AirPods Pro.

So the pharmacy, the electronics store and the earbuds in a patient’s pocket are now competition at the low end. That’s real. But the data suggests the in-person clinic didn’t lose its place:

MarkeTrak 2025 found about four in 10 respondents with hearing difficulty own a hearing aid, and 70% of OTC owners are first-time buyers. That last number is the marketing opportunity. OTC is bringing new people into the category, and a lot of them will want help. Per HHTM’s coverage of a 2026 BMC Geriatrics study, MarkeTrak 2025 also found 85% of hearing aid owners prefer to make future purchases in person with professional help.

The clinics doing well with this have stopped fighting OTC in their ads. They offer an “OTC check-up” visit: bring in what you bought, we’ll test your hearing, check the fit and settings, and tell you honestly if it’s enough. Some of those patients leave happy with their OTC pair and leave you a review. Others need more than a mild-to-moderate device can do, and now they trust you.

Texas licensing and the ad rules most clinics haven’t read

Two TDLR programs cover this field. Hearing instrument fitters and dispensers are licensed under Occupations Code Chapter 402. Audiologists are licensed under Chapter 401 through the Speech-Language Pathologists and Audiologists program, and need a doctoral degree in audiology (or a master’s conferred by the end of 2007), per TDLR’s audiologist license page. TDLR’s fitter and dispenser FAQ refers to people licensed to fit and dispense under either chapter, which is why you’ll see both kinds of practices competing for the same patient.

For fitters and dispensers, Texas Occupations Code §402.103 spells out what makes an ad false, misleading or deceptive. Several items are aimed squarely at the way hearing aids have been advertised for decades:

Texas says an ad is misleading if it…What that means for your ads
Advertises a discounted price without identifying the specific product or its usual price (in the ad or at the item’s location)“40% off premium hearing aids” alone isn’t enough. Name the model and show the usual price.
Shows several hearing instruments but prices only one without saying whichEvery device pictured with a price needs to be clearly matched to it.
Calls a product innovation “new” when it’s been offered in Texas for 12 months or moreRetire “new technology” headlines after a year.
Claims you manufacture instruments at your office unless you disclose the manufacturer“Custom made here” needs the real manufacturer named next to it.
Misstates your professional achievements, education or qualificationsTitles and credentials must be exact, everywhere, including directory listings.

Audiologists have their own board rules, and anyone using “Dr.” should name the degree. If you’re running a direct mail piece that a manufacturer’s co-op program wrote, read it against this table before it goes to print. Those templates are written for 50 states, not Texas.

Medicare and Medicare Advantage: the page that books appointments

Almost every hearing aid patient over 65 asks about Medicare, and the honest answer has layers.

  • Original Medicare. It doesn’t cover hearing aids or exams for fitting them, per Medicare’s hearing aid page.
  • Diagnostic exams. Part B covers diagnostic hearing and balance exams when a doctor orders them, and patients can see an audiologist once every 12 months without an order for non-acute hearing loss, per Medicare’s hearing and balance exam page. They pay 20% after the Part B deductible.
  • Medicare Advantage. Many Part C plans add hearing benefits Original Medicare lacks. The details vary by plan, and some route the benefit through a hearing network the plan contracts with.

Your patients’ plans can change every January. Build an insurance page that lists the plans and hearing networks you’re in, with a “last updated” date, and a form where the patient or their daughter can enter the plan name and get a callback with their actual benefit. Then build a Google Ads ad group for searches like “hearing aids covered by Humana” or “UnitedHealthcare hearing aid benefit” and send it to that page. Only bid on plan names you actually accept.

Medicare’s fall open enrollment and the first weeks of January are your peak season for benefit questions. Plan the budget around it.

Your audience is two people: the patient and the adult child

The need is large and mostly untreated. NIDCD estimates about 28.8 million U.S. adults could benefit from hearing aids, and fewer than one in three adults 70 and older who could benefit have ever used them. Among adults 20 to 69 it’s about 16%.

The decision usually involves a spouse or an adult child who’s been repeating themselves at dinner for two years. Market to both:

  • The patient (65 to 85): Facebook, direct mail, local TV and radio, church bulletins, and the seminar circuit. They read long copy and call the number.
  • The adult child (40 to 60): Google search on their phone, YouTube, Instagram. They book online for Mom and want prices and the insurance answer before they call.

Facebook is still the workhorse for the older audience. Hearing aids aren’t a Meta special ad category the way housing is, so age targeting is available. The limits come from Meta’s health advertiser restrictions on conversion events, which can affect clinics that send health-related events from their site. Instant forms with a same-day callback hold up best. See our paid social service for how we run these.

Direct mail still works for this audience in Kingwood, Atascocita, Sugar Land, Pearland and the older sections of The Woodlands. It works best as a reminder of something specific, like a screening event date or an OTC check-up offer with a deadline, and with a tracked phone number so you can see what it actually produced.

Speed matters with both audiences. An adult child who fills out a form on her lunch break and hears back two days later has already called someone else. Seminars and community events that fill the schedule

Hearing seminars at libraries, senior centers, churches and 55+ communities are an old tactic that still books appointments in Houston. The good ones are educational: how hearing loss affects memory and conversation, what Medicare does and doesn’t cover, OTC versus prescription, how to talk to a parent who won’t admit it. The bad ones are 45 minutes of a pitch with lukewarm coffee.

What we’d do:

  • Partner with a host who already has the audience: a library branch in Spring, a Kingwood church’s senior ministry, an activities director at a senior living community. Our senior living marketing post explains what those communities care about.
  • Offer screenings at the event or on a booked follow-up date, with the terms stated up front.
  • Collect sign-ups on paper and in a tablet form, then call within two business days.
  • Track every event as its own source in the CRM so you know which venues pay off.

“Free hearing test” offers and the rules on free

The free screening is the oldest offer in hearing care, and it still works. It also attracts scrutiny because it has been abused.

the FTC’s guide on the word “free” says the conditions for getting something free must be stated clearly and conspicuously at the outset of the offer, not in an asterisk. It also says a single product or service shouldn’t carry a “free” offer in one trade area for more than six months in a 12-month period. Texas adds its own price disclosure rules from the table above, and Medicare rules come into play if you bill Medicare for the same service you’re advertising as free. Ask your compliance advisor about that last part before you run the ad.

We’d rather call it what it is. A “free hearing screening” is a short pass-fail check. A “comprehensive hearing evaluation” is a diagnostic test. Mixing them up in ads is how practices get complaints. Financing offers need the same honesty: under Regulation Z §1026.24, an ad that states the number of payments, the amount of any payment or the finance charge has to include the full terms and the APR. “Hearing aids from $49 a month” is a triggering term, so the fine print has to be there.

Google Ads for hearing aids and audiology

Search is where the adult child shows up, and where patients go when the TV is too loud for the rest of the family. The core ad groups:

  • Hearing test near me, hearing test Houston, audiologist The Woodlands, hearing exam Cypress.
  • Hearing aids near me, hearing aid prices, hearing aid clinic Sugar Land.
  • Insurance terms for plans you accept.
  • Tinnitus treatment, if you do real tinnitus management.
  • Hearing aid repair, adjustments and “help with OTC hearing aids.” Cheap clicks, grateful patients.

Google treats health conditions and disabilities as sensitive categories in personalized ads, so plan on keyword and location targeting instead of remarketing lists built from your hearing loss pages. Competing with Costco and the big-box centers

Costco is a serious hearing aid retailer. Hearing Health & Technology Matters cites analyst estimates that it may hold 12% to 15% of all U.S. hearing aid sales, with pairs running roughly $1,399 to $1,699. There are warehouses across Greater Houston, and your patients have heard the price from a neighbor.

You won’t win that comparison on price alone, and you shouldn’t try to win it by bashing a store your patients like. Compete on what a clinic does that a warehouse center generally doesn’t advertise:

  • Doctoral-level diagnostics, medical referral when something isn’t right, and tinnitus care.
  • Appointments that run as long as they need to, with the same provider each time.
  • Clear pricing, including an unbundled option where the device and the follow-up care are priced separately, so price shoppers can compare apples to apples.
  • Service for devices bought elsewhere, at a posted fee.

That last one sounds like helping the competition. In practice it fills open appointment slots and introduces you to people who’ll buy their next pair from whoever took care of them.

HIPAA, reviews and follow-up

Most audiology practices bill insurance and are HIPAA covered entities. Keep marketing tags off intake and appointment forms that collect health information unless the vendor signs a business associate agreement, per HHS’s bulletin on online tracking. Reply to reviews without confirming anyone is a patient or mentioning their hearing loss.

Ask every patient for a review after the fitting follow-up, when they can hear the grandkids again, and never offer anything for it. Google’s prohibited content policy bans incentives and selective asking; our guide to Google reviews has request templates. A printed card with a QR code works better than a text for some 80-year-olds, and a text to the adult child who booked the appointment works better than both.

Then keep them. Annual hearing checks, cleaning reminders and the four-to-five-year upgrade conversation are where a clinic’s profit lives.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more hearing aid patients in Houston?

Show up where both older adults and their adult children look. That means Google search for hearing tests and hearing aids near the clinic, Facebook and direct mail for patients 65 and older, and educational seminars at libraries, churches and senior communities. Publish a clear Medicare and Medicare Advantage page, offer help for people who bought OTC hearing aids, and ask every patient for an honest review.

Did OTC hearing aids hurt audiology practices?

OTC hearing aids added competition at the low end, but they also brought new people into the category. MarkeTrak 2025 found 70 percent of OTC owners are first-time buyers, and 85 percent of hearing aid owners prefer future purchases in person with professional help. Many clinics now offer an OTC check-up visit and treat those buyers as future patients rather than lost sales.

Does Medicare pay for hearing aids in Texas?

Original Medicare doesn’t cover hearing aids or exams for fitting them. Part B covers diagnostic hearing and balance exams when ordered, and patients can see an audiologist once every 12 months without an order for non-acute hearing loss. Many Medicare Advantage plans add hearing aid benefits, but the details vary by plan, so clinics should verify each patient’s benefits.

Can a hearing aid clinic advertise a free hearing test?

Yes, if the offer is honest. The FTC says the conditions of a free offer must be stated clearly at the outset, not in a footnote, and that the same free offer shouldn’t run more than six months a year in one area. Texas rules also require naming the product and usual price in discount ads. Describe a screening as a screening, not a full evaluation.

Who licenses hearing aid dispensers and audiologists in Texas?

The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation licenses both. Hearing instrument fitters and dispensers fall under Occupations Code Chapter 402, and audiologists are licensed under Chapter 401 through TDLR’s Speech-Language Pathologists and Audiologists program. Since 2024 Texas rules exempt certain OTC hearing aid activities, like sales, consistent with the FDA rule that states can’t require special licensing to sell OTC devices.
