Verdict first: you can still run ads, use tracking and ask for reviews as a Houston medical, dental, med spa or therapy practice. What you can’t do is let patient information slide out to Meta or Google while you do it. And since late 2022, the official line on what counts as “sliding out” has moved four times.

Most practice owners got the memo in fragments: a vendor email about “HIPAA-safe pixels,” a Meta notice about restricted events, a colleague in the Texas Medical Center who heard a court “struck down the pixel rule.” Each is partly true, which is how practices end up either afraid of all marketing or careless because they think the rules went away.

This is a rules guide. Each rule gets what it says, what it means for your website and ads, and a Houston example. It’s written for owners and office managers from the Medical Center to Market Street in The Woodlands, and for the marketing people who work for them.

Not legal advice. We’re marketers. We read the guidance, the policies and the enforcement actions closely because we have to build campaigns around them, but your situation depends on facts we can’t see. Run your setup past a healthcare attorney or your compliance officer before you change it.

Is HIPAA-compliant marketing possible in Houston? The short answer

Yes. HIPAA-compliant marketing for a Houston practice means keeping ad pixels and Google Analytics off patient portals, booking flows and intake forms; using vendors that sign a business associate agreement for anything touching patient data; writing ads that never imply the viewer has a condition; and answering reviews without confirming anyone is a patient.

The rules behind that answer come from at least seven places, including a Texas layer most national articles skip, and they don’t all apply to the same businesses.

Rule 1: The OCR tracking guidance, and the part a Texas court threw out

What it says. HHS’s Office for Civil Rights published its bulletin on tracking technologies in late 2022 and updated it in March 2024. The core sentence hasn’t changed: regulated entities are not permitted to use tracking technologies in a way that results in impermissible disclosures of PHI to tracking vendors. A vendor that receives PHI on your behalf is a business associate whether or not anyone signed a BAA. A line in your privacy policy isn’t enough, and per HHS OCR’s tracking technologies guidance, a cookie banner asking visitors to accept tracking is not a HIPAA authorization.

In July 2023 the FTC and OCR sent a joint FTC and HHS letter to about 130 hospital systems and telehealth providers naming the Meta pixel and Google Analytics specifically. That’s when a lot of Houston health systems quietly stripped tags from their sites.

What the court changed. The American Hospital Association sued in the Northern District of Texas. On June 20, 2024, the court vacated one piece of the guidance, which OCR called the “proscribed combination”: the idea that an IP address connected to a visit on a public, unauthenticated page about a specific condition or provider is enough to trigger HIPAA. OCR appealed to the Fifth Circuit, then withdrew the appeal on August 29, 2024, per Epstein Becker Green. Holland & Knight’s write-up of the ruling has more on the reasoning.

What it means in practice. Your public pages about knee replacement or Invisalign are in much safer territory than they were in 2023. Everything else in the guidance is still live. OCR still says tracking on user-authenticated pages (patient portals, telehealth logins) generally has access to PHI. Scheduling tools and symptom checkers can collect PHI. A login page is public, but the credentials typed into it are identifiable information. Mobile apps a practice offers generally collect PHI, device IDs included.

The court did nothing for the booking widget that fires a “Schedule” event to Meta with the service name and a hashed patient email in the payload. That one is still the problem.

A Houston example: a cardiology group near the Texas Medical Center has a public “AFib treatment” page with a Meta pixel, linking to online booking on the same domain. The page itself is now lower risk. The booking flow, the intake questions and the “Your AFib consult is confirmed” page are where the pixel has to stop.

Rule 2: Is the Meta Pixel HIPAA compliant?

Not on its own, and not on any page where patient information is in play. Meta’s ad tools aren’t offered as a HIPAA service with a BAA you can sign, so treat any PHI that reaches Meta through the pixel or Conversions API as a disclosure with no agreement behind it.

Meta has also built its own fence, separate from HIPAA. Starting in January 2025, Meta began restricting what health and wellness advertisers can send and optimize for, after categorizing data sources late in 2024, according to Cardinal Digital Marketing. Search Engine Land describes three tiers:

  • Core Setup. Custom parameters and the parts of URLs after the domain stop being shared, and automatic advanced matching may be unavailable.
  • Restricted standard events. Mid- and lower-funnel events like purchase and add to cart can’t be used for optimization. Landing page views, view content and custom events still work.
  • Full restriction. Meta says it “may fully restrict all events in specific regions or all regions,” which leaves you with targeting and upper-funnel optimization only.

Check yours in Events Manager, under the data source’s Settings tab. If Meta miscategorized you, you can request a review.

Two things practices miss. First, Meta’s categories don’t follow HIPAA’s. A cash-pay med spa in The Woodlands that isn’t a HIPAA covered entity can still land in a restricted tier. Marketing Brew reported on a memo suggesting medical Botox (for migraines) could be restricted while cosmetic Botox might not be. Second, lead ads that run entirely inside Facebook and Instagram appear unaffected so far, per Cardinal, because the form data is generated within Meta. That makes instant forms the most workable conversion path for many Houston practices right now. Keep the questions to name, phone, email and preferred location; a dropdown asking which condition brings them in is the wrong instinct.

Then there’s the creative. Meta’s personal attributes standard bans ads that assert or imply personal attributes, physical and mental health included. Meta’s own examples: “Do you have diabetes?” and “Depression getting you down? Get help now.” For a therapy practice in Sugar Land, “Anxiety therapy in Sugar Land, evening appointments, most plans accepted” is fine. “Is anxiety running your life?” gets rejected, and it should.

Rule 3: Can a medical practice use Google Analytics and Google Ads?

Yes, with limits on both. Start with Analytics. Google’s own HIPAA page for Analytics says Google doesn’t offer a BAA for it, that authenticated pages are likely HIPAA-covered and shouldn’t carry GA tags, and that HIPAA-regulated customers must not use GA in any way that gives Google access to PHI. That’s Google telling you where the line is, in writing.

In practice: GA4 on public pages, never on the portal, the booking steps or a confirmation URL that names a service. If your booking tool appends ?service=botox&provider=dr-lee to the URL, that string goes to Google with every page view.

Now Google Ads. Keyword-targeted search is the cleanest channel in healthcare: “pediatric dentist The Woodlands” tells you what the person wants without you needing to know who they are.

The restriction is on audiences. Google’s health in personalized advertising policy says advertiser-curated audiences (Customer Match, your data segments, lookalike segments and audience expansion) can’t be used on ads promoting sensitive health content, which Google gives examples of: STD treatment, mental health counseling for depression, anxiety and addiction, pregnancy and infertility treatment. Predefined Google audiences and location targeting are still allowed. Uploading your patient list to Customer Match is a bad idea under HIPAA anyway, since a list of your patients is itself PHI.

For conversion tracking, count actions without sending patient detail. A call from the ad, a click on the “Book” button, a generic “appointment request started” event. We cover the mechanics in our guide to tracking forms and calls in GA4 and Google Ads; for healthcare, strip anything clinical from the event names and parameters before you follow it.

Rule 4: Server-side tracking fixes the plumbing, not the law

Server-side tracking is the most oversold product in healthcare marketing right now. Here’s what it actually does.

With normal tags, the visitor’s browser talks straight to Meta and Google. With a server-side setup, the browser talks to a server you control, and that server decides what to forward. That’s useful, because you can strip IP addresses, drop URL paths, rename events and refuse to forward anything from the booking flow.

What it doesn’t do is change what counts as a disclosure. If your server forwards “appointment booked, cardiology, this hashed email” to Meta, you disclosed PHI to Meta, just with extra steps. OCR’s guidance points to the legitimate version: a customer data platform or tracking vendor that signs a BAA with you, receives the data, and de-identifies it before anything goes to an ad platform. OCR also notes that a vendor de-identifying data after receiving it doesn’t cure a disclosure that had no BAA behind it.

Our rule for Houston practices is simple. If a vendor sells you “HIPAA-compliant tracking,” ask three things: will they sign a BAA, exactly which fields leave their server for Meta and Google, and can they show you the outbound payload. If any answer is vague, keep shopping.

A cheaper path works for many small practices: no ad pixels past the homepage and service pages, Meta lead ads for social, call tracking under a BAA, and keyword-targeted search. You lose some optimization and a whole category of risk. For a three-provider dental office in Spring, that trade usually makes sense.

Rule 5: Texas HB 300 is stricter than HIPAA in ways marketers feel

Texas passed HB 300 in 2011, amending Chapter 181 of the Health and Safety Code (the Texas Medical Records Privacy Act). It sits on top of HIPAA. Three parts matter for marketing.

The covered entity definition is broad. The Texas Optometry Board’s HB 300 summary calls it “very broad.” It reaches anyone who assembles, stores, or comes into possession of Texas residents’ PHI, which can include billing companies, IT vendors and marketing agencies, plus practices that never bill insurance.

Marketing needs authorization. Under Section 181.152, a covered entity generally needs a patient’s written or electronic authorization before using or disclosing PHI for marketing, with narrow exceptions (face-to-face communication, nominal promotional gifts, communication the patient orally requested). Marketing sent by mail must go in an envelope showing only names and addresses, identify the sender, include a toll-free number, and explain how to get off the list. Removal requests must be honored within 45 days.

Electronic disclosure needs notice and authorization. Section 181.154 requires notice to patients whose PHI is subject to electronic disclosure, and generally a separate authorization for each electronic disclosure outside treatment, payment, operations and a few other exceptions.

Add the training requirement (staff trained within 90 days of hire and again when the law changes their duties) and the penalties. HIPAA Journal lists civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation for negligence, $25,000 for knowing or intentional violations, and $250,000 for intentional violations for financial gain, with up to $1.5 million a year for a pattern. The Texas Attorney General enforces, and licensing boards can discipline on top.

What we’d do about it: add a plain-language marketing consent checkbox to new-patient intake (unchecked by default), keep a record of it in the practice management system, and send promotional email or texts only to patients who checked it. Whether a particular appointment-reminder text or a recall email counts as “marketing” under Texas law is a question for your attorney. Getting consent up front makes most of those questions go away.

Does HIPAA apply to a med spa or a cash-pay clinic?

Sometimes not, and people get excited about that too early. Under HHS’s covered entity definition, a health care provider is a HIPAA covered entity only if it transmits health information electronically in connection with standard transactions, which mostly means billing insurance. A cash-only med spa in The Woodlands or an IV hydration lounge in Katy may fall outside HIPAA.

It doesn’t fall outside everything else:

  • Texas Chapter 181’s broader definition can still reach it.
  • The FTC Act applies. BetterHelp wasn’t a HIPAA entity either, and the FTC’s BetterHelp order required it to pay $7.8 million after it shared email addresses, IP addresses and intake questionnaire answers with Facebook and others for advertising, despite promising privacy.
  • The Texas Data Privacy and Security Act took effect July 1, 2024. It exempts HIPAA-regulated organizations and most small businesses, but per the Texas State Law Library, “health condition” is sensitive data and even a small business needs consent to sell it.
  • Meta’s restrictions apply based on Meta’s categories, not your legal status.

So a med spa should run the same playbook as a dental office, with slightly more freedom on creative. Before-and-after photos need written patient consent regardless; our med spa marketing guide covers the rest of the channel mix.

Rule 6: Can a dentist or doctor respond to Google reviews?

Yes, as long as the reply never confirms that the reviewer is a patient or mentions anything about their care. This is the rule practices break most often, usually at 10 p.m., right after reading a one-star review that isn’t fair.

OCR has settled at least two review cases. Elite Dental Associates in Dallas paid $10,000 in 2019 after responding to Yelp reviews with patient information. In 2023, Manasa Health Center, a New Jersey psychiatric practice, paid $30,000 after a reply to a negative review included the patient’s mental health diagnosis and treatment, and OCR found three more patients’ information in other replies. OCR’s director put it bluntly: “Simply put, this is not allowed.”

A reply that works for almost any review, good or bad:

“Thank you for taking the time to write this. Our privacy obligations mean we can’t discuss anyone’s care online, but we take every concern seriously. Please call our office manager at [your number] and we’ll talk it through.”

For a five-star review, resist “So glad your implant healed well, Maria!” That sentence confirms she’s a patient and names her treatment. “Thank you, we really appreciate this” is plenty.

Asking for reviews is fine. Keep the request generic (“Thanks for visiting us today, would you share a quick review?”) and send it only to patients who agreed to receive texts. Don’t offer anything in exchange; the FTC’s review rule covers that, and our post on the fake reviews rule explains it. The rest of the review process is in our complete guide to Google reviews.

What’s still safe for Houston and The Woodlands practices

After all those rules, here’s what we’d happily run tomorrow for a practice anywhere from the Medical Center to Conroe.

  • Medical. A specialist group with offices near the Texas Medical Center and in The Woodlands runs keyword search campaigns per location (“orthopedic surgeon The Woodlands,” “sports medicine near Hughes Landing”), with call tracking and a booking button tracked as a click. The Texas Medical Center reports about 10 million patient encounters a year, so a lot of north-side patients search for a closer option first.
  • Dental. A Woodlands family dental office runs Google search, Business Profile posts and three-field Meta lead ads for new-patient exams, with no pixel on the booking widget.
  • Med spa. A Market Street area med spa runs Meta lead ads and Instagram creative showing the space, the injectors and pricing, with consented before-and-afters on the website only. Retargeting limited to video viewers and page engagers on Meta.
  • Therapy. A group practice in Sugar Land and the Heights runs search ads on service plus location, writes ads about format and availability (telehealth, evenings, insurance) and never about the viewer’s state of mind. Website analytics on public pages only.

SEO is the quiet winner here. A strong service page for “TMJ treatment Spring TX” doesn’t require any tracking of who reads it. Our web design and SEO team builds those, and the medical practice marketing guide, dental guide and therapist guide cover each specialty’s channel mix.

A 30-day cleanup plan for your practice website and ads

If you’ve read this far and suspect your site is leaking, this is the order we’d fix it in.

The inventory is where the surprises are. Run a tag scanner (or your browser’s developer tools) on the homepage, a service page, the booking flow and the confirmation page. Most practices find tags nobody remembers: an old pixel from a previous agency, a chat widget, a heatmap tool recording form fields. Vendors that touch PHI usually include the booking tool, call tracking, the CRM and the email platform, so those are the BAAs to chase first.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to use Facebook ads for a medical practice in Houston?

Yes. Facebook and Instagram ads are legal for medical practices, but the ads can’t imply the viewer has a condition, and the Meta pixel shouldn’t fire on booking, intake or portal pages where patient information is present. Many Houston practices now rely on Meta lead ads, which keep form data inside Meta. Check Events Manager to see whether Meta has placed your data source in a restricted health category.

Did the 2024 court ruling make tracking pixels HIPAA compliant?

No. The June 2024 ruling in AHA v. Becerra vacated only the part of OCR’s guidance that treated an IP address plus a visit to a public condition page as protected health information. OCR still says tracking on patient portals, scheduling tools, symptom checkers and practice apps can disclose PHI, and a vendor receiving PHI needs a business associate agreement or patient authorization.

Does a med spa in The Woodlands have to follow HIPAA?

It depends on billing. A provider is a HIPAA covered entity if it conducts standard electronic transactions, mostly insurance billing, so some cash-only med spas fall outside HIPAA. They still face Texas Chapter 181, which defines covered entities more broadly, plus the FTC Act and Meta’s own health data restrictions. Ask a healthcare attorney to confirm your status before relaxing anything.

What should a Texas dental office say when replying to a negative Google review?

Say nothing that confirms the reviewer is a patient or describes their care. A safe reply thanks them, says privacy rules prevent discussing anyone’s care online, and invites them to call the office manager directly. A Dallas dental practice paid OCR $10,000 in 2019 over review responses, so write a standard script and make sure everyone who can reply uses it.

Is Google Analytics 4 allowed on a healthcare website in Texas?

On public pages, generally yes, with care. Google says it won’t sign a business associate agreement for Analytics and tells HIPAA-regulated customers to keep tags off authenticated pages and avoid any use that gives Google access to PHI. Keep GA4 off portals, booking steps and confirmation pages, and keep service names and patient details out of URLs and event parameters.
