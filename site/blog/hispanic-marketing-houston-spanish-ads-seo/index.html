Tuesday, 7:15 p.m., somewhere off Airline Drive. A water heater quits. Someone types “plomero cerca de mi,” taps the first ad (which is in Spanish), and lands on an English homepage with a little Translate dropdown in the corner. She calls anyway. The phone menu offers Spanish as option four, after billing.

She hangs up and calls the next company.

That’s how most Hispanic marketing in Houston fails. The ad was fine. Everything after the click wasn’t.

Spanish-language marketing works when Spanish gets treated as its own channel. That means Spanish keywords and ads, Spanish pages at their own URLs, a Google Business Profile a Spanish speaker can actually read, and somebody who picks up the phone and answers in Spanish. Owners almost always ask us about the ads first. We’d start with the phone and work backward.

Below is how each piece works in 2026, including a September Google Ads change most Spanish campaigns haven’t caught up with.

How many Houston customers speak Spanish at home?

More than most owners guess.

According to Census QuickFacts, 46.1% of Harris County residents are Hispanic or Latino, and 45.0% of residents age 5 and up speak a language other than English at home. Montgomery County, where we’re based, is 30.2% Hispanic per the same Census source. Inside Houston city limits, the City of Houston Planning table built on the 2020-2024 American Community Survey counts 808,128 residents age 5 and older who speak Spanish at home, or 37%. For the whole metro, the Greater Houston Partnership put it at 29.5% speaking Spanish at home.

The trap is assuming Hispanic and Spanish-dominant mean the same audience. They don’t. In its 2023 report, Pew Research Center found that 75% of U.S. Latinos say they can carry on a conversation in Spanish pretty well or very well. Among third-generation-or-later Latinos, though, 65% say they can’t. And 72% of second-generation Latinos say they use Spanglish at least sometimes.

A customer in Cypress whose grandparents came up from Monterrey is probably reached just fine by your English campaigns. A family that landed in Gulfton three years ago needs Spanish from the ad all the way to the invoice. We plan by language. Never by last name.

To size it for your shop, pull your last 200 calls or CRM records and count who asked for Spanish. Around 5% calls for a solid Spanish page and one bilingual person on the phone. Closer to 30%, you want a full Spanish program.

Spanish Google Ads after the September 2026 language change

Until this fall, every Search campaign had a language setting, and the standard advice was to build a Spanish campaign and set it to Spanish. Google’s language targeting help page says it judges which languages someone understands from the search term, their language settings and preferences.

In September 2026 the campaign-level language setting for Search went away. Per the same page, “Search ads will automatically match based on the language of your ads,” and Google prioritizes ads that match the language of the search term. In Performance Max, the setting no longer applies on the Search Network, though it still governs YouTube, Display, Discover and Gmail. TechWyse points out a gap: Google hasn’t announced reporting that shows which language an ad actually served in.

What we’re doing in our own accounts:

  • Spanish still gets its own campaign. Targeting used to be the reason. Now it’s budget and reporting: when Spanish and English ad groups share a budget, one starves the other, and you can’t tell which paid off.
  • Ads and landing pages are written in Spanish, since those are now the main signals Google reads. A Spanish ad pointing at an English page is a mismatch Google can see.
  • Someone reads the auto-generated text. If AI Max or automatically created assets are on, check what Google wrote; TechWyse flags that generated creative in one language can serve to a searcher using another.
  • Location goes to “Presence.” Google’s advanced location options default to “Presence or Interest,” which pulls in people who are merely curious about Houston.

Translation vs transcreation: why word-for-word Spanish ads flop

Translation swaps the words. Transcreation rewrites the message so it hits a Spanish speaker the way the English version hits an English speaker, and for ads it’s the only version that pulls its weight.

We fix the same things over and over. A literal “free estimates” is grammatically fine, but plenty of Houston customers say “cotización gratis.” “Licensed and insured” should name the Texas license, since someone calling a stranger in their second language leans harder on proof. Register trips people up too. “Usted” is polite and safe for most service businesses; “tú” suits a gym or a taquería with a younger crowd. Pick one and stick with it everywhere.

Spanish runs longer than English. Write Spanish headlines to the 30-character limit from scratch rather than cramming a translation in.

And Houston isn’t one dialect. It’s heavily Mexican-heritage, with big Central American communities on top of that, so plain neutral Spanish with a few Texas terms is the safe middle. Before anything goes live we’d have a native speaker who lives here read every ad, page and phone script. Machine translation is a perfectly good first draft (we use it). We’ve never seen it survive as the last one.

Bilingual website SEO: /es/ pages, hreflang and the translate widget

If your Spanish plan is a Google Translate dropdown, you don’t have Spanish pages as far as search is concerned. The widget translates in the visitor’s browser; there’s no Spanish URL for Google to crawl, rank or send an ad to.

Google’s guidance on multilingual sites recommends “using different URLs for each language version of a page” rather than switching language with cookies or browser settings. The same page tells you to skip automatic redirects based on a guessed language, link the language versions to each other, and keep each page in one language instead of running translations side by side.

For a small business site, that works out like this.

  • Put Spanish pages in a folder, like /es/plomeria/ next to /plumbing/. It’s the simplest setup to manage and to point ads at.
  • Add hreflang so each English page names its Spanish twin and the reverse (“en-us” and “es-us”, plus an x-default). Each page lists both versions, itself included, or Google ignores the pair.
  • Put a plain “Español” link in the header and skip auto-redirects; a bilingual customer with an English phone may still want the Spanish page.
  • Translate the title tag, meta description, alt text, form labels, error messages and the thank-you page. Everybody forgets the thank-you page.

You don’t need the whole site in Spanish on day one. Start where the money is, which for most shops means the top few services, the contact page and a page per service area. A Spanish page for Pasadena or Channelview will out-earn a Spanish “About us” every time. Our post on local landing pages covers the structure, and our web design and SEO team builds bilingual sites this way.

Google Business Profile in Spanish: what’s possible and what isn’t

There’s no Spanish toggle in Google Business Profile, and a few of the obvious workarounds will get you in trouble.

Google’s guidelines for representing your business rule out “repeating the same business name in multiple scripts or languages,” and they allow one profile per location. So “Rodriguez Plumbing / Plomería Rodriguez” is out as a name, and a second Spanish profile at the same address is a duplicate. If your sign and website genuinely say both, talk to someone before touching the name field.

What you can do:

  • Write a bilingual description. You get 750 characters, so lead with your primary language and follow with a short Spanish version.
  • Publish Spanish posts as their own posts, linking to the Spanish page. Nothing translates them for you.
  • Ask Spanish-speaking customers for reviews in Spanish (send the request by text, also in Spanish), and reply in whatever language the review came in.
  • Check for language attributes under Edit profile. Some categories now let you list languages your staff speak, and in a Local Search Forum thread a GBP specialist called them a cleaner fix than splitting the profile.
  • Photograph your Spanish signage, if you really have some. “Se habla español” on the front door does more than you’d think.

For phones set to Spanish, Google translates standardized fields like categories and hours itself. Free-text fields stay as you typed them, hence the Spanish paragraph in the description. For the rest of the profile, our Google Business Profile guide covers the basics.

Facebook and Instagram ads in Spanish

Meta used to offer a “Hispanic Affinity” targeting option. Bloomberg reported that Facebook retired it in August 2020. Good riddance, frankly; ethnicity was the wrong proxy for the Pew reasons above.

Language and place do the job better. The ad set’s audience controls include a Languages field that almost everyone leaves blank. For a Spanish campaign, set it to Spanish (All) and run Spanish creative. Meta also supports multi-language ads, where one ad carries several language versions and each viewer sees the one matching their settings. We still prefer separate ad sets for an ongoing program, for clean budgets and reporting.

On Meta, though, the creative decides most of it.

  • Shoot your own staff and trucks on actual Houston streets. A stock “Hispanic family” in a beige kitchen reads as fake to everybody, Hispanic or not.
  • Have the bilingual tech or front-desk person talk to the camera in Spanish. A 30-second phone video usually beats the polished one.
  • Build the instant form in Spanish, down to the thank-you screen, and make sure whoever calls the lead back speaks Spanish.
  • Caption everything. Lots of people scroll with the sound off.

Ads in Meta’s special ad categories (housing, employment, credit or financial products) get even tighter targeting limits, so language and creative carry more weight. Our paid social team handles those reviews.

Spanish-language radio, TV and streaming in Houston

Houston has a deep Spanish-language media market. For some businesses it’s the one place in this guide where broad reach is still cheap enough to test.

  • Univision 45 (KXLN), licensed to Rosenberg and owned by TelevisaUnivision, carries UniMás on its second channel.
  • Telemundo Houston (KTMD, channel 47) belongs to NBCUniversal’s Telemundo Station Group and is licensed to Galveston.
  • Que Buena 102.9 (KLTN) plays Regional Mexican under TelevisaUnivision’s Uforia group.
  • Amor 106.5 (KOVE-FM) is Spanish adult contemporary, also Uforia, also licensed to Galveston.

Spanish streaming, TelevisaUnivision’s ViX for one, is reachable through programmatic connected TV.

We’d hold off on radio until Spanish search, Spanish pages and the phones work. Radio makes people search for you, and if that search lands on an English-only site, you’ve paid for a competitor’s lead. After that, a two-month test with its own tracking number is fair. Answering the phone in Spanish (where most campaigns break)

This is the part most shops get wrong, and it’s the cheapest one to fix.

A Spanish-speaking caller who hits an English greeting, a buried menu option or an English-only voicemail does not leave a message. They call the next ad.

For a shop with one or two bilingual people on payroll, we’d set it up roughly like this.

  1. Give the Spanish campaigns their own tracking number. You’ll know how many Spanish calls the ads produced.
  2. Route that number straight to the bilingual staff first, then to the main line. No menu.
  3. Record a short bilingual greeting and voicemail on every line, with Spanish in the first five seconds.
  4. Put the Spanish hours in the ads (“Hablamos español, lunes a sábado”). No Spanish on Sundays? Say so.
  5. Send missed-call texts in Spanish from the Spanish number.
  6. Once a month, listen to a handful of Spanish calls. Did someone answer, did the job get booked, and did the caller understand the price?

The tools are covered in our call tracking guide. For what it’s worth, a part-time bilingual dispatcher is cheaper than most leaky Spanish ad budgets we’ve audited.

Cultural do’s and don’ts that don’t rely on stereotypes

Most bad Hispanic marketing is lazy rather than malicious. Papel picado clip art, a mariachi stock photo, a “¡Feliz Cinco de Mayo!” post from a company that ignores Spanish the other 364 days.

A steady Spanish presence all year beats a burst during Hispanic Heritage Month (September 15 to October 15). People remember who was around in February.

Match the language the customer chose, every time. If someone filled out the English form, follow up in English. Switching to Spanish because of a last name is exactly the assumption that grates on bilingual customers, and it can sting: Pew found that 54% of Latinos who speak little or no Spanish say another Latino has made them feel bad about it. Your follow-up email shouldn’t pile on.

Spell out price, warranty, financing and deposits in plain terms; nobody of any background likes surprises on an invoice. Where you can, feature real Spanish-speaking customers (with permission) in their own words.

Humor and slang stay out of the ads unless a native speaker from here wrote them.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use Google Translate on my website for Spanish customers?

Not as your Spanish strategy. A translate widget converts text in the visitor’s browser, so there is no Spanish URL for Google to index or for an ad to link to, and the wording is often clumsy. Google recommends separate URLs per language. Build real Spanish pages for your main services and contact page, have a native speaker review them, and pair them with hreflang.

Do Spanish Google Ads cost less than English ads in Houston?

Often, though not always. Fewer advertisers bid on Spanish keywords in many service categories, so clicks tend to cost less, but there is also less search volume. Prices vary by trade and zip code, so check Keyword Planner for your own terms. What happens after the click matters more. A Spanish ad that leads to an English page or an English-only phone line throws the savings away.

Can I have a Google Business Profile in Spanish and English?

Not as two profiles for one location. Google allows one profile per location and bans repeating your business name in multiple languages. You can still write a bilingual description, publish Spanish posts, collect and answer Spanish reviews, show Spanish signage in photos, and use language attributes if your category has them. Google translates standardized fields like categories and hours on its own.

How do I target Hispanic customers on Facebook?

Target by language and location, not ethnicity. Meta removed its Hispanic Affinity option in 2020. In the ad set audience settings, set Languages to Spanish for Spanish campaigns and pair it with Spanish creative, Spanish instant forms and a Spanish-speaking follow-up. Many Hispanic customers in Houston prefer English, so your English campaigns should already reach them with relevant, inclusive creative.

What is the difference between translation and transcreation?

Translation converts the words from one language to another. Transcreation rewrites the message for the audience so it carries the same meaning, tone and persuasion, which can mean different headlines, examples or local terms. For ads and landing pages, transcreation by a native speaker who knows Houston usually performs better than a literal translation, which can sound stiff or miss common Texas Spanish words.
