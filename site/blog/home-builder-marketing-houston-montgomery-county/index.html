Saturday at the model home off FM 1488. Eleven groups walked through. Your sales counselor thinks three of them mentioned seeing “an ad,” one came with an agent from Katy, and the rest “just drove by.” Monday, your marketing person shows you a Meta report with 214 leads for the month.

Nobody in the building can connect those two numbers. That’s the real problem in most home builder marketing.

Home builder marketing that works in Houston and Montgomery County gets your communities and quick move-in homes in front of buyers already searching a specific area, then stays in front of them for the six to twelve months they take to decide. Small builders can win that part by being sharper than the big guys (louder is off the table anyway). Where almost everyone leaks money is the tracking: who actually walked the model, and who signed.

This playbook is for custom, semi-custom and small production builders with one to maybe eight active communities.

Why marketing a home builder is different from marketing a contractor

A roofer’s customer decides in a week. Your buyer might take a year, and most of that year they’re not ready to talk to you.

Before a family signs on a new build, a lot has to line up: the area (often a school district first), a lot or plan they like (for custom work, maybe land they already own near Lake Conroe), financing that often depends on selling their current house, and enough trust in you to hand over the biggest check of their lives.

So a lead from a Facebook form in March is rarely a sale in April. Fine, as long as nobody in the company judges campaigns by 30-day results.

Every campaign needs a “not yet” path. Usually that’s an email sequence and a retargeting audience, plus some reason to come back, like a plan release, a phase opening or an incentive deadline. Judge the whole thing on appointments and model visits. Raw lead counts will lie to you for months.

The Houston new-home market you’re selling into right now

At a Greater Houston Builders Association luncheon in June 2025, Zonda’s chief economist said quick move-in homes were averaging 3 to 3.2 per community in Houston against a national average of 2.3, days on market had risen to 44, and new-home prices were about 5% below their peak while still roughly 40% above 2019. Rate buydowns and closing-cost help were everywhere. That’s per Community Impact’s write-up of Zonda’s Houston briefing. With that much finished inventory sitting longer, your quick move-in page and the ad budget behind it matter a lot more than they did in 2021.

The master-planned communities set the tone. RCLCO’s 2025 list of the nation’s top-selling MPCs had Sunterra in Katy at No. 5 with 1,024 sales and Tamarron at No. 9 with 974, plus Anniston, The Grand Prairie, Meridiana, Sienna, Elyson, Lago Mar and Jordan Ranch further down, according to Houston Agent Magazine’s summary of RCLCO’s 2025 rankings. Bridgeland in Cypress keeps showing up in those rankings too.

North of Houston, the growth is along I-45 and SH 242. Community Impact profiled Grand Central Park (Johnson Development, at I-45 and Loop 336), The Woodlands Hills (Howard Hughes), Artavia (planned for more than 6,500 homes), Evergreen from Shea Homes at SH 242 and FM 1314, and Cielo off Airport Road. Add Woodforest in Montgomery and the smaller subdivisions around Magnolia and Willis, and a buyer has a lot of choices within a 20-minute drive.

For custom builders there’s better news. NAHB counted 186,000 custom home starts nationally in 2025, up 3% while total single-family starts fell more than 6%, and custom work was almost 20% of single-family starts, per NAHB’s Eye on Housing. Buyers with land and equity kept building.

You can’t outspend a 3,000-acre MPC’s lifestyle campaign, and we wouldn’t try. What you can do is pick up the phone on Saturday and put real prices and lot premiums online, next to a quick move-in page that’s accurate as of this morning. Fewer builders manage that than you’d guess.

Community pages, plan pages and quick move-in pages: the builder website that sells

Most builder websites we look at are gorgeous and nearly useless for search. A hero video, community pages full of lifestyle copy, and no price anywhere.

Page typeWhat the buyer is askingWhat the page must show
Community page (one per community)“Is this area right for us, and can we afford it?”Price range “from,” lot sizes, school district, HOA dues, MUD or PID tax rate, model address and hours, drive times to I-45, the Grand Parkway or the Hardy Toll Road
Plan page (one per plan)“Does this layout work?”Square footage, beds/baths, base price, which communities offer it, floor plan image, video walkthrough, options with typical upgrade costs
Quick move-in page (one per home)“What can we close on soon?”Address or lot, price, completion date, current incentive with its end date, photos of this house, a “schedule a tour” button
Build-on-your-lot page“Will you build on our land in Montgomery County?”Areas served, minimum lot requirements, septic/well experience, process and timeline, starting price per square foot range
Area pages (Conroe, Magnolia, Tomball, Willis)“New homes in Magnolia under $500K”Your communities and inventory in that town, with links down to each

The MUD tax rate line is a Houston-specific one. Out-of-state buyers from California or the Northeast get blindsided when a MUD tax stacks on top of county, city and school district rates, and builders who explain it up front lose fewer contracts at the option period. We’d rather a buyer learn it from you on page one than from their lender in week three.

Quick move-in pages need to come down (or switch to “Sold”) the day the home goes under contract. Stale inventory burns a lead’s trust and your ad spend at the same time. And each community page should carry the model’s exact address and hours, because “new homes near me” searches pull from Google Maps, and your model or sales office listing is how you show up there. Our team builds these sites under web design and SEO, and the local landing pages guide goes deeper on area pages.

Google Ads for “new homes in [community]” searches

Search is where buyers tell you exactly what they want. “New homes in Grand Central Park.” “Quick move in homes Conroe.” “Custom home builder Magnolia TX.” “Build on your own lot Montgomery County.” Each deserves its own ad group and a landing page from the table above.

How we’d structure it for a builder with three communities:

  • One campaign per community, with keywords like the community name plus “new homes,” “homes for sale” and the builder names you compete with there. Send that traffic to the community’s page, never the homepage.
  • An area campaign for searches like “new construction homes Conroe TX,” “new homes Magnolia” and “new build homes Montgomery TX,” landing on the area page that lists everything you have in that town.
  • Quick move-in. “Move in ready homes,” “quick move in homes,” “spec homes” plus town names. When inventory is high this is often the best-converting campaign you’ll run, and it pays to push it hardest near month-end and quarter-end incentive deadlines.
  • Custom or build-on-your-lot: “custom home builder [town],” “build on your lot,” and “barndominium builder” only if you actually build them.
  • Brand. It’s cheap, and it stops a competitor or a listing portal from sitting on top of your own name.

Build the negative list on day one. Rent, rental, apartments, “for lease,” jobs, careers, “superintendent,” “floor plans free,” manufactured, mobile, modular (unless you sell them), foreclosure and HUD homes.

Now the policy part. Housing ads in the U.S. fall under Google’s personalized advertising restrictions. You can’t exclude by age, gender or parental status, you can’t target marital status or life events like “recently married,” and ZIP code targeting has to be replaced with city, county or radius targeting, per Google’s housing, employment and credit policy FAQ. The rule applies to Search, Display and video. In practice that shifts the targeting work onto keywords and locations, where the intent lives anyway.

If you build inside someone else’s MPC, bid on the community name; just keep other companies’ trademarks out of your ad text. Our search marketing team sets these up, and our Houston CPC benchmarks will give you a feel for click costs before you set a budget.

Facebook and Instagram ads for builders: living with the Housing special ad category

Every Meta ad for a home for sale has to run under the Housing special ad category. Builders who skip the declaration get ads rejected, and repeat offenders risk the ad account.

The restrictions, as summarized in Jon Loomer’s guide to Meta’s special ad categories: age is locked to 18 to 65+, gender is locked to all, location targeting must include at least a 15-mile radius around any point you choose, there’s no ZIP code targeting, many detailed targeting options disappear and you can’t exclude any, and lookalike audiences aren’t available. Special Ad Audiences, the old workaround, were discontinued in 2022.

So on Meta, the creative does the targeting. That’s the part most builders get wrong. A pretty drone shot of a community pool reaches everyone within 15 miles, and everyone ignores it. An ad that says “4-bed, 2,450 sq ft, Conroe ISD, from the low $400s, move in by March” finds the right people because the wrong people scroll past.

What tends to work for small builders:

  • Short walkthrough videos of a specific quick move-in home, filmed on a phone, with the price and completion date on screen.
  • Plan-release and phase-opening announcements with a real date.
  • Instant forms with two or three qualifying questions (timeline, whether they own a home they need to sell, whether they own land). Higher-intent questions mean fewer leads and better ones.
  • Retargeting website visitors and video viewers.

We’d budget Meta as the warm-up and the reminder, and Google as the closer. If you only have $3,000 a month, the bulk of it belongs on search and quick move-in campaigns. See our paid social service if you’d rather hand this part off.

Fair Housing Act rules for builder ads, photos included

The ad platforms enforce their rules with targeting restrictions. Federal law goes further and covers what your ad says and shows, everywhere.

Under 24 CFR 100.75, discriminatory advertising includes using “words, phrases, photographs, illustrations, symbols or forms” that suggest a home is or isn’t available to people because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status or national origin. It also includes “selecting media or locations for advertising” that deny segments of the market information about housing. Texas has its own Fair Housing Act with the same protected classes, so there’s no relief at the state level.

For builders, the traps are usually small and accidental:

Describe the home and the location, and leave the buyer you’re imagining out of the copy. “Walk to the elementary school” describes a fact. “Perfect for young families” describes people, and familial status is protected. If you photograph people for your marketing, use a range of real-looking buyers across all your materials (the same young couple and golden retriever in every shot is its own problem). Put the Equal Housing Opportunity logo or statement on your site, brochures and print ads. And if you sell an active-adult or 55+ section, have your attorney confirm it qualifies for the housing-for-older-persons exemption before your ads say “adult community.”

If a phrase makes you hesitate, cut it. We’re marketers, and that’s the cheapest legal advice we’ve got.

Realtor co-op and broker outreach still matter

A big share of new-home buyers in Houston show up with an agent. Ignoring agents doesn’t save you the commission. It just sends their clients to the builder down the road who treats agents well.

Since the NAR settlement practice changes took effect in August 2024, offers of compensation no longer go on the MLS, and buyers sign written agreements with their agents before touring. Builders still decide their own co-op policy, and agents now ask about it up front. Put yours where agents can find it: a broker page on your website with the co-op terms, the registration rule (most builders require the agent to register the client on the first visit), and a downloadable inventory sheet.

The old-fashioned stuff still works in The Woodlands and Conroe. A quarterly broker lunch at the model still fills chairs. So does a monthly agent email with updated quick move-ins and incentives. Mostly it comes down to knowing, by name, the agents who actually sell your area on HAR. Our post on how local agents market themselves is worth reading to see the world from their side.

Zillow New Construction, NewHomeSource and the listing sites

Plenty of buyers browse the portals before they ever search a builder name. Two matter most for new construction.

Zillow New Construction. Builders can send community and plan data through Zillow’s new home feed, and paid Promoted Communities placement puts your communities in front of shoppers in your area. Per Zillow’s Listing Connect page, spec homes need to be listed in an MLS to appear alongside agent listings, and Listing Connect ties those MLS listings back to your community so leads still route to you. Pricing is negotiated, so get a quote and treat it like any other ad buy.

NewHomeSource. The new-construction-only portal run by BDX, which Zonda bought BDX in February 2024. Buyers on it are by definition shopping new, which tends to mean better lead quality and fewer leads.

We wouldn’t pay for both until you can measure one. Run the portal your buyers mention most for a full quarter and judge it on cost per appointment. Cost per lead flatters portals, because their leads are often early-stage and only turn into anything if your follow-up is good.

Tracking model home visits back to the ad

Back to the Saturday-versus-Monday problem. You want every model visit and every contract tied to the first and last place that buyer came from.

A simple version any builder can run:

  1. Give each channel its own tracked phone number (Google Ads, Meta, Zillow, NewHomeSource, signs, website) and record calls. Our call tracking guide covers setup.
  2. Put an appointment scheduler on every community and quick move-in page. A booked tour is the best mid-funnel signal you’ll get.
  3. Register every model visitor on a tablet with name, email, phone, how they heard about you, and whether they have an agent.
  4. Keep every lead and visitor in one CRM with the original source attached. Match walk-ins to earlier online leads by email or phone.
  5. Send appointments, visits and contracts back to Google Ads and Meta as offline conversions, so the platforms learn which clicks turn into buyers.

Below is an illustrative monthly funnel for a small builder with two active communities. The numbers aren’t a benchmark, just a picture of what to count and where it lives.

On spend, plenty of small builders put somewhere around 1% to 2% of revenue into marketing, with more during a community launch. Treat that loosely. The better number is marketing cost per closing, which you can only see once the funnel above is wired up.

Frequently asked questions

How do home builders get leads?

Most builder leads come from Google searches for a community or town plus new homes, listing portals like Zillow and NewHomeSource, Facebook and Instagram ads, realtor referrals, and drive-by traffic at the model. The best-performing source is usually search traffic landing on an accurate community or quick move-in page. Track every lead to a model visit and a contract so you know which source actually sells homes.

How much should a home builder spend on marketing?

Many small builders spend somewhere around 1% to 2% of revenue on marketing, with extra during a community or phase launch. Treat that as a starting point. A better method is to work backward from your closing goal and your measured cost per closing by channel, which only works once appointments, visits and contracts are tracked to their source.

Can I target by age or ZIP code in Facebook ads for new homes?

No. Meta requires new-home ads to run under the Housing special ad category, which locks age to 18 to 65+, includes all genders, removes ZIP code targeting and requires at least a 15-mile radius. Lookalike audiences are unavailable and detailed targeting is limited. Google has similar housing restrictions on age, gender, parental status and ZIP codes. Write ads specific enough that the right buyers recognize themselves.

What is the best way to market a custom home builder?

Show finished work and real numbers. A custom builder needs a build-on-your-lot page with areas served and a price-per-square-foot range, a portfolio organized by style and size, strong Google reviews, and search ads for phrases like custom home builder plus a town name. Meta works well for video walkthroughs of finished homes. Respond fast, because custom buyers interview several builders.

Should a small builder list homes on Zillow and NewHomeSource?

Usually yes, but test one at a time. Zillow reaches the most shoppers, and spec homes must be on the MLS to appear alongside agent listings. NewHomeSource reaches buyers who are only shopping new construction. Run one for a full quarter and judge it by cost per appointment and cost per contract rather than cost per lead, since portal leads are often early in their search.
