Wednesday, 4:15 p.m. You’re in an attic in Spring with a flashlight in your teeth when the phone buzzes twice. One is an agent you’ve worked with for six years, asking if you can do Friday in Cypress. The other is a number you don’t know. You call it back from the driveway an hour later. The buyer already booked someone else, because that someone had a “book now” button and a price on the website.

That second call is where most of the growth in home inspector marketing is hiding.

The short version for Houston and The Woodlands: keep the agent relationships, but earn them the only way TREC allows, which is by being good, fast and easy to schedule. Then build a second pipeline that doesn’t depend on agents at all. That means a Google Business Profile full of reviews, a website with prices, a sample report and online booking, plus add-on services that raise the ticket. Houston’s clay soil and flood history are the content that sets you apart. And you need a plan for months when HAR’s numbers go soft, because they’re soft right now.

This is written for one- to five-inspector companies working Harris and Montgomery counties. We haven’t run marketing for a home inspection company, so where we share numbers from other home service accounts, we say so.

Where Houston home inspection jobs actually come from

Ask most inspectors where their work comes from and you’ll hear “agents” before the question is finished. That’s true, and it’s also a risk. An inspection business that lives on eight agents is one brokerage change away from a bad quarter.

Buyers research everything now. A first-time buyer in Tomball reads three reviews of the dishwasher, so of course they’ll Google “home inspector near me” once the option period starts. Many agents hand over three names, and the buyer checks all three on Google. Your profile and website decide which name gets the call even when the referral was real.

So think of it as two funnels that share a front door:

  • Agent-influenced. The agent mentions you, the buyer looks you up, then books. Your reviews and website still close it.
  • Buyer-direct. No agent recommendation, or the buyer ignores it. They search “home inspection The Woodlands” or “home inspector Katy TX,” compare prices and availability, and book whoever makes it easy.

A third, smaller stream (pre-listing, new construction and investors) gets its own section below.

The TREC rules that shape every inspector’s marketing

Texas inspectors work under rules that most marketing agencies have never read. Read them before you approve a single ad.

Your license number goes on everything. Under TREC Rule 535.221, an inspector’s advertisement includes a lot more than ads: inspection reports, business cards, invoices, signs, brochures, websites, email, text messages, directory listings and broadcast ads. Every one must show your name (or a DBA registered with TREC) and your license number. Websites can put the license numbers on one prominent page such as the homepage or About page, and on social sites the number can sit on the profile page. If you’re a real estate inspector or apprentice, your ads also have to name your sponsoring professional inspector and say you’re sponsored.

Register the DBA with TREC, not just the county. TREC’s 2024 article on inspector DBAs spells out that filing an assumed name with the Secretary of State doesn’t satisfy TREC. Each inspector registers the company name under their own license, and notifies TREC within 30 days of starting or stopping use of it. If you’re adding a second inspector this year, put that on the onboarding checklist next to the truck magnet.

No referral fees, either direction. TREC Rule 535.220 lists real estate agents and brokers among “settlement service providers,” and inspectors may not pay or receive a fee or other valuable consideration to or from them for referring inspections or for being placed on a list of preferred providers. The same rule bars inspecting a property when your compensation or future referrals depend on what you report, and it asks you to assure clients of your independence from outside influence.

Paid vendor lists are a narrow exception. Per TREC’s guidance on preferred provider lists, a brokerage can sell advertising on a vendor list only if the list clearly says placement is based on advertising, the price is a market rate, and any ranking discloses how it was built. A “platinum preferred inspector” package that quietly buys referrals is exactly what the rule targets.

None of this is legal advice, and TREC updates its rules, so check the current text before you build a campaign around a gray area. If something feels like a way to pay for referrals with extra steps, it probably is.

Earning agent referrals without paying for them

Agents can’t be bought, at least not legally. They can be made to like working with you. What we hear agents in The Woodlands and Kingwood care about is short and boring.

  • You answer the phone, or call back inside an hour.
  • Same-week availability, including Saturdays.
  • A report delivered the same night or the next morning, with a summary page a buyer can actually read.
  • Findings written in plain, calm language. “Recommend evaluation by a licensed plumber” lands better than three paragraphs of alarm, and it’s still honest.
  • Scheduling that doesn’t require four texts. An online booking link the agent can forward wins over “call me.”

Notice what’s missing from that list. Softening findings to save deals is off the table under 535.220, and agents who want that aren’t referral partners you should keep. Your report is written for your client, who is the buyer.

Ordinary relationship marketing is still fine. A monthly agent email about something useful (what a Houston sewer scope finds, how to read an elevation survey) keeps your name in front of them. Keep coffee and lunches modest and never tie them to referrals, since “valuable consideration” is the phrase in the rule. When unsure, ask your attorney or TREC.

Buyer-direct: winning the Google search in Houston and The Woodlands

The buyer who searches has a deadline. The option period on a TREC contract is often a week to ten days, so they need someone this week, and they decide in minutes.

Google Business Profile first. Primary category: Home inspector. Add services one by one with short descriptions (“Buyer’s home inspection, TREC REI 7-6 report, same-day delivery”), list add-ons separately, set accurate hours, and link your booking page as the appointment URL. If you work from home, set it up as a service-area business with the towns you really cover, from The Woodlands and Conroe down to Spring and Cypress. Post photos from real inspections: the attic, the electrical panel, the drone shot of a roof in Magnolia. Our Houston map pack guide covers the ranking side.

The website that books inspections: prices, sample report, online scheduling

Most inspector websites we see have a stock magnifying glass and a contact form. Buyers want three things on the first screen.

A price. Even a calculator by square footage and age, or a simple table that starts at [your price] for up to 2,000 square feet. Buyers comparing three inspectors will call the one who shows a number first. Hiding it to “discuss value” mostly loses you the comparison.

A sample report. A real, anonymized report from a typical Houston house, with photos and the summary page. The full TREC REI 7-6 format is long and technical, and buyers love seeing how you explain it. This is also your best agent marketing, because agents judge you by how your reports read to nervous buyers.

Online scheduling with the add-ons attached. The buyer picks a date and adds the sewer scope, WDI and pool inspection as checkboxes with prices. The booking tool should collect the agent’s name, send the agreement for signature and take a deposit if that’s your policy. Most inspection software does all of this. Turning it on is the marketing.

Then the trust stuff: your TREC license number on the About page (which also satisfies 535.221(d)), each inspector’s photo and license, and a page per service area. Our local landing pages guide shows how to write area pages that aren’t copy-and-paste, and our web design team builds them.

Add-on services: sewer scope, termite, pool and foundation elevation

Add-ons are how a $400 job becomes a $700 job, and in Houston several of them are close to essential. Each one also gives you a keyword to rank for and a reason for a buyer to pick you over a cheaper inspector who can’t do it.

Add-onLicense or rule to checkHouston marketing angle
WDI / termite inspectionOfficial WDI inspection by a certified applicator or technician in TDA’s Termite and WDI category (4 TAC 7.175)Buyers often want it with the main inspection; say clearly who performs it
Sewer scopeCovered by your inspection agreement; describe limits clearlyOlder homes in Spring Branch, Meyerland and the Heights; tree roots from big oaks

Foundation elevation surveyReport observations; leave engineering conclusions to engineersClay soil, slab foundations and buyers who’ve heard repair horror stories
Sprinklers, wells, septic, outbuildingsAlso optional systems under 535.233Acreage in Magnolia, Montgomery and north of Conroe

The WDI one has a trap. A TREC license doesn’t cover it. Per 4 TAC 7.175, the official WDI inspection has to be done by a TDA Structural Pest Control Service licensee in the right category, and the Texas WDI report is a separate form from REI 7-6, as Spectora’s Texas WDI explainer explains. TREC’s inspector FAQ even addresses the inspector who advertises WDI inspections when nobody on staff is licensed, and says that can be misleading advertising under both agencies’ rules. If you subcontract to a licensed pest company, say so on the page.

Pools and acreage systems are optional under TREC Rule 535.233, so price them separately. Put each add-on on its own page with its own price. “Sewer scope Houston” is a real search, and a single page that says “we offer many services” won’t rank for any of them.

Houston clay soil and flood history: content buyers actually read

Most inspector blogs are generic (“10 things we check”). In Houston, you have better material than that, and buyers are already scared of it.

Clay soil and foundations. KPRC reported in September 2026 that Houston’s heavy clay swells when wet and shrinks and cracks when dry, and that repeated cycle contributes to foundation movement. A local foundation contractor in the same story put repair costs anywhere from about $3,000 to $20,000 and up, with severe cases at $40,000 to $50,000. That one sentence explains why a buyer will pay extra for elevation readings. Useful posts: which slab cracks you note and why you write “recommend evaluation by a structural engineer” instead of a diagnosis.

Flood history. Since September 1, 2019, SB 339 has required Texas sellers to disclose more about flood risk, including the 500-year floodplain, reservoir proximity and whether the home has flooded before, per the Texas Tribune. The same story noted that, based on city of Houston data, most structures affected by Harvey were outside the 100- and 500-year floodplains. So buyers in Kingwood, Katy and Meyerland have good reason to ask what flood clues an inspector looks for. A post on “signs a house may have flooded” (patched drywall at the same height in every room, new baseboards in an older house, rust lines on the water heater) is exactly the kind of practical content buyers share with their spouse.

Stay in your lane: point out evidence and recommend further evaluation. You’re not the floodplain manager or the engineer.

A few other Houston-specific topics that earn links and searches: what the February 2021 freeze did to attic plumbing, radiant barrier and attic heat, polybutylene and galvanized supply lines in 1970s and 1980s homes, and what to check on a new build in a master-planned community before the builder’s walkthrough.

Local Services Ads for home inspectors

Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes Home inspector, so you can run pay-per-lead ads with the Google Verified badge at the very top of the results. Availability still varies by area, and the only way to know for your ZIP code is to start the sign-up and see what Google offers.

Inspection searches are short and urgent, and the LSA box is the first thing a buyer sees. You pay per lead, not per click. Ranking leans on reviews, responsiveness and proximity, so 9 reviews won’t beat a company with 400.

Reviews: the inspector’s best ad

Reviews do double duty for inspectors. Buyers read them, and agents check them before they put your name on a list.

The timing is tricky. Right after the inspection, the buyer is anxious about your findings. The best moment is usually a day or two after the report, once they’ve read it and maybe used it to negotiate repairs. A text that works:

Hi Maria, this is Dan from [company]. Hope the report helped with your repair request on the Oak Ridge North house. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other buyers find us: [link]. Thanks, and congrats on the house.

Send the same link to every client, not only the happy ones. Google’s rules prohibit review gating and incentives, and filtering out unhappy clients usually backfires anyway. Reply to every review with specifics. If a buyer complains that you “missed” something, explain calmly what was visible and accessible on inspection day; future clients read the reply more closely than the complaint. When the market slows: HAR’s numbers and what to do with them

Inspection volume follows contracts, and contracts are down. HAR’s September 2026 market report showed 7,030 single-family sales in September 2026, down 5.7% from a year earlier, with active listings up 6.3% to 40,192, a 5.5-month supply and 58 average days on market. October was weaker: My Neighborhood News’ summary of HAR’s October report put single-family sales at 6,348, down 14.9%, with a $320,000 median price and an average mortgage rate of 7.33%.

Fewer contracts means fewer inspections. What we’d do:

  • Sell pre-listing inspections. With more than five months of supply, sellers need an edge. A pre-listing inspection lets them fix things before a buyer’s inspector finds them. Pitch it to listing agents, not just buyer’s agents.
  • Go after new construction. Builders have inventory to move and buyers in Conroe and Montgomery are buying new. Phase inspections (foundation pre-pour, pre-drywall, final) and 11-month warranty inspections are steady work that doesn’t care about resale volume.
  • Market to investors and property managers. They buy in all markets and order repeat inspections.
  • Keep the buyer-direct funnel running. Don’t cut search ads the month you need them most; cut wasted keywords instead.

Frequently asked questions

How do home inspectors get more clients in Houston?

Most Houston inspectors get work from agent referrals and from buyers who search Google directly. Grow both. Keep agents happy with fast scheduling, same-day reports and clear writing, and build a buyer-direct pipeline with a strong Google Business Profile, steady reviews, a website showing prices and a sample report, online booking, and search ads for terms like home inspector The Woodlands or sewer scope Houston.

Can a Texas home inspector pay real estate agents for referrals?

No. TREC Rule 535.220 lists real estate agents and brokers as settlement service providers, and inspectors may not pay or receive a fee or other valuable consideration to or from them for referrals or for placement on a preferred provider list. Paid vendor lists are allowed only at market advertising rates with clear disclosure that placement is advertising. Check the current rule or an attorney before any arrangement.

Do Texas inspectors have to put their license number in ads?

Yes. TREC Rule 535.221 requires an inspector’s name or TREC-registered business name and license number on advertisements, which include reports, business cards, invoices, signs, websites, emails and texts. A website can show license numbers on one prominent page such as the homepage or About page, and social media accounts can show them on the profile page. Sponsored inspectors must also name their sponsor.

Can a home inspector in Houston and The Woodlands offer termite inspections?

Only if the person doing the official wood destroying insect inspection holds a Texas Department of Agriculture Structural Pest Control Service license in the termite and WDI category. A TREC inspector license alone does not cover it, and TREC warns that advertising WDI inspections without a licensed person on staff can be misleading. Many inspectors get the TDA license or partner with a licensed pest company and disclose it.

Are Google Local Services Ads available for home inspectors?

Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes Home inspector, so many inspection companies can run pay-per-lead ads with the Google Verified badge. Availability varies by location, so start the sign-up with your business address to see if it’s offered in your Houston area ZIP codes. Ranking depends heavily on reviews and how fast you respond, so build reviews before expecting volume.
