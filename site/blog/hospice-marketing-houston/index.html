It’s 11:40 on a Wednesday night in a hospital hallway in The Woodlands. A daughter has just been told her father’s cancer isn’t responding and the doctor “wants to talk about comfort care tomorrow.” She types “what does hospice cost” into her phone, then “hospice near me,” then “is hospice giving up.”

The next morning, the case manager on that floor hands her a list. Which hospice gets the call depends on two things: who that case manager trusts to admit quickly and well, and what the daughter found on her phone at midnight.

Hospice marketing in Houston runs on those two tracks. Physicians, hospital case managers, skilled nursing facilities and assisted living communities refer most patients, so the referral relationship program is the core. Families still check you on Google and Care Compare before they agree, and sometimes they ask for you by name. Both tracks have to be handled with care, because the subject deserves it and because hospice is one of the most closely watched corners of Medicare.

How patients actually reach a hospice

Hospice is now a normal part of end-of-life care. MedPAC’s March 2026 report found 1.82 million Medicare beneficiaries received hospice care in 2024, and 52.9% of Medicare decedents used it, a new high. Medicare spent $28.3 billion on hospice that year across 6,706 providers, about 82% of them for-profit.

The number that should shape your marketing is the median length of stay: 19 days. Half of hospice patients get less than three weeks of care. A median that short suggests many referrals come late, when families have little time to use what hospice offers. The best hospice marketing in this city is aimed at getting the conversation started earlier, with physicians and with families.

Medicare eligibility frames the whole thing. Per Medicare’s hospice page, a patient’s hospice doctor and regular doctor (if they have one) certify a life expectancy of six months or less, and the patient chooses comfort care instead of curative treatment for that illness. So every admission involves a physician, and most start with someone in a clinical role saying the word “hospice” first.

The rules: anti-kickback, Texas law and why Houston is watched closely

Before anything else, the compliance part, because it shapes everything else in this post. We’re marketers, not lawyers, so treat this as a map of where to ask questions.

Federal anti-kickback. Offering or paying anything of value to induce referrals of Medicare or Medicaid patients can be a crime. The OIG Special Fraud Alert on hospices and nursing homes (April 1998) is still worth reading because it lists the arrangements OIG considers suspect: free goods or below-market goods to a nursing home to induce referrals, paying room and board above what Medicaid would pay, providing staff at the hospice’s expense to do the nursing home’s work, and swapping referrals. In a 2000 OIG opinion, OIG approved a volunteer-based free services program but repeated its “serious concerns” about free or below-market goods offered to referral sources.

Gifts to patients and families. OIG’s 2016 nominal value policy sets the nominal value for gifts to beneficiaries at $15 per item and $75 per patient per year. It applies to patients, not referral sources.

Texas law. Texas Occupations Code §102.001 makes it an offense to knowingly pay or accept anything of value for securing or soliciting patients for a licensed provider, regardless of payer.

Houston-specific scrutiny. CMS put new hospices in Arizona, California, Nevada and Texas under a provisional period of enhanced oversight starting July 13, 2023, with medical review before claims are paid, per CMS’s enhanced oversight fact sheet. CMS said in August 2023 that it had made unannounced visits to more than 7,000 hospices. And in October 2025 the U.S. Attorney’s Office in Houston announced charges against seven Houston-area defendants in an alleged $110 million hospice fraud scheme, including alleged kickbacks to group home owners and a hospital discharge coordinator, and marketers allegedly telling families patients qualified when many weren’t terminally ill. Those are allegations, not convictions, but every case manager in Houston has read that kind of story.

What this means for a legitimate hospice: your marketing team’s pay plan, your gifts policy, your “community liaison” job description and your relationships with ALFs and group homes should all be reviewed by a healthcare attorney. Liaisons paid per admission is a structure to discuss with counsel before you adopt it. And your marketing should make it obvious, to referral sources and families, that you’re the careful one.

Physician, hospital and SNF relationships: the core of hospice growth

Referral sources pick a hospice for reasons that have little to do with brochures. They pick the one that answers the phone at 6 p.m. on a Friday, admits the same day, keeps the attending physician informed, and doesn’t generate complaints from families.

Your liaison program should sell those things, with proof:

Referral sourceWhat they need from youWhat to bring them
Oncologists, cardiologists, pulmonologistsConfidence you’ll keep them involved and manage symptoms wellA one-page summary of your physician communication process, clinical education on prognosis conversations
Hospital case managers and discharge planners (Texas Medical Center, The Woodlands, Kingwood, Katy campuses)Fast response and same-day or next-day admissionsA single intake number, your actual response and admission times, weekend coverage details
Skilled nursing facilitiesA hospice that works with their staff without disrupting themClear coordination roles, written care plan communication, education for floor staff
Assisted living and memory careHelp keeping residents in place through end of lifeDementia-specific education, family meeting support
Primary care and geriatric practicesHelp starting the conversation earlierPatient-facing materials on hospice vs. palliative care, in English and Spanish

Educational in-services for nurses and case managers are the most useful thing a liaison can offer, and they’re appropriate when they’re genuinely educational. Keep the food modest and the content clinical. Track every referral source in a CRM with referral counts, conversion to admission, and time from referral to admission.

Assisted living partnerships deserve their own playbook. We covered how those communities market themselves in our senior living marketing post, which is useful reading for any liaison calling on them. We won’t repeat it here.

Care Compare: the quality data families and case managers check

Medicare publishes hospice quality data on Care Compare, and it’s more visible than many hospices assume. CAHPS Hospice Survey star ratings, based on surveys of family caregivers after a patient’s death, first appeared with the August 2022 Care Compare refresh for hospices with 75 or more completed surveys. Per CMS’s hospice public reporting page, the assessment measures are now based on the HOPE tool, which replaced HIS on October 1, 2025, alongside CAHPS and claims-based measures.

How to use it in marketing, honestly:

  • If your star rating is strong, link to your Care Compare listing from your website and liaison materials. Link to the source rather than screenshotting it, so the data stays current.
  • Quote specific measures accurately, with the reporting period. Don’t round up, and don’t present a single strong measure as your overall rating.
  • If your rating is weak, fix the care first. Marketing a hospice with a two-star family caregiver rating is buying attention for a problem.
  • Check what families see when they search your name. Care Compare, Google reviews and your own website should tell the same story.

Texas HHS licenses hospices as a service type under the Home and Community Support Services Agency license, per Texas HHS. Put your license and Medicare certification on your About page. Families in this market have learned to look.

Content for families and caregivers

The daughter in the hallway at midnight needs plain answers. Most hospice websites give her a mission statement and a stock photo of two hands.

Write the pages families actually search for:

  • What does hospice cost? Per Medicare’s hospice page, care from a Medicare-approved hospice has no cost to the patient, with copays of up to $5 for outpatient drugs for pain and symptoms and 5% for inpatient respite care. Room and board at home or in a nursing home isn’t covered. Say all of it, including the room and board part.
  • Can Dad keep his doctor? Yes, if he chooses his regular doctor or nurse practitioner as his attending.
  • Hospice vs. palliative care, in plain language.
  • What the first week looks like, from the first call to the first nurse visit.
  • Caregiver guides: what to expect in the last days, how to talk to children, how to take a break (respite), and grief support after.
  • Hurricane and power outage plans. Houston families caring for someone at home on oxygen worry about this every June. Tell them your plan.

Write like a hospice nurse talks: calm, specific, no euphemism overload. And put a phone number staffed by a person at the top of every page.

Google Ads with sensitivity

Paid search can work for hospice, mostly on brand terms, “hospice near me,” “hospice care [town]” and the cost and eligibility questions. It needs restraint.

Google treats health conditions as a sensitive category in personalized advertising, and per Google’s health in personalized advertising policy, ads in that category can’t use Customer Match, your data segments or lookalike audiences. We’d go further by choice. Don’t retarget people who visited your “signs that death is near” page. A family that just lost someone doesn’t need your banner ad following them around for 30 days.

Negative keywords matter more than usual, because hospice search volume is full of job seekers and people looking for something else:

Track calls as conversions, schedule ads around when intake is staffed (which should be always), and keep ad copy factual: “Medicare-Certified Hospice Serving Montgomery and Harris Counties. Call Any Time.” Our Houston CPC benchmarks give a feel for click costs, and our search team sets these accounts up.

Google reviews, handled with care

Reviews matter for hospice the way they matter for any local business, and asking for them is harder. The family is grieving, and the patient usually isn’t the one who can review.

What we’d suggest:

  • Never ask during active care, and never at the bedside.
  • If you ask at all, do it through your bereavement follow-up, weeks later, as an optional line in a letter or call: “If you’d like to share your experience, it helps other families find care.”
  • Ask every family the same way. Google’s prohibited content policy bans selectively asking only happy families and bans incentives.
  • Reply to every review without confirming the person was a patient or naming a diagnosis, per HHS’s bulletin on online tracking and HIPAA generally. “Thank you for sharing this. We’re holding your family in our thoughts” is enough for a positive review.
  • For a negative review, reply once, calmly, with a name and number for the administrator. Then call.

Spanish-speaking families in Houston

Harris County is 44.0% Hispanic, and the Houston metro is 38.2%, per the City of Houston planning department’s ACS summary. Hospice use is lower among Hispanic Medicare decedents: 40.4% in 2024 against 55.6% for White decedents, per MedPAC’s March 2026 report.

Some of that gap is about trust, language and how families talk about death, and marketing can’t fix all of it. But a hospice that wants to serve Houston well should have:

  • Spanish pages written or reviewed by a fluent speaker who works in healthcare, not machine-translated.
  • A Spanish-speaking intake option, said plainly on the site and in ads.
  • Bilingual staff, chaplains and social workers, introduced by name.
  • Community education with parishes, churches and clinics in Pasadena, Aldine, Spring Branch, Gulfton and Conroe, done as education and not as a pitch.

Community education that earns trust

Advance care planning workshops, caregiver support groups and talks at churches, libraries and senior centers do two things. They help families have the conversation earlier, which addresses that 19-day median. And they make your hospice the familiar name when the moment comes.

Keep it educational. Bring a nurse or social worker, not just a liaison. Leave written materials in English and Spanish. Partner with organizations that already serve caregivers. Employer lunch-and-learns for working caregivers can work well in The Woodlands, where plenty of people in their 50s are caring for a parent in Spring or Conroe. Our home care client post covers similar outreach from the home care side.

What to measure, and a first 90 days

The metrics that matter: referrals by source, referral-to-admission conversion, time from referral to admission, length of stay at admission (are referrals coming earlier?), live discharge rate, Care Compare scores and review volume. Website traffic matters only as far as it produces calls.

A first 90 days for a hospice tightening its marketing: have counsel review liaison pay and gifts in weeks 1 to 2. Rewrite the cost, eligibility and caregiver pages in weeks 2 to 6, in English and Spanish. Set up the CRM and response-time tracking by week 4, and launch restrained brand and local search campaigns by week 8. Book three community education events by week 12.

Frequently asked questions

How do hospices get referrals in Houston?

Most hospice referrals come from physicians, hospital case managers, skilled nursing facilities and assisted living communities. They choose hospices that respond quickly, admit the same or next day, keep the attending physician informed and generate few family complaints. Educational in-services, a single intake number, tracked response times and honest quality data matter more than brochures. Have a healthcare attorney review gifts, events and liaison pay.

Can a hospice give gifts to referral sources?

Be very careful. The federal anti-kickback statute can apply to anything of value offered to induce Medicare or Medicaid referrals, and OIG has flagged free goods and services to nursing homes as suspect. Texas law also bans paying for patient referrals. OIG’s $15 per item and $75 per year nominal value limit applies to gifts to patients, not referral sources. Ask a healthcare attorney first.

What is the CAHPS Hospice star rating on Care Compare?

It’s a star rating based on the CAHPS Hospice Survey, which asks family caregivers about the care their loved one received. Medicare began publishing hospice star ratings on Care Compare in August 2022 for hospices with at least 75 completed surveys. Families and case managers can see it, so link to your listing and quote measures accurately with the reporting period.

Is it appropriate to run Google Ads for a hospice?

Yes, with restraint. Search ads for brand terms, hospice near me, and cost or eligibility questions help families who are already looking. Google limits personalized targeting for health topics, and we recommend skipping retargeting of grieving families altogether. Use strong negative keywords for jobs and unrelated searches, keep ad copy factual and track calls, which is how most families reach a hospice.

How should a Houston hospice ask for Google reviews?

Never during active care or at the bedside. If you ask, include an optional line in your bereavement follow-up weeks later, and ask every family the same way, since Google bans selective asking and incentives. Reply to reviews without confirming the person was a patient or mentioning any diagnosis, and handle negative reviews with one calm reply and a direct call from the administrator.
