Sunday night, 9:15. You’re looking at the booking inbox. Two people want a price on a one-time deep clean and haven’t answered your reply from Friday. A tenant in Midtown needs a move-out clean by Thursday so she gets her deposit back. A Facebook message says “how much for a 3 bed 2 bath?” and nothing else. And your best biweekly client in Cypress texted that they’re “going to pause for a while.”

That inbox is the whole business. One-time jobs fill the calendar this week, and recurring clients pay the bills all year.

To get more cleaning clients in Houston, make it easy to get a price and book online, show up in Google Maps for the neighborhoods you serve, collect reviews from every recurring client, and work Nextdoor and local Facebook groups where neighbors ask for referrals. Then measure the number that matters: what it costs you to land one client who books recurring service and stays.

This guide is for owner-operated maid services and small cleaning companies with one to ten crews, from Kingwood to Sugar Land.

Why recurring clients are the whole game

Most cleaning owners know recurring work is better. Fewer have put numbers on how much better, and that changes what you can afford to spend to get a client.

Take an illustrative biweekly client paying $160 a visit. If they stay a year, that’s 26 visits and $4,160 in revenue. A one-time deep clean at $320 is a good day, and then it’s over. Even if half your recurring clients leave in the first six months, the average recurring client is worth several times a one-time job.

That math gives you a ceiling on acquisition cost. If a recurring client is worth $4,000 a year in revenue and your margin after labor and supplies is 25% to 35%, spending $150 to $250 to land one is reasonable. Spending $150 to land a one-time clean that never comes back usually isn’t.

So treat one-time and move-out cleans as a way in. Price them to make money on their own, then offer every one-time customer a recurring rate before the crew leaves. “Your first recurring clean is at our regular biweekly price instead of the deep-clean rate” is a natural pitch, because the house is already in good shape.

Let people get a price and book without a phone call

“How much for a 3 bed 2 bath?” is the most common message a cleaning company gets, and most owners answer it slowly or vaguely. The companies growing fastest in Houston answer it instantly, on the website.

What a booking page needs:

  • Price by bedrooms, bathrooms and approximate square footage, with clear add-ons (inside oven, inside fridge, interior windows, laundry, pet hair).
  • Separate prices for standard, deep and move-in/move-out cleans, and the recurring discount shown next to each.
  • Available dates you can actually fill, with crew capacity behind them.
  • A card on file at booking, with your cancellation policy in plain words.
  • Your service area by ZIP code, so someone in Pearland doesn’t book when you only serve The Woodlands.

One Texas detail to get right on that page: sales tax. Per the Texas Comptroller’s cleaning and janitorial services publication, maid, janitorial and custodial services should collect state and local sales tax, and tax is due on the charge to clean “a home, office, warehouse, garage, restaurant, or any other building.” The exception is a self-employed person working directly as an employee of the household, not a subcontractor for a maid service. If you run a company with crews, show prices “plus tax” or build it in, and say which. Surprise tax on the first invoice is a common reason a new client doesn’t rebook.

If you keep a phone-quote option, answer within five minutes during business hours. A homeowner messaging three cleaning companies books with whoever replies first with a real number. Google Business Profile for a cleaning company that doesn’t have a storefront

Most cleaning companies run from a home office or a small warehouse that customers never visit. Google calls that a service-area business, and the rules are specific.

Per Google’s service-area rules, if customers don’t come to your address, remove it from the profile and list service areas instead. You can list up to 20 cities or ZIP codes, you can’t use a radius, and the whole area shouldn’t go past about two hours of driving. For a cleaning crew that’s irrelevant, because nobody drives two hours to clean a house. List the areas you actually serve and keep the list tight enough that your crews aren’t spending half the day on the Grand Parkway.

The rest of the setup:

  • Category: pick the house cleaning category as primary if residential is your main work. Add a commercial cleaning category only if you really take office contracts.
  • Services: list standard cleaning, deep cleaning, move-in/move-out, post-construction and recurring service, each with a short description and a starting price.
  • Photos: before and after shots of kitchens and bathrooms (with client permission and no personal items showing), your team in uniform, your branded car.
  • Booking link: point the appointment link straight to your online booking page.
  • Posts: monthly offers, holiday booking reminders, open recurring slots in a given area.

Rankings in Maps lean heavily on how close the searcher is to your business location, which a service-area business still has behind the scenes. That’s why a company based in Spring usually shows up more easily in Spring than in Sugar Land. Our map pack guide explains what you can and can’t influence.

Reviews: ask after the second clean, not the first

Reviews are the main thing a stranger uses to decide whether to let your crew into their home. Trust is the product.

The rules from Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps apply: no discounts or free add-ons for reviews, no asking only the clients you know are happy, and no discouraging negative reviews. Ask everyone.

The timing trick that works for recurring services: ask after the second or third visit, once the client has seen consistency. A first clean can be great and still leave the client unsure whether you’ll show up on time next time. By the third clean, they know.

A text that tends to work:

“Hi Priya, it’s Ana from [Company]. Hope the house felt good after today’s clean. Would you mind leaving us a quick Google review? Mentioning that you’re a biweekly client in Katy really helps other families find us: [link]. And if anything was missed, reply here and we’ll come back.”

Reply to every review within a couple of days. On a bad one, skip the defense, name the fix, and offer a re-clean. People shopping for a cleaner read the replies to see how you treat problems. The complete guide to Google reviews has more scripts.

Move-out cleans: apartments, property managers and realtors

Houston is a renter’s city. In Census QuickFacts for 2019 to 2023, only 42.0% of occupied homes in the City of Houston were owner-occupied, so most are rented. Every lease that ends is a possible move-out clean.

The tenant’s motivation is the deposit. Under the Texas Property Code, as summarized in the Texas State Law Library’s guide to deposit refunds, a landlord has to refund the deposit within 30 days after the tenant surrenders the premises (once the tenant gives a written forwarding address), can’t keep any of it for normal wear and tear, and generally has to itemize deductions. Tenants who know a dirty oven or a grimy shower can come out of their deposit will pay for a professional move-out clean.

How to get that work:

  • A move-out page and ad group. Keywords like “move out cleaning Houston,” “apartment move out cleaning,” “end of lease cleaning” and “move in cleaning,” with a checklist of what you clean (inside appliances, cabinets, baseboards, blinds).
  • Apartment locators and leasing offices. Houston has a big apartment locator industry. Locators and leasing agents get asked “do you know a cleaner?” constantly. A one-page flyer and a reliable 48-hour turnaround earns referrals.
  • Property managers. Single-family rental managers in Katy, Cypress and Humble need turnover cleans between tenants. That’s steady B2B work with invoicing instead of card-on-file.
  • Realtors. Listing agents want a pre-listing deep clean before photos, and buyer’s agents get asked about move-in cleans. Our post on how local agents market themselves shows what they care about.

Move-out customers are, by definition, leaving. Some are moving across town, though. Ask for the new address and offer a move-in clean or a recurring rate at the new place.

Nextdoor and Facebook: where neighbors ask for a cleaner

Search Facebook groups for The Woodlands, Kingwood or Pearland and you’ll find a post asking for a house cleaner recommendation almost every week. Same on Nextdoor. For a cleaning company, these referral threads are one of the cheapest sources of recurring clients there is.

Nextdoor

Claiming a Nextdoor business page is free, and verified neighbors can recommend you and “Fave” you. Per Nextdoor’s own guide to recommendations, your Fave count helps you rank higher in Nextdoor search. The same guide cites Nextdoor’s own survey numbers: 78% of neighbors say they’re more likely to trust a small business a neighbor recommended, and businesses with even one recommendation get three times as many page views. Treat those as Nextdoor’s figures, but the direction matches what we see.

What to do: claim the page, ask your recurring clients who use Nextdoor to recommend you (the same ask as a Google review, sent to the ones you know are on the app, without any reward), and post when you have openings in a specific neighborhood. Whether paid Nextdoor ads are worth it is a separate question we answered in is Nextdoor advertising worth it.

Facebook groups and Facebook ads

In groups, don’t spam the wall with ads. Most neighborhood groups ban it, and admins remember. The better play is to get your clients to tag you when someone asks for a recommendation. A reply from a real neighbor saying “Ana’s crew has cleaned our house every other Friday for two years” beats any ad.

Facebook and Instagram ads can work for a cleaning company with an offer and a tight area. What we’d test:

  • A short video of a crew finishing a kitchen, shot on a phone, with the price for a standard clean on screen.
  • An offer aimed at recurring service, like a discount on the first clean when you book biweekly.
  • Targeting a few ZIP codes where you already have clients, so new bookings fit your routes.
  • Sending clicks to your booking page. If you use instant forms, ask for home size and preferred frequency so you’re not chasing price shoppers. Our guide to fixing bad Facebook leads has the form settings.

Google Ads and Local Services Ads for cleaning companies

House cleaning services is a Local Services Ads category on Google’s Local Services Ads category list, so you can run pay-per-lead ads at the top of searches like “house cleaning near me” and “maid service Houston.” For a cleaning company, LSA is usually worth a test once you have 30 or more Google reviews. Without reviews, you’ll pay for leads that pick the competitor with 300.

Spanish-language marketing: for customers and for crews

In the City of Houston, 47.2% of residents age 5 and up speak a language other than English at home, and 44.1% are Hispanic or Latino, per Census QuickFacts. For a cleaning company, that matters on both sides of the business.

Customers. Many Houston families would rather book in Spanish. A Spanish version of your booking page, a bilingual phone line and a few Spanish-language reviews open up a part of the market that a lot of English-only competitors ignore. Our Hispanic marketing guide covers Spanish ads and SEO in detail. Translate the pages properly, with a fluent speaker, rather than running them through a tool and hoping.

Crews. Hiring is the real growth limit for most cleaning companies, and recruiting is a marketing job. The BLS occupational data for May 2023 counted 15,270 maids and housekeeping cleaners in the Houston metro, with a mean wage of $14.49 an hour. You’re competing with hotels, hospitals and every other cleaning company for those people. A careers page in English and Spanish, with pay, schedule and how tips work, does better than a vague “we’re hiring” post.

If you advertise jobs on Facebook or Instagram, the ads must run under Meta’s Employment special ad category. Per Jon Loomer’s guide to Meta’s special ad categories, that locks age to 18 to 65+ and all genders, requires at least a 15-mile radius with no ZIP code targeting, removes lookalike audiences, and keeps instant forms from asking about age, gender or relationship status. Write the ad so the right applicants recognize themselves: pay range, days, area, “no nights or weekends.”

Retention: the first three cleans decide it

Marketing gets clients in. Retention decides whether the marketing was worth it. In accounts we’ve looked at across home services, a client who makes it past the first few visits tends to stay much longer, so the first month deserves extra care.

  • Same crew, same day. Clients hire a company and stay for the people. Keep the crew consistent and tell the client when it changes.
  • Notes on every home. Where the dog stays, which bathroom gets the special cleaner, “don’t move the papers on the desk.” Put it in your software so a substitute crew knows.
  • A check-in after the first and third clean. One text: “Anything we should do differently next time?” Fix it, then thank them.
  • A real pause option. Summer travel and holidays cause cancellations. Offer a pause with a return date instead of a cancel, and send a reminder the week before.
  • Holiday and season pushes. Thanksgiving and Christmas deep cleans, spring cleaning, back-to-school. Offer existing clients first pick of the slots.

Track monthly churn: recurring clients lost divided by recurring clients at the start of the month. If it’s high, fix service before you spend more on ads. Pouring new clients into a leaky schedule just raises your cost per retained client.

What to track: cost per booked recurring client

Most cleaning owners track leads, or worse, likes. The number that tells you whether a channel works is cost per booked recurring client, measured for each source.

To get it, tag every booking with its source (Google Maps, LSA, search ads, Nextdoor, Facebook, referral, apartment locator). Your booking software or a simple “How did you hear about us?” field can do it, and call tracking numbers handle phone bookings. Our tracking guide walks through the setup. Then once a month, divide what you spent on each source by the recurring clients it produced who are still active after 60 days.

A first 90 days for a small company:

  1. Month one: online pricing and booking, Google Business Profile cleanup, Nextdoor page claimed, review texts going to every recurring client after their second clean.
  2. Month two: move-out page and outreach to five apartment locators or property managers, plus one Facebook ad test in your best ZIP codes.
  3. Month three: Local Services Ads or search ads once reviews are in place, Spanish booking page if your market calls for it, and the first look at cost per recurring client by source.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more clients for my house cleaning business?

Make it easy to get a price and book online, set up a Google Business Profile as a service-area business, and ask every recurring client for a Google review after their second or third clean. Claim your free Nextdoor page and have clients recommend you there and in local Facebook groups. Then add Local Services Ads or search ads once you have enough reviews to compete.

How much should a cleaning company pay to get a new client?

It depends on whether the client recurs. A biweekly client who stays a year can bring in around $4,000 in revenue at typical Houston pricing, so spending $150 to $250 to land one is often reasonable. Spending that on a one-time clean usually isn't. Track cost per booked recurring client by source, counting only clients still active after 60 days.

Do cleaning services charge sales tax in Texas?

Generally yes. The Texas Comptroller says maid, janitorial and custodial services should collect state and local sales tax, and tax is due on cleaning a home, office or other building. The exception is a self-employed person working directly as a household employee rather than for a maid service. Show prices plus tax or with tax included, and confirm details with the Comptroller or your accountant.

Is Nextdoor good for marketing a cleaning business?

Often, yes. Neighbors regularly ask Nextdoor for cleaner recommendations, and a business page is free to claim. Verified neighbors can recommend you and Fave you, and Nextdoor says your Fave count helps you rank higher in its search. Ask existing clients who use Nextdoor to recommend you, post when you have openings in a neighborhood, and test paid ads separately.

How do I get move-out cleaning jobs in Houston?

Build a move-out cleaning page with a clear checklist and price, then run search ads on phrases like apartment move out cleaning Houston. Introduce yourself to apartment locators, leasing offices, single-family rental property managers and realtors, since they're asked for cleaner referrals constantly. Fast turnaround matters because Texas landlords must refund deposits within 30 days of move-out, and tenants want a clean unit before inspection.
