Three website quotes on one desk in a Research Forest office park: $1,400, $8,800 and $31,000. Same business, same request, same “we just need a new site.” The owner’s first instinct is that somebody is crazy. Usually nobody is. The three quotes describe three different products that happen to share a name.

Short answer: most small business websites in The Woodlands and Houston cost between $3,000 and $15,000 to build in 2026, plus roughly $300 to $2,500 a year to keep online and current. A five-page template site sits at the bottom. A custom service business site with location pages, booking and real SEO work lands in the middle. Ecommerce and custom features push past $20,000.

The rest of this guide is the detail behind that number: what each price tier actually buys, the line items hiding inside a quote, the yearly bills nobody mentions, Texas sales tax (yes, it applies), and how to tell a fair quote from a padded one.

Why do two Houston web designers quote $2,000 and $20,000 for the same site?

Because they aren’t quoting the same site. When we lay two quotes side by side for an owner, the gap almost always comes from four things: who writes the words, whether anyone researched what customers search for, how many pages target specific services and towns, and what happens after launch.

Neither quote is automatically wrong. A $2,000 site is a reasonable purchase for a new business that gets its customers from referrals and just needs to look real when someone checks. It’s the wrong purchase for a plumbing company that needs Google to send 40 calls a month.

The question to ask each bidder is simple: “What will this site do for my business in a year that the cheaper one won’t?” A good designer has a specific answer. A weak one talks about colors.

The yearly bill after launch

The build is one check. The site also has running costs, and they’re where surprise invoices come from.

Platform or hosting. Squarespace’s published pricing lists Basic at $25 a month billed monthly ($19 a month on an annual plan), Core at $39 ($29 annually), Plus at $65 ($49) and Advanced at $139 ($99). Shopify’s pricing page lists Basic at $39 a month or $29 billed yearly, plus card fees starting at 2.9% + 30¢ online. A WordPress site on decent managed hosting usually runs $25 to $60 a month in our experience, more for busy stores. Static sites like ours can cost almost nothing to host.

Domain. A .com still costs most businesses somewhere around $15 to $25 a year at retail. The wholesale price went up again with Verisign’s November 2026 increase, from $10.26 to $10.97, so expect registrars to nudge renewals. Put the domain in your own name and on your own card.

Email. Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, per user per month. Not part of the website, but often bundled into a designer’s invoice so you lose track of it.

Plugins, apps and licenses. Booking tools, form builders, review widgets, premium themes. Each is $5 to $50 a month and they add up quietly.

Maintenance and updates. $50 to $500 a month depending on what’s included. For WordPress this means updates and security patches. On any platform it should include small text and photo changes, so your hours and prices don’t sit wrong for six months.

Be wary of any agency whose mandatory monthly plan is the only way your site stays online, especially if the site lives on their account. Paying for ongoing help is fine. Being unable to leave is not.

Does Texas charge sales tax on website design?

Often, yes, and plenty of owners find out from their accountant after the fact. Texas Comptroller Publication 96-259 lists “web hosting, web site creation and maintenance” as examples of data processing, a taxable service in Texas, and says 20 percent of the charge for data processing is exempt.

The rule was amended in 2025. Per Grant Thornton’s summary of the amended rule, design that only produces a blueprint or plan for a website isn’t taxable, while creation, repair and maintenance are taxable when they involve storing, manipulating, compiling or entering data. In practice, most website builds land on the taxable side.

Rates: the state rate is 6.25 percent, and cities, counties and special districts can add up to 2 percent, for a combined maximum of 8.25 percent, per the Comptroller’s sales tax page. Much of the Houston area, including The Woodlands, sits at that 8.25 percent cap, but check the exact rate for your address.

An illustrative example: on a $10,000 build that’s fully taxable as data processing, 20 percent is exempt, so tax applies to $8,000. At 8.25 percent, that’s $660. Ask your bidders whether their quote includes tax, and ask your CPA how your specific contract should be treated. We’re marketers; this paragraph is a heads-up, not tax advice.

The costs owners forget: photos, speed and accessibility

Three things rarely show up in the first quote and almost always come up later.

Real photos. A half-day shoot of your team, trucks, office or work in progress usually runs $500 to $2,000 around Houston. It’s the cheapest trust you can buy. Customers in Kingwood can spot the same smiling stock-photo technician that’s on a competitor’s site in Katy.

Speed. Google measures real visitors’ experience with Core Web Vitals. Google’s Core Web Vitals thresholds call a page good when its largest content loads within 2.5 seconds, it responds to taps within 200 milliseconds and the layout barely shifts (a CLS of 0.1 or less), measured at the 75th percentile of visits on mobile and desktop. Heavy page builders, autoplay video and a dozen tracking scripts are how sites miss those numbers. Ask bidders what scores their recent sites get on a phone, not a laptop.

Accessibility. the Justice Department’s web accessibility guidance says Title III of the ADA covers businesses open to the public and points to WCAG as a helpful technical standard for websites. Building to WCAG from the start costs a little more. Retrofitting after a demand letter costs a lot more. Our website accessibility post covers the basics.

How many pages does a Houston service business need?

Page count drives price more than anything else, so it’s worth getting right. For a service business, a page earns its keep if it answers a search someone actually types.

  • Home, about, contact. Everyone needs these.
  • One page per main service. “Water heater replacement” and “slab leak repair” are different searches with different buyers. Lumping them on one “Services” page throws away rankings.
  • One page per town you really serve, with real local detail: which neighborhoods, how fast you get there, local jobs you’ve done, local reviews. The Woodlands, Spring, Conroe, Tomball, Magnolia and Kingwood are the usual set for a company based off I-45 or the Grand Parkway.
  • Reviews or project gallery, financing if you offer it, and an FAQ.

A typical home service build ends up at 15 to 30 pages. That sounds like a lot until you see the analytics a year later and notice the Conroe page brings in more calls than the homepage. Area pages only work if they’re genuinely different from each other, though. Swapping the town name into the same paragraph 12 times is a known pattern, and Google treats it that way. Hosting and hurricane season

A Houston-specific cost question: where does your site live when the power goes out? After Hurricane Beryl in July 2024, CenterPoint’s outage count started at about 2.26 million customers, per the Texas Tribune. A site hosted on a server in your office closet went down with the building. A site on a reputable cloud host or CDN stayed up, which mattered for every business that needed to post “we’re open on generator power” or “closed until Thursday.”

Two cheap habits help. Make sure someone outside your office can edit the site (a phone login is fine). And keep a ready-made alert banner you can switch on in two minutes. Our hurricane season marketing playbook has the rest of the storm checklist.

How to read a website quote before you sign

Most bad website experiences we hear about weren’t caused by bad design. They came from a vague contract. Before you sign, walk through these.

Three red flags we see in Houston quotes over and over: the designer registers the domain in their own name, the price includes “SEO” with no description of what that means, and there’s no line about what happens to the site if you stop paying the monthly plan. Our agency contract red flags post covers more of them.

Timelines matter too. A template site can launch in two to four weeks. A custom service business site usually takes eight to twelve weeks, and the delay is almost always content: photos not shot, copy not approved, an old site login nobody can find.

Is a $10,000 website worth it? Run the math on your own numbers

The useful way to judge a website price is by what it returns, which means doing a little arithmetic with your own numbers. Here’s an illustrative example for a home service company in Spring.

The old site converts 2% of visitors into calls and forms. The new one, with clear service pages, prices, reviews and a click-to-call button, converts 4%. On 1,200 visits a month, that’s 24 extra leads. If 40% book and the average job is $650, the difference is roughly 10 jobs and $6,000 a month. A $10,000 site pays back in under two months on those assumptions.

Change the assumptions to your own. If your business lives on referrals and the site gets 150 visits a month, the math says buy the cheaper site and spend the difference elsewhere. If you run Google Ads, the math gets more lopsided, because every click you pay for lands on the site. Doubling the conversion rate halves your cost per lead without changing the ad budget. Frequently asked questions

How much should a website cost for a plumber or contractor in The Woodlands?

Most contractors in The Woodlands and Houston spend $6,000 to $18,000 on a site that actually generates calls. That budget covers a page for each main service, pages for the towns you serve such as Spring, Conroe and Tomball, written copy, real photos, click-to-call, quote forms and call tracking. A cheaper template can work for a new company that runs on referrals, but it rarely ranks.

How much does it cost to maintain a website per month?

Plan on roughly $25 to $150 a month for hosting or a platform plan, about $15 to $25 a year for a .com domain, and $50 to $500 a month if you pay someone for updates, security and small edits. WordPress sites need more upkeep than hosted builders like Squarespace or Shopify. Add any paid plugins or apps, which often run $5 to $50 each per month.

Is it cheaper to build a website on Wix or hire a web designer in Houston?

Building it yourself on Wix, Squarespace or Shopify is cheaper in cash, usually a few hundred dollars a year. It costs time, and most DIY sites underperform on copy, speed and local SEO. Hiring a Houston designer costs more up front but should come with written copy, service and area pages and tracking. If your customers find you through Google, the paid site usually earns back the difference.

Do I have to pay sales tax on a website in Texas?

Usually. The Texas Comptroller treats web hosting and website creation and maintenance as data processing, a taxable service, with 20 percent of the charge exempt. Design that only produces a plan or blueprint is not taxable under the 2025 rule. With the 8.25 percent combined rate common around Houston, tax on a $10,000 build can be about $660. Confirm treatment of your contract with a CPA.

Who should own my domain and website when I hire a designer?

You should. Register the domain in your name with your own email and card, and make sure the contract says you own the finished site, content and photos, with logins handed over at launch. If the site runs on the designer's hosting account, ask how you would move it if you leave. Ownership problems are the most common reason Houston owners end up paying for a site twice.
