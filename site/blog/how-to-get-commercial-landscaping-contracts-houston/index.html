To get commercial landscaping contracts in North Houston, pick the buyers who fit your crew size, learn when each one bids, and be ready before the bid opens. That means insurance certificates, references, a capability page and a site walk-through habit. The buyers here are property management companies, HOAs, municipal utility districts, The Woodlands Township, cities and school districts. Each one has its own rules and calendar.

Most landscapers who move into commercial work can already price a job. Where they lose is earlier: they never get invited, or they hear about the bid after it closes. A Texas HOA generally has to take bids on service contracts over $50,000, and The Woodlands Township took bids in spring 2026 for streetscape work that starts in January 2027. Dates and rules like those decide who’s in the running, so that’s where this guide spends its time.

How to get commercial landscaping contracts: know who signs the check

“Commercial” covers very different buyers. A retail center owner can sign a contract in a week. A school district might take four months and a board vote. Here’s how they compare:

The HOA rule comes from Texas Property Code section 209.0052, which requires an association contracting for services over $50,000 to “solicit bids or proposals using a bid process established by the association” (developer-controlled associations are exempt). The $100,000 thresholds for cities, counties and school districts come from SB 1173, which raised them from $50,000 starting September 1, 2025. We’ll cover the MUD rule below.

A rule of thumb: start with the private buyers if you have one or two crews. Public bids reward companies that already have references, insurance and paperwork ready.

HOAs and property management companies

For most landscapers, this is where commercial growth starts. Master-planned communities across Spring, Cypress, Tomball and Conroe have common areas, entry monuments, detention ponds and amenity centers that need weekly care.

The person to know is the community manager, not the board. Management companies in Houston often run dozens of associations, so one good relationship can lead to several bids. What a manager wants most is a contractor who shows up when promised and never makes them look bad in front of the board.

How to get on the bid list

  1. Make a list of the management companies that run communities inside your service area. HOA websites and resale certificates usually name them.
  2. Ask for 15 minutes with the community manager. Bring a one-page capability statement and photos of comparable sites.
  3. Offer a free site walk with a written observation report: dead plant material, irrigation leaks, drainage issues. That’s useful to them even if they don’t switch.
  4. Show up where managers meet. Industry groups for community association managers and commercial property managers hold local events in Houston. Sponsoring a single chapter breakfast puts you in a room with more managers than a month of cold email will reach.
  5. Follow up when the budget is being built. Many HOA boards approve next year’s budget in the fall, so contract changes often get discussed between August and November.

MUDs: the buyer most landscapers forget

If you’ve bought a house in North Houston, you’ve probably paid a MUD tax. A municipal utility district is a political subdivision of Texas, authorized by the TCEQ, that provides water, sewer and drainage to a development, as a JBGoodwin explainer puts it. An elected board runs it, and many MUDs also maintain parks, trails, detention ponds and landscaped rights-of-way.

They hire landscapers. one Harris County MUD’s June 2026 agenda shows the board discussing ongoing landscaping matters under an existing maintenance contract that expires October 31, 2026 and has a 30-day notice requirement for changes or termination. Contracts like that come up for review regularly, and a short notice period means a board that’s unhappy can switch quickly.

The bidding rules are set in the Water Code. After HB 3507 took effect in 2023, district contracts covered by Section 49.273 need written competitive bids from at least three bidders when they’re over $25,000, and advertised bids above $150,000. Many landscape contracts fall in that middle band, so the district will be looking for three qualified bidders. Be one of them.

How to get in front of MUDs:

  • Board meetings are public and agendas are posted. Read the agendas for districts in your area and look for landscape or maintenance items.
  • MUDs lean on their operators, engineers and attorneys. Those firms often recommend contractors. Get to know them.
  • Attend a meeting before you bid. Boards remember who showed up.

Public bids: The Woodlands Township, cities, ISDs and the ESBD

Public work takes more paperwork, but it’s the most open door in commercial landscaping. Anyone who qualifies can bid, and the rules are written down.

The Woodlands Township. The Woodlands Township’s bid page lists its solicitations, with notices and documents as PDFs and a “Sign-Up for Updates” link for email alerts. In 2026 it bid Streetscape and Town Center Maintenance Services, covering village and neighborhood entries, right-of-way mowing, litter control and Town Center streetscape and waterway maintenance. According to a summary of the 2027-2029 streetscape RFB, the contract runs 36 months, from January 1, 2027 through December 31, 2029. The bids were due in spring 2026, about eight months before the work starts. Miss that window and the next chance may be three years away.

Cities. City of Conroe’s 2026 annual mowing RFP is a useful example. It was posted June 25, held a pre-proposal meeting July 2 at the Public Works training room, and took proposals through OpenGov Procurement on July 16. Scoring was 60 points for cost, 25 for experience and qualifications, 10 for references and 5 for prior city experience. It also asked for a certificate of insurance and Form 1295, among other items.

School districts. Conroe ISD’s purchasing page says vendors must register through its eBid system to respond to RFPs, registration is free, and registered vendors are notified automatically. It also notes that registering doesn’t make you an approved vendor; you still have to win a bid. Most Houston-area ISDs work the same way, so register with each district you want to serve.

State agencies. Under state purchasing rules, state agencies post solicitations estimated over $25,000 on the Electronic State Business Daily (ESBD). That matters if you want state facilities or universities. Cities, MUDs and ISDs usually post on their own portals, so you’ll need to watch both.

Build a bid calendar

Most landscapers who lose public and HOA work don’t lose on price. They find out about the bid after it closes.

A bid calendar fixes that. It’s just a spreadsheet with every buyer you want, their contract end date, when they bid and where they post. Fill it in once, then check it every Monday.

Where to find the dates:

  • Public entities. Bid tabulations and award notices list the contract term. Count back six to nine months from the end date and that’s roughly when the next bid will post.
  • MUDs. Board agendas and minutes mention contract renewals and expirations.
  • HOAs and property managers. Ask directly: “When does your current landscape contract renew?” Most will tell you.
  • Office and retail. Leases and management changes trigger new contracts. A center that just changed hands is worth a call.

Sign up for email alerts on every portal, including the Township, each city’s OpenGov or bid page, each ISD eBid system and the ESBD. Then put a reminder on your calendar 30 days before each expected bid date to prep paperwork.

How to get more commercial landscape accounts from office parks and retail

Private commercial work along I-45, the Grand Parkway and Research Forest Drive is relationship-driven. There’s rarely a public RFP. The facilities manager calls three companies they know.

So the job is getting known before they need you.

  • Drive the corridor. Make a list of office parks, medical buildings, retail centers and churches between the Hughes Landing area and the Grand Parkway. Note who looks under-maintained.
  • Find the manager on LinkedIn. Search “property manager” or “facilities manager” plus The Woodlands, Spring or Conroe. Connect with a short note that names the property, not a pitch.
  • Lead with an observation. “Noticed the irrigation at your east entrance has been running in the rain” is a better opener than a brochure.
  • Offer a walk-through. A 20-minute site walk with the manager is where commercial contracts are won. Bring a tablet, take photos, and send a written summary the same day.
  • Post your commercial work. Photos of a property you maintain, with permission, on LinkedIn and your Google Business Profile.

The capability statement page your prospects will check

Before a property manager invites you to bid, they’ll look you up. If your website is all residential mowing specials, you look small.

Build one page, at a URL like /commercial/, that works as your capability statement:

  • Types of properties you maintain, with photos of each
  • Crew count, equipment and service area map
  • Licenses: TDA pesticide license and TCEQ irrigator license holders on staff, if you have them
  • Insurance summary and a line saying COIs are available on request
  • Three references with names and roles (with their permission)
  • A downloadable one-page PDF version
  • A short form asking for property type, size and contract end date

Insurance, licenses and references commercial buyers ask for

Commercial buyers will want proof before they sign. Expect requests like these:

  • Insurance. a Heart of Texas Council of Governments grounds RFP in Waco required $2 million in general liability, $1 million in auto liability and workers’ compensation, plus proof of bonding and insurance for all employees. Local requirements vary, so read every RFP and talk to your agent before the bid is due.
  • Licenses. Any herbicide or pesticide work for hire needs a license from the Texas Department of Agriculture. Irrigation repair or service needs a TCEQ license under TCEQ’s landscape irrigation rules (RG-470).
  • References. That same RFP asked for at least three references for similar services. Collect them now, from your best residential and small commercial clients.
  • Form 1295. In Texas, Form 1295 applies to government contracts that need a governing body vote or are worth at least $1 million. You fill it out through the Texas Ethics Commission’s online filing system.
  • Scope detail. That RFP spelled out at least 38 mowings a year, quarterly sprinkler testing and pre-emergent in February and September. Expect that level of detail and price every line.

Scoring varies too. Conroe’s mowing RFP put 60 points on cost; the Waco-area RFP put only 40 on cost and 35 on demonstrated performance. Read the scoring and spend your effort where the points are.

Commercial landscaping bids: an RFP response checklist

Use this before you submit anything:

  1. Read the whole package, including every addendum. Large public bids like the Township’s streetscape contract often issue more than one.
  2. Attend the pre-bid meeting or site walk, even if it’s optional.
  3. Submit questions before the deadline, in writing.
  4. Match your pricing to the bid form exactly. Don’t add line items they didn’t ask for.
  5. Include every required form: COI, Form 1295 if needed, signed addenda, references.
  6. Write a short cover letter that restates the scope and names your account manager.
  7. Submit a day early through the right portal. Late is disqualified.
  8. After the award, request the bid tabulation and see where you landed.

If you win, the first 90 days decide the renewal. Send a monthly report with photos, keep a single point of contact, and fix complaints within 48 hours.

Your first move doesn’t need a bid package. Open a spreadsheet tonight and add five buyers: two management companies, one MUD, the Township and the ISD closest to your yard. Frequently asked questions

How do I get commercial lawn care contracts?

Start with property management companies and HOAs in your service area, since they buy often and decide faster than public agencies. Meet community managers, offer free site walks, and build a commercial page with insurance, licenses and references. Then register on city, ISD and Township bid portals and track contract end dates so you see bids before they close.

How do I get commercial contracts for landscaping with no experience?

Begin with small commercial properties like churches, medical offices and small retail centers, where a manager can hire you without a formal RFP. Ask your best residential customers to be references. Do a few jobs well, photograph them, and use them to qualify for HOA and MUD work. Public bids usually come later, once you have references and insurance.

Does an HOA in Texas have to take bids for landscaping?

Generally yes for larger contracts. Texas Property Code section 209.0052 says an association that proposes to contract for services costing more than 50,000 dollars must solicit bids or proposals using its own bid process. Associations still under developer control are exempt during the development period. Smaller contracts can be awarded at the board's discretion.

What insurance do I need for commercial landscaping bids?

Expect general liability, auto liability and workers' compensation, plus a certificate of insurance naming the client. One Texas grounds RFP required 2 million dollars in general liability and 1 million in auto liability. Requirements vary by buyer, so check each RFP and confirm coverage with your agent before the bid is due.

Where can I find landscaping bids in Houston?

Check The Woodlands Township bid page, each city's procurement portal such as Conroe's OpenGov page, and each school district's eBid system. State agencies post solicitations over 25,000 dollars on the Electronic State Business Daily. MUD board agendas and HOA managers are the best source for private and district work, which rarely appears on public portals.
