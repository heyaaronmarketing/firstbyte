How to Get More Dog Grooming Clients in The Woodlands and Houston

It’s 7:40 on a Tuesday. A groomer in Spring has three texts asking about Saturday, a doodle in the tub that’s matted to the skin, and a calendar that’s full until the week before Thanksgiving. She’s also paying for a Facebook boost every month because someone told her she should “always be marketing.”

She doesn’t need the boost. If you want more dog grooming clients in The Woodlands, Spring, Conroe and Houston, the order of operations is boring and it works: get your Google Business Profile right for a salon or a van, prebook every dog before it leaves, ask for a review every day, and put real prices on your website. Paid ads come last. Only buy them when you have empty slots to sell.

We rarely meet a groomer with a demand problem. What we see is a full Saturday and a dead Tuesday, regulars who drift to eleven weeks between visits, or a van profile that tells Google it’s a storefront in somebody’s cul-de-sac. All of that gets covered below, along with the parts that only apply to our corner of Texas.

The demand is real. APPA’s May 2026 industry release puts dog ownership at 53% of U.S. households, about 71 million homes, and AVMA’s pet ownership data counted a record 89.7 million pet dogs in 2024. Locally, the Census Bureau estimates Montgomery County grew 25.9% between April 2020 and July 2025, to 781,194 people. Every one of those new subdivisions off the Grand Parkway seems to come with a goldendoodle.

First, count your empty grooming slots

Before you spend a dollar, do the capacity math. It takes ten minutes.

Say you run a two-table salon in The Woodlands. Each groomer finishes six or seven dogs a day, five days a week. That’s roughly 60 to 70 grooms a week. If your average regular comes back every six weeks, that schedule supports something like 360 to 420 active dogs. Now count the dogs you groomed in the last 90 days. Close to that number? Then new clients aren’t your problem, and what you need is a waitlist or a price increase (or a third groomer, if you can find one who’ll stay).

Well under it means the gaps are hiding somewhere specific. Grooming demand is lumpy: Saturdays and the two weeks before Christmas fill themselves, while Tuesday afternoons in late January sit there like an empty parking lot.

  • Pull a slot report from MoeGo, Gingr, DaySmart or whatever you book in, covering the last 12 weeks by day and time.
  • Mark the chronic holes. Usually that’s early-week afternoons, plus the first two weeks of January and late August, when families are back on school routines and the budget is tight.
  • Count your lapsed clients, meaning dogs whose last groom was more than 12 weeks ago. Owners always guess low on this one. It’s also the only list you can market to for free.

A mobile groomer does the same math with one more variable: drive time. Six dogs a day in one neighborhood is a great day. Six dogs spread from Magnolia to Kingwood is a bad one, no matter how much each paid.

Google Business Profile setup for a grooming salon vs a mobile grooming van

Mobile groomers lose more ground here than anywhere else, and it takes about an afternoon to fix.

Google treats a business that serves customers at its address differently from one that goes to the customer. Per Google’s service-area rules, you can list up to 20 service areas, the outer boundary shouldn’t be more than about two hours of driving from your base, and if you don’t serve customers at your address you should remove it from the profile. A mobile van parked at your house in Conroe is a service-area business. Leave the home address visible and you’re inviting a suspension, and strangers in your driveway.

Then fill in what most groomers skip, starting with services and short descriptions (bath, full groom, deshed, puppy intro, nail trim, teeth), photos of real dogs with owner permission, and a booking link. Can pet groomers use Google Local Services Ads?

Yes, on paper. “Pet grooming” is on Google’s Local Services Ads category list, along with pet boarding, pet training and veterinarians. Local Services Ads are the boxes at the very top of some searches, and you pay per lead (a call or message) instead of per click.

Two honest caveats. Google says the program is only available for certain categories in certain places, so the real test is entering your business and ZIP at signup and seeing whether grooming comes up for you. And we haven’t seen enough grooming accounts in this market to quote you a typical cost per lead with a straight face. Pet LSAs are newer and thinner than the plumbing and HVAC ones most published numbers come from.

If it’s available in your area, we’d test it before regular Google Ads. Keep the weekly budget small and answer every lead within minutes. You’ll get some junk (job seekers, the occasional lost-dog call), and those are worth disputing. Our breakdown of Local Services Ads vs Google Ads was written for home services, but the mechanics are the same.

Rebooking: the 4-to-8-week schedule that does most of your marketing

The cheapest grooming client you will ever get is the one already standing at your counter.

Every dog should leave with the next appointment on the calendar. Not “we’ll text you.” Booked.

The interval depends on coat and lifestyle, and your groomers know it better than any chart we could draw. Roughly:

  • Doodles, poodles and poodle mixes: 4 to 6 weeks. Push it to 10 and you’re shaving out mats, and the owner blames you for the short haircut.
  • Shih Tzus, Yorkies, Maltese and other drop-coat small breeds: 4 to 6 weeks, often with a face-and-feet tidy in between.
  • Goldens, Huskies, Aussies and other double coats: 6 to 8 weeks for a bath and deshed, tighter in shedding season.
  • Labs, Boxers and short coats: 8 to 12 weeks for a bath, nails and ears.

Write the interval into the client record and let the software do the nagging, with a reminder three days out and another the morning before. Some people will still walk out without booking. They get a “time for Biscuit’s next groom?” text at the breed interval, then a second one two weeks later, and if 12 weeks pass with nothing, they move to a lapsed list that gets one friendly note and an open slot. Send a confirmation text the same day for every booking, too, because people forget what they agreed to at the counter while holding the leash of a dog who wants to leave.

Prebook rate is the one number we’d want every salon manager to know by heart. Moving it from 50% to 75% does more than most ad budgets will.

Shedding season, shave-down season and the Houston grooming calendar

The American Kennel Club says the heaviest shedding usually happens in spring and fall, and that heavy shedders may need grooming more often for a while. In Houston, “spring” can mean late February. The first 80-degree week sends every Golden owner in Creekside Park looking for a deshed, usually on the same Saturday. A rough year, planned ahead:

  • February: email and text double-coat owners about a deshed package before the hair is on their couch. Open extra deshed slots.
  • May to June: brace for shave-down requests from Husky and Pyrenees owners trying to beat the heat. Decide your policy on shaving double coats in advance, put it on the website, and give the front desk one script for explaining it. That policy page will save you a few bad reviews.
  • July and August: mobile groomers, this is when a van with strong AC and a generator is a selling point. Say so in your photos and your profile.
  • October: open the holiday book. Thanksgiving and Christmas week grooms sell out every year. Tell regulars first, then the public.
  • January: your slowest stretch. Send win-back texts to lapsed clients and run new-puppy offers. Resist the urge to panic-buy ads.

Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30, and a storm week cancels a lot of appointments at once. We’d keep a short list of flexible clients (retirees, work-from-home people, the guy with three Shelties) who’ll take a slot on a day’s notice once the weather clears.

Dog grooming marketing ideas that work in our neighborhoods

People pick a groomer the way they pick a pediatrician. A neighbor said she was good, and the shop is close. So put your effort where neighbors talk to each other.

Nextdoor and neighborhood Facebook groups

Claim your free Nextdoor Business Page and ask your happiest clients for a recommendation there. When someone in Sterling Ridge or Alden Bridge posts “who grooms doodles without shaving them bald?”, recommendations from real neighbors do the selling. Don’t plug yourself from a personal account; post from the business page. Our full take on whether Nextdoor advertising is worth it covers the paid side, and grooming is one of the categories where it tends to fit.

Neighborhood Facebook groups work the same way, with stricter admins. Use the weekly business thread and let clients do the tagging.

The van is your billboard

A van parked in a driveway for 90 minutes gets seen by every dog walker on the street. Wrap it with the business name, phone number, a short web address and the words “mobile dog grooming,” big enough to read from across a cul-de-sac. We’ve seen gorgeous wraps with a script logo nobody could make out at 30 feet. Pretty, and useless.

Referral partners

Vets who don’t groom, trainers, dog daycares without a grooming room, and the pet supply stores on FM 1488 or along Kuykendahl all see your future clients first. Drop off cards, offer to groom their staff dogs once, and ask whether they’d hand out a new-puppy card. It’s slow and it costs almost nothing.

Photos that look like here

A before-and-after of a matted rescue is the most-shared post a groomer will ever make. Get written permission from the owner, keep the dog the hero, and skip stock photos entirely. If you want help planning that kind of content, see our post on UGC vs influencers.

Reviews: how many to ask for, and how to ask without breaking Google’s rules

Ask every client. That’s the whole strategy, and it’s also the rule.

Google’s review policy says businesses can’t offer incentives for reviews (no “$5 off your next groom”), can’t discourage negative reviews, and can’t selectively ask only the happy customers. Review-gating tools that send unhappy clients to a private form and happy ones to Google are exactly what that rule describes. The FTC has its own rule on fake and incentivized reviews too; our fake reviews rule guide lays it out.

The ask that works for groomers is a text sent an hour after pickup, with the after photo attached: “Biscuit looked so good today! If you have a minute, a Google review helps us a lot: [link].” People love a photo of their own dog, and plenty of them will post it before they get around to the review link. Some will do both.

Reply to every review. A short haircut on a matted doodle will eventually earn you a bad one, and when it does, explain the coat condition and the options you offered without arguing. Future clients read your reply more carefully than the complaint.

Should groomers post prices online?

Yes. We’d put this near the top of the list, and most groomers hate hearing it.

Sure, every dog is different. But a person comparing three groomers on their phone at 9 p.m. will call the one whose website gives them a range and skip the two that say “call for pricing.” Hidden prices also produce the angriest conversations you’ll have all week, the ones at pickup.

Here’s the page layout we’d use:

  1. List bath and full groom prices by size (small, medium, large, giant) as “starting at” numbers.
  2. Explain what moves the price: coat type, matting, behavior, time since last groom.
  3. Name the add-ons with prices: deshed, teeth, nail grind, de-matting by the quarter hour.
  4. For mobile, say whether there’s a trip fee and which ZIPs it covers.
  5. Put the matting and shave-down policy on the same page.

If you run ads, that’s the page they should land on. In our experience a short, honest pricing page beats a beautiful homepage for bookings, and our landing pages 101 post explains why.

When not to advertise your grooming business

An agency telling you to spend less is a little odd. Bear with us.

If your groomers are booked four weeks out and you’re turning away new clients, stop paying for ads. Every new client you can’t fit is a bad first impression and possibly a bad review. Spend that money on a bather, a better dryer, or a raise for the groomer you can’t afford to lose.

Run a real waitlist. A simple form collecting name, dog, breed, ZIP and preferred days is enough, and when a regular cancels, the slot goes to the waitlist first. The day you hire, that list becomes your launch campaign, already paid for.

Got slots to fill? Start small. For a single salon, we’d test Google search ads on terms like “dog groomer near me”, “dog grooming the woodlands” and “mobile dog grooming spring tx” with a tight radius and a budget around $15 to $30 a day (our working range, not a benchmark). In the location settings, choose “Presence” over the default “Presence or interest” (Google Ads location options explains the difference) so you aren’t paying for people in Dallas researching Houston groomers. Add negatives for “jobs”, “school”, “certification” and “DIY”. Our Google Ads team can set this up.

We’d use Meta ads in grooming for exactly one job, which is selling puppy intro grooms to new dog owners within a few miles. A 20-second video of a nervous puppy getting a gentle first bath beats any discount we’ve tried writing. Our paid social page covers how we run those.

One more trust point. Texas doesn’t license dog groomers statewide; as News 4 San Antonio reported, anyone in the state can become a groomer without formal training or licensing (El Paso passed its own ordinance). Owners know this, which is why certifications, insurance and a clear safety policy belong on your site. And if you operate inside Houston city limits, check whether you need the city’s Commercial Pet Facility Permit, which covers facilities keeping dogs or cats for a commercial purpose and explicitly includes mobile pet service businesses. Outside the city, ask your own city or county; rules vary, and we’re marketers, not lawyers.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more clients for my dog grooming business?

Start with what you already have. Prebook every dog before it leaves, text lapsed clients who haven't been in for 12 weeks, and ask every client for a Google review with the after photo. Then set up your Business Profile correctly, post prices online and get recommended on Nextdoor. Paid ads come last, and only when you have open slots to fill.

How do mobile dog groomers advertise?

Mobile groomers do best with a Google Business Profile set up as a service-area business with the home address hidden, a clearly wrapped van, Nextdoor recommendations and tight Google search ads covering only the ZIP codes on their route. Keep the area small. Route density matters more than reach, because drive time between appointments eats your profit.

Do Google Local Services Ads work for dog groomers?

Pet grooming appears on Google's Local Services Ads category list, so it can work, but availability depends on your location. Check by entering your business and ZIP code at signup. If it's offered, test it with a small weekly budget, respond to leads within minutes and dispute leads that aren't grooming requests.

How often should dogs be groomed?

It depends on the coat. Doodles, poodles and drop-coat small breeds usually need a full groom every 4 to 6 weeks. Double-coated breeds like Goldens and Huskies do well with a bath and deshed every 6 to 8 weeks, more often during spring and fall shedding. Short-coated dogs can go 8 to 12 weeks for a bath, nails and ears.

Do you need a license to groom dogs in Texas?

Texas has no statewide license for dog groomers, though some cities set their own rules; El Paso passed a groomer ordinance. Inside Houston city limits, a Commercial Pet Facility Permit from BARC may apply, and it includes mobile pet service businesses. Check with your own city or county before opening or adding a van.

Should I advertise if my grooming schedule is already full?

No. If you're booked weeks out and turning people away, ads create clients you can't serve and reviews you don't want. Start a waitlist, raise prices if demand supports it, or hire. Restart advertising when you have a new groomer or regular open slots you can name by day and time.
