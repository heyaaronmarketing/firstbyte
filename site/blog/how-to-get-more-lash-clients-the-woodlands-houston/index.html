It’s 9:40 on a Tuesday night and your DMs say “how much for a full set?” for the fourth time today. Two of those people will never answer your reply. One will book the $79 new-client special, show up, love it, and never come back for a fill. The fourth might become a regular. You just can’t tell which one yet.

Most of how to get more lash clients is boring. Be easy to find for people within a 15-minute drive, and put your prices and policies up before anyone has to DM for them. Then build every offer around the second visit, because a lash studio lives on fills. The full set is what it costs to get her in the chair. The fills every two to three weeks are the business.

We work with appointment-based businesses around The Woodlands, Spring and north Houston, and this is the playbook we’d hand a solo lash artist in a salon suite or a four-chair lash and brow studio off Woodlands Parkway. Much of it is plumbing (booking links, tracking, the Business Profile fields nobody bothers with), which is less fun than shooting a Reel and pays better.

What one lash client is actually worth

Before you spend a dollar on ads, run the math on one client. Most techs think in appointments. Think in people for a minute.

Say your classic full set is $180 and a fill is $75, booked every three weeks. A client who stays a full year comes in roughly 17 times: the set plus about 16 fills. That’s around $1,380 a year from one person, before brow services, tips or the friend she sends you. A client who comes in once on a half-price coupon is worth $90 and a chair you could have given to someone else.

That gap is why we distrust offers built around the first visit. If you can raise your 90-day return rate from, say, 45% of new clients to 65%, you’ll make more money than you would by doubling your ad budget, and the only cost is a little process.

When we say “more lash clients” in this post, we mean more clients who come back for a fill inside 21 days.

Do you need a license to do lash extensions in Texas?

Yes. Put it on your website and booking page too, because clients do ask, usually right after a bad experience somewhere else.

Texas regulates lash work through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR). The eyelash extension specialist curriculum is 320 clock hours (80 hours of theory, 240 of practice) under 16 TAC §83.202, and the license requires passing written and practical exams. A licensed esthetician (750 hours) or cosmetology operator (1,000 hours) can also apply extensions. TDLR’s TDLR’s Barbering and Cosmetology at a Glance sheet lists the specialty license fee at $50 and shows 418,635 barbering and cosmetology licensees in fiscal 2025, which tells you how crowded the field is.

Here’s the part people miss. TDLR’s scope-of-practice page for eyelash extension specialists says an eyelash extension specialist “cannot perform any other barbering or cosmetology services” beyond extensions. If your menu lists lash lifts, lash tints, brow lamination or brow tints, those are separate services, and TDLR’s page for extension specialists doesn’t cover them. In practice studios run them under an esthetician or cosmetology license. Confirm with TDLR before you advertise a service your license may not cover, because an ad is a public record of what you offered.

Microblading and permanent makeup are a different agency

TDLR’s own “who regulates what” page lists microblading under the Texas Department of State Health Services and the Texas Medical Board, and permanent makeup under DSHS. Texas DSHS requires a tattoo studio license for any business putting pigment under the skin, and its page says that “includes studios that perform traditional tattooing, permanent cosmetics, microblading and scarification.” Microblading is legal in Texas, but a lash salon with a cosmetology establishment license can’t simply add it to the menu. If you do offer it, put the DSHS license where people can see it.

Watch the words you use about tints and glue

The FDA’s eye cosmetic safety page warns that permanent lash and brow tints and dyes “have been known to cause serious eye injuries, including blindness,” and it only allows a narrow silver nitrate exception for professional use. So don’t write “FDA approved lash tint” in a caption. FDA treats lash extensions and their adhesives as cosmetics. Post your patch-test policy and glue-sensitivity options. Clients with touchy eyes are looking for exactly that, and it won’t draw a letter from anybody.

How to get more lash clients on Instagram and TikTok

Instagram is still where lash clients go to vet you. They found you on Google or through a friend, and then they open your grid to decide. TikTok is better for being discovered by people who weren’t looking for you yet. Treat them differently.

What books appointments on Instagram, in our experience:

  • Eyes-open shots in daylight. The ring-light close-up with eyes shut looks great to other lash artists, but clients want to see how a set looks on a face at Market Street on a Saturday. Window light, eyes open, slight angle.
  • The two-week retention photo. Ask a regular if you can photograph her at a fill. Lashes that still look full at day 14 are the best proof of skill you have, and almost nobody posts them.
  • Pinned posts with prices, fill policy and how to book, so the DM never has to happen.
  • Your town in the name field. Instagram search reads profile names and captions, so “Lash extensions, The Woodlands” helps someone scrolling in Alden Bridge find you.

TikTok rewards talking. A tech explaining why her client’s lashes fell out after a different salon used too-heavy volume fans will outperform another pretty “after” shot. Nobody needs you to dance. A point of view and a decent mic will do.

A word on paid social. Meta’s health and wellness ad standards say ads must not “imply or attempt to generate negative self-perception,” and cosmetic ads must target people 18 and older. So “Tired of thin, sparse lashes?” is the kind of headline that gets rejected or quietly limited. “Soft hybrid sets in The Woodlands, fills from $75” is fine and frankly converts better. If you run Meta ads, run them to a booking page or a short landing page, not to your profile, and start around $10 to $20 a day inside a 6 to 10 mile radius. Our paid social team sets these up for appointment businesses all the time, and we’d still tell you to get organic working first.

Google Business Profile for a lash and brow studio

When someone in Spring types “lash extensions near me,” the map pack decides who gets the first look. Your Business Profile is how you get into it, and most lash profiles we look at are half empty.

Pick your primary category carefully. For most studios that’s “Eyelash salon.” Add secondary categories only for services you actually perform under the right license; a brow category on a lash-only license is a mismatch waiting for a complaint. Then fill the services list with real prices and durations, because Google uses that text to decide which searches you’re relevant for.

Google lets you add booking links to your profile, and its Google’s help page on booking links says you can add up to 10 links per category. Use one, point it at your real booking page, and tag it (more on that below). If your booking software is one of Google’s integrated providers, a “Book” button may show up on its own. Check that it goes where you want.

If you work out of a salon suite building, list the suite number in the address. Clients circling a parking lot off Research Forest at 7 a.m. will mention it in a review if they can’t find you. And if you work from home and don’t want your address public, set the profile as a service-area business only if you really travel to clients; otherwise you’ll need to show where people come.

Post a photo every week. Google doesn’t publish a magic frequency and we won’t pretend to know one, but it takes five minutes and a stale profile looks closed.

Track your booking link or you’re guessing

Ask most lash artists where new clients come from and you’ll hear “Instagram, mostly.” Then we look at the booking software and half the new clients say “Google” or “friend.” Feelings are a terrible budget tool.

The fix is free. Give every place your booking link lives its own tagged version using UTM parameters, the little bits of text after a question mark that analytics tools read. For example:

  • Instagram bio: yourbookingpage.com/?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=bio
  • Google Business Profile: ?utm_source=google&utm_medium=gbp
  • TikTok bio: ?utm_source=tiktok&utm_medium=bio
  • Meta ads: ?utm_source=meta&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=newclient

Now the honest caveat. Some booking platforms pass those tags into their reports and some don’t, and many open in an app or a pop-up that breaks tracking. We’ve seen both. So we also add a required “How did you find us?” question to the new-client form with fixed options (Instagram, TikTok, Google search, Google Maps, friend, walked by). It isn’t perfect. Combined with the UTM data, it’s close enough to decide where your next $300 goes.

Once a month, pull two numbers by source: new clients, and how many of them came back for a first fill within 21 days. Twenty bargain hunters from one source lose to eight regulars from another, and you’ll only spot that if you’re tracking the second visit.

Pricing pages that filter for regulars

Hiding prices to “get them on the phone” works for roofers. It doesn’t work for lashes. People comparing studios on their phone at 10 p.m. skip the ones that make them DM for a number.

A good lash pricing page sets expectations and spells out the fill rules. It also, quietly, turns away the people who were only ever coming once. Here’s the layout we’d use (the prices are placeholders, so swap in yours):

The “half remaining” rule is common in the industry, though studios set their own threshold. Whatever yours is, write it down, because one of the most common complaints in lash reviews reads like “she charged me for a full set at my fill.” It’s almost always a policy that lived only in the tech’s head.

Put your deposit and no-show policy on the same page. A $25 to $50 deposit cuts no-shows sharply and tells people you run an actual business. In our experience, whoever argues about a fair deposit is the same person who’d have no-showed.

We’d also show the maintenance cost right there on the page, something like “Most clients spend about $75 to $95 every 3 weeks to keep a full look.” Anyone who flinches at that wasn’t going to be a regular anyway.

New-client offers that don’t attract one-time bargain hunters

The 50%-off full set is the most popular lash promotion we see in Houston and the worst for retention. It trains people to shop for the next 50%-off full set. Some of them will hop to a new studio every month, and there are enough lash studios between Tomball and Kingwood to let them.

What we’ve watched hold up better:

  • Full-price set, discounted first fill. “New clients: your first fill is $45 if you book it before you leave.” The discount only pays out if they come back, which is the whole point.
  • A complimentary touch-up inside 7 days, which makes trying you less risky and gets you a second look at your own work.
  • Referral credit after the friend’s first fill: $20 each for the referrer and the friend, but only once the friend returns. That one line in the terms does most of the filtering.
  • A lash lift as the way in. It’s cheaper and shorter, and some lift clients move up to extensions once they trust you.

We’d skip Groupon-style deal sites for lashes. They fill a slow Wednesday with people who came for the price, and the platform takes a cut on top of the discount.

And book the fill before the client leaves the chair. Obvious, sure. But ask studio owners what share of new clients pre-book and most can’t tell you, and we’d take that number over a follower count any day.

Reviews: when to ask and what not to offer

Reviews do two jobs for a lash studio. They push you up the map pack, and they answer the safety question (“will this hurt my natural lashes?”) better than you can.

Two rules first, because we see both broken all the time. Google’s review policy says businesses may not “offer incentives, such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services” in exchange for reviews, and may not “selectively solicit positive reviews.” So no “$10 off your fill for a five-star review,” and no texting only the clients you know are happy. Our guide to the FTC fake reviews rule covers the federal side.

Timing matters more than the ask. Right after the first set, the client hasn’t lived with the lashes yet. By her first fill she knows how they held up through a humid week, a workout, maybe a pool day in July. Ask then. Something like:

“So glad they held up! If you have a minute, a Google review helps other people in The Woodlands find me. Here’s the link.”

Send the link by text with your Business Profile’s review URL so it’s one tap. Reply to every review, and use the service name naturally in your reply (“Thanks for coming in for your hybrid fill”). For a longer process, see our complete guide to Google reviews.

Should a lash studio run Google Ads?

Sometimes. For a solo artist with a full book, no. For a studio with three techs and open chairs on weekdays, a small search campaign is often the fastest way to fill them, because people searching “lash extensions near me” or “lash lift the woodlands” want an appointment this week.

How we’d set one up:

  1. Search campaign only, no Performance Max to start. You want to see the search terms.
  2. Target a radius around the studio. For a studio near Hughes Landing that might be 6 to 8 miles, enough to pull from Shenandoah, Oak Ridge North and parts of Spring without paying for clicks from Katy.
  3. Keywords around “lash extensions,” “eyelash extensions,” “lash lift,” “brow lamination” plus your town names.
  4. Negatives from day one: course, training, certification, classes, jobs, kit, glue, DIY, wholesale, Amazon. Plenty of lash searches come from people shopping for training, and none of them want a fill.
  5. Send the click to a page with prices and a booking button, not your homepage.
  6. Budget $15 to $30 a day for a month, then judge it on booked new clients and return rate, not clicks.

Frequently asked questions

How do lash techs get clients fast?

The fastest route is usually your existing circle plus Google. Finish your Google Business Profile with real prices and a booking link, ask every current client for a review at her fill, and offer a referral credit that pays out after the friend's first fill. Post eyes-open daylight photos on Instagram with your town in the profile name. Ads can speed things up once your booking page shows prices.

Do you need a license to do lash extensions in Texas?

Yes. TDLR licenses eyelash extension specialists, which requires a 320-hour approved program and written and practical exams. Licensed estheticians and cosmetology operators can also apply extensions. The specialist license covers extensions only, so lash lifts, tints and brow services generally need an esthetician or cosmetology license. Confirm your scope with TDLR before adding services to your menu or your ads.

How often should lash clients come in for fills?

Most studios book fills every two to three weeks, because natural lashes shed and take extensions with them. Many studios also set a minimum, such as about half the lashes still in place, before a visit counts as a fill instead of a new full set. Whatever your rule is, publish it on your pricing page and booking confirmation so nobody is surprised at checkout.

Should I offer discounts to get new lash clients?

Discount the second visit, not the first. A half-price full set attracts people who shop by price and rarely return. A full-price set with a discounted first fill, a short complimentary touch-up window, or a referral credit that only pays after the friend returns all reward the behavior you want. Track how many new clients book a fill within 21 days to see which offer works.

Is microblading legal in Texas?

Yes, but it is regulated as tattooing, not cosmetology. Texas DSHS requires a tattoo studio license for businesses that perform permanent cosmetics and microblading, and TDLR's own guidance lists microblading under DSHS and the Texas Medical Board. A salon license from TDLR does not cover it. If you offer microblading, keep the DSHS license current and visible to clients.
