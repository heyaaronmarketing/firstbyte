How to Get More Lawn Care Customers in The Woodlands and Houston

The cheapest way to get more lawn care customers in The Woodlands and Houston is to sell the streets you already mow. Hang door hangers on the houses around every finished lawn, keep your Google Business Profile and Nextdoor page busy with real photos, and ask happy customers for reviews and neighbors. Once the route is full, add fertilization, weed control and irrigation work to the same addresses, which pays more per stop and keeps billing alive in January.

Say you run two crews out of Spring. Your problem probably isn’t finding lawns. It’s that the lawns you have are spread from Magnolia to Kingwood. So we start with the route, not the ads.

How to get more lawn care customers on the streets you already mow

A lot of mowing companies take any lead inside 25 miles and then spend half the day on FM 1488 and the Grand Parkway.

Route density is the profit in mowing. If your crew finishes a lawn and the next one is three doors down, you’re mowing. If it’s 20 minutes away on the Grand Parkway, you’re paying two people to sit in a truck. So the best new customer is a neighbor of an existing one, and your marketing should be built around that.

Here’s rough math for one two-person crew. These are our planning numbers, not industry data:

Route typeAvg. drive between lawnsLawns per 9-hour dayRevenue at $45 a lawn
Scattered (whole metro)18 minutes11$495
Clustered (a few ZIPs)8 minutes15$675
Dense (same village or subdivision)2 minutes19$855

Same crew, same fuel, nearly 75% more revenue on the dense route. That’s why we tell lawn companies to pick five or six neighborhoods and own them before advertising anywhere else.

The “neighbor of” playbook

  1. Every time you start a new account, hang door hangers on the 10 nearest houses that day. Say “We mow for your neighbor on Timber Ridge” on the hanger (street name only, never the address).
  2. Offer a neighbor rate: a small discount for any house on the same street, because it costs you less to serve.
  3. Leave a yard sign for 48 hours after the first mow, if the customer agrees and the HOA allows it. Check the deed restrictions first.
  4. Track which streets produce. If Alden Bridge converts and Cochran’s Crossing doesn’t, shift the hangers.

Lawn care advertising ideas that work in HOA neighborhoods

The Woodlands, Spring, Cypress and the master-planned communities along the Grand Parkway are built for lawn care marketing. Lots are similar sizes, so pricing is easy. Neighbors watch each other’s yards. And the rules push people toward hiring help.

The Township’s covenant FAQ says deed restrictions “require that properties be kept in good order and repair. That includes yard maintenance,” and violations lead to notices to the owner. A homeowner who just got a covenant letter about an overgrown yard is about as ready to buy as anyone you’ll ever meet. The same FAQ says The Woodlands encourages “natural yards” with less grass and more forest, which is a selling point for bed maintenance, mulch and pine straw, not only mowing.

Lawn care advertising ideas we’d try, roughly in order of cost:

  • Nextdoor Business Page and posts. Free, and neighbors ask “who mows for you?” constantly. Respond fast, with a photo. Our Nextdoor advertising breakdown covers when paid ads and Opportunity Alerts are worth adding.
  • Door hangers around active jobs. Cheap and pointed at the exact houses that help your route.
  • Yard signs. Short-term, permission-based, and only where the deed restrictions allow them.
  • Truck and trailer wraps. Your trucks are parked in these neighborhoods all week. Put the phone number big enough to read from a driveway.
  • Village association and HOA newsletter ads. Many accept local business ads. Ask the management company.
  • Facebook and Instagram ads limited to your ZIP codes. Before-and-after photos of a real lawn in the neighborhood beat any stock image.

We’d skip mass mailers to the whole county. They spread you thin, which is the opposite of what your route needs.

When Houston homeowners pick a lawn service

Up north, lawn care is a short-season business. Here the grass grows most of the year, which changes when your marketing has to run.

AgriLife’s St. Augustinegrass maintenance calendar, published through the Harris County Extension office, has weekly mowing starting in spring once St. Augustine is actively growing, fertilizer going down after the lawn has been mowed twice, and irrigation running until the turf stops growing in late fall. Even in December through February it says to mow “if necessary” to keep winter weeds from flowering. Houston’s average last freeze lands around February 15, according to KPRC, and Space City Weather notes it runs later, into late February or early March, for areas north of the city like The Woodlands.

The grass matters too. AggieTurf calls St. Augustinegrass “one of the most widely planted turfgrass species in Texas,” and the AgriLife guide Turfgrass Selection for Texas says it’s best adapted to Southeast Texas and is the most shade-tolerant warm-season grass. That shade tolerance matters under The Woodlands’ pine canopy. St. Augustine also comes with problems most homeowners can’t diagnose, like chinch bugs in summer, large patch in fall and take-all root rot, and each one is a reason to call a pro.

How that turns into a marketing calendar:

  • February is your sign-up month. Homeowners decide who’s mowing this year before the first spring cut. Push door hangers and ads in late January and February, not April.
  • Summer is retention season. July heat browns lawns and frustrates people. A text that says “your lawn is heat stressed, here’s what we’re doing” keeps the account.
  • Fall is your upsell window. AgriLife times the last nitrogen 4 to 6 weeks before the first frost and fall pre-emergent for winter weeds. That’s a natural pitch for a fertilization program.

How to get more lawn care leads from Google

Door hangers reach people who weren’t looking. Google reaches the ones who are looking today. Somebody in Creekside Park types “lawn service near me” and picks from the three businesses in the map pack, so you want to be one of them.

Set up your Google Business Profile properly

  • Choose “Lawn care service” as your primary category. Add “Landscaper” or “Landscape designer” as secondary only if you really do that work.
  • If you don’t have a storefront, hide your address and set a service area by city or ZIP. List the places you want to win, not all of Harris County.
  • Upload photos every week from real jobs. Fresh photos tell customers you’re active.
  • Turn on messaging and answer within the hour during the season.

Consider Local Services Ads

Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes “Lawn care services” and “Landscaping services” in the U.S., so you can get the Google Screened badge and pay per lead instead of per click. LSA leads can cost less than search clicks that never call, but you need a background check, insurance and good reviews to get approved. Our LSA vs Google Ads comparison walks through the tradeoffs.

Search ads, carefully

Regular Google Ads can work for higher-ticket jobs like landscape installs, sod and irrigation repair. For weekly mowing, the margins are thin and clicks aren’t cheap, so keep the budget small, target only your route ZIPs and track every call. Lawn care marketing ideas that cost almost nothing

Some of the best lawn care marketing ideas don’t need a budget at all. They need a habit.

  • Ask for reviews in person, then text the link. The moment a customer says “the yard looks great” is the moment to ask. Our guide to Google reviews has scripts that don’t feel pushy.
  • Mention specifics in your replies. “Thanks, Linda, glad the St. Augustine bounced back after the chinch bug treatment” helps your profile more than “Thanks!”
  • Referral credit. One free mow for each neighbor who signs up for a season. It costs you about $45 and buys a customer worth well over $1,000 a year.
  • Seasonal texts. A February “we’re booking spring starts” text to last year’s customers and anybody who got a quote but didn’t buy.
  • Photos with every invoice. A quick photo of the finished lawn makes the work visible to people who are at the office when you mow.
  • Partner with real estate agents. Listings need curb appeal fast, and agents remember who saved a closing.

Most competitors try these for a month and drop them by June. Keep doing them through August and you’ll stand out.

Turn mowing into fertilization and weed control (license first)

Mowing gets you in the door. Fertilization and weed control is where the margin is, and it keeps billing in months when the mower barely runs.

Here’s the rule you can’t skip. According to the Texas Department of Agriculture, anyone who applies any pesticide to grass, trees, shrubs or other plants for hire must be licensed by TDA in the 3A category or by the Structural Pest Control Service, which is now part of TDA. Herbicides count as pesticides, so weed control and most weed-and-feed products fall under that rule. Applying fertilizer only does not need a license from either agency.

So a mowing company can sell a fertilizer-only program on day one. Weed control, fire ant treatment and chinch bug work need the license, or a licensed partner who does the application.

How we’d pitch the program:

  1. Start with a free lawn assessment for current mowing customers in late winter, before pre-emergent timing.
  2. Show what’s wrong with photos: weeds, thin spots, signs of chinch bugs or large patch.
  3. Offer a set program on the AgriLife calendar, billed monthly so it feels like the mowing bill.
  4. Report after each visit with a photo and a note on what you applied.

GreenPal’s 2026 Houston price guide puts Houston fertilization and weed control at $50 to $150 per application and $500 to $1,500 a year for a bundled program, with most programs running seven or eight applications.

Irrigation checks: the upsell that needs a TCEQ license

Sprinklers are the other big add-on in Houston, especially after a summer like most of ours. They’re also where many lawn companies get sloppy.

Under TCEQ’s landscape irrigation rules (RG-470), a person who “sells, designs, installs, maintains, alters, repairs, or services an irrigation system” needs a TCEQ irrigator license. Homeowners working on their own property are exempt; your crew is not. That covers more than new installs. Swapping a broken head or changing a controller program for a customer counts as servicing. Backflow assemblies also have to be tested by a licensed backflow tester after they’re installed or repaired.

If someone on your team holds the license, market it hard. A spring “sprinkler checkup” and a summer “water-saving audit” are easy sells in neighborhoods where people get covenant letters about brown lawns. If nobody does, refer the work to a licensed irrigator you trust and ask for referrals back.

The pricing and upsell math

Here’s why the upsell matters more than the next mowing customer.

GreenPal’s 2026 Houston price guide puts a typical Houston mow, edge and blow at $35 to $55 per visit for a standard suburban lot. Nationally, LawnStarter lists $44 to $71, averaging $57. Take $45 a visit and about 34 visits a year, weekly through the main season and every other week on the shoulders. That’s roughly $1,530 a year from one yard.

Add a $700 fertilization program and two $95 irrigation checks and the same address is worth about $2,420, with no extra drive time. Those are illustrative figures, and yours will vary with lot size, products and labor.

Quick pricing tips:

  • Quote by lot size and obstacles, not a flat number for every house.
  • Offer annual agreements with monthly billing so revenue stays steady in January.
  • Raise prices at season start, with notice, instead of mid-year.
  • Don’t discount mowing to win an account. Discount the add-on instead.

How to get more customers for my lawn care business this month

For a plan you can start Monday, this is the order we’d go in:

  1. Pick your target neighborhoods. Five or six villages or subdivisions where you already have accounts.
  2. Fix your Google Business Profile. Right category, a service area that matches your route, 20 recent photos.
  3. Order door hangers and signs. Hang 10 around every job for the next month.
  4. Text every current customer for a review and a referral, with the credit offer.
  5. Post on Nextdoor once a week with a real before-and-after.
  6. Price your upsell program and pitch it to existing customers first.
  7. Track it. Ask every new caller how they found you and write it down.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more lawn care customers fast?

Market to the neighbors of customers you already have. Hang door hangers on the houses around each job, ask for a yard sign, and offer a neighbor rate. Then update your Google Business Profile, post before-and-after photos on Nextdoor, and text current customers for reviews and referrals. These steps cost little and fill your route faster than broad advertising.

What is the best advertising for a lawn care business?

For most local lawn companies, the best return comes from a complete Google Business Profile, steady reviews, Nextdoor, and door hangers around active jobs. Google Local Services Ads include lawn care and landscaping categories and charge per lead. Paid search and social ads can work for bigger jobs like installs and irrigation repair, but keep them limited to your route ZIP codes.

Do I need a license to spray weeds in Texas?

Yes, if you do it for hire. The Texas Department of Agriculture says anyone applying any pesticide to lawns, trees or shrubs for hire must be licensed through TDA's 3A category or the Structural Pest Control Service. Herbicides are pesticides. Applying fertilizer only does not require a license. Check TDA's current rules before you sell weed control.

How much should I charge to mow a lawn in Houston?

GreenPal's 2026 Houston guide puts a standard suburban mow, edge and blow at about 35 to 55 dollars per visit, with larger lots costing more. LawnStarter's national range is 44 to 71 dollars. Price by lot size, obstacles and how close the yard is to your existing route, and set annual agreements with monthly billing.

How do lawn care companies get recurring customers?

They sell season-long agreements instead of one-off mows, bill monthly, and add services that run year-round, like fertilization, weed control, bed maintenance and irrigation checks. In Houston, St. Augustine needs care from early spring into late fall, so a program built on the AgriLife maintenance calendar gives customers a reason to stay all year.
