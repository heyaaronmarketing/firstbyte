How to Get More Remodeling Leads in Houston (and Stop Renting Them)

How to get more remodeling leads in Houston and The Woodlands: the lead-cost math, project pages, Google Business Profile, reviews and referral partners.

The fastest way to get more remodeling leads is to buy them. The way to get more remodeling leads that actually sign, at a margin you like, is to own them: a portfolio website with a real page for every finished project, a Google Business Profile you feed with job photos and reviews every week, and three or four referral partners who send you homeowners nobody else is bidding on. Lead marketplaces can fill a slow month. They shouldn’t be the plan.

We’ll start with the math, because once you see what a shared lead costs per signed job, the rest of this guide reads differently. Everything below is written for remodelers working Houston, The Woodlands, Kingwood, Spring and Conroe.

How to get more remodeling leads you own instead of rent

A rented lead is one you pay for every time: a marketplace match, a click, a shared form fill. Stop paying and it stops coming. An owned lead comes from something you built once and keep compounding, like a project page that ranks, a profile with 140 reviews, or a realtor who has sent you six kitchens.

Both cost money. The difference shows up a year later. Rented leads cost the same in month 13 as in month one (usually more, as competitors bid up the same homeowners). Owned sources get cheaper per job as the photos, reviews and relationships pile up.

The market makes this more urgent. Harvard’s Joint Center for Housing Studies, in its July 2026 forecast, expects U.S. home improvement spending to grow from $517 billion to $519 billion between mid-2026 and mid-2027, an annual growth rate of just 0.5%. That’s flat. In a flat market you don’t get more jobs by waiting for demand to rise. You take them from the remodeler down the road, and the ones who win are the ones homeowners already trust before they call.

Demand for professionals isn’t the problem. In the 2025 Houzz & Home Study, about nine in ten renovating homeowners hired one. The question is whether they find you first and believe you quickly.

The lead-cost math: marketplace leads vs. leads you own

We covered Angi and Thumbtack pricing, billing and the FTC’s HomeAdvisor order in detail in our guide to whether Angi and Thumbtack leads are worth it, so we won’t repeat it here. The short version: you pay for the match, not the job, and on Angi’s own earnings call pros were winning about one in six leads.

What matters for a remodeler is cost per signed job, and how much margin you give up to get it. Here’s a worked example with round numbers. Say you run a four-person remodeling company in Spring and your average bathroom job is $25,000 at a 30% gross margin, so each signed job is worth about $7,500 in gross profit.

Are exclusive remodeling leads worth paying for?

“Exclusive remodeling leads” is the pitch lead vendors use once contractors get tired of shared ones. Sometimes it means the vendor sells the lead to only you. Sometimes it means only you this hour. Ask for the definition in writing, and ask how the vendor generated the lead, because a homeowner who filled out a “get three free quotes” form expects three quotes, no matter what the vendor promised you.

Here’s how we’d test an exclusive lead vendor:

  1. Buy a small batch, 20 leads or fewer, before signing any term contract.
  2. Call every lead within ten minutes and log whether the homeowner remembers asking.
  3. Ask each homeowner, “How many remodelers have reached out so far?” Their answer tells you how exclusive it was.
  4. Track each lead through to signed or lost in your CRM, not in the vendor’s dashboard.
  5. Compare cost per signed job against your Google Ads and referral numbers after 60 to 90 days.

The only truly exclusive remodeling lead is a homeowner who looked you up by name. The rest of this page is about producing more of those.

Build a portfolio site that sells the job before the call

Most remodeling websites have one “Gallery” page of 60 unlabeled photos. Google can’t tell what they show, and homeowners can’t tell whether that kitchen was $40,000 or $140,000.

Build a page for each finished project instead. One kitchen, one page. It ranks for searches like “kitchen remodel Alden Bridge” or “walk-in shower Kingwood,” it gives your Business Profile posts somewhere to link, and it answers the questions a homeowner is too polite to ask on the first call.

Then build service pages that pull those projects together. A homeowner searching for kitchen remodeling leads you to a kitchen remodeling page with your five best kitchen projects, a realistic price range, your process and a consult form. Do the same for bathrooms, additions, whole-home remodels and anything else you want more of. If you serve several cities, our guide to local landing pages covers how to build city pages that aren’t thin copies of each other.

A couple of details change lead quality more than design does:

  • Publish a price frame. “Most of our primary bathroom remodels land between $35,000 and $70,000” scares off a few shoppers and saves you a dozen wasted site visits.
  • Ask one qualifying question on the form. “What’s your rough budget?” with ranges, or “When would you like to start?” Both help you prioritize callbacks without adding friction.

Google Business Profile: the free remodeling lead source most shops starve

Google’s own help page on local ranking is blunt about it: “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking.” For a remodeler that means a weekly routine, not a profile you set up once and forget:

  • Categories. Pick the primary category that matches the work you want most (for many shops that’s a remodeler category, with kitchen and bathroom remodeler as secondaries if you do them). Don’t add categories for work you turn down.
  • Service area. List the cities and ZIP codes you’ll drive to and hide your address if clients don’t visit. A shop in The Woodlands might list Spring, Conroe, Magnolia, Tomball and Kingwood.
  • Project photos every week. Before, during and after. Tradespeople on site, not stock images. Name the neighborhood in the caption when the homeowner agrees.
  • One post per finished project, linking to that project’s page on your site.
  • A review request on the day of the final walkthrough, while the homeowner is standing in the new kitchen. Our guide to Google reviews has the text-message wording.

Google’s Local Services Ads now include both kitchen remodeling and bathroom remodeling, plus general contractor, countertop and flooring categories, per Google’s Local Services Ads category list. LSA leads are pay-per-lead and come with the Google Verified badge, so they’re a reasonable paid layer on top of a strong profile. We compare them with regular search ads in Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads, and our local SEO guide for Houston contractors covers the rest of the map-pack setup.

Houston selling points: Harvey rebuilds, aging finishes and The Woodlands’ design review

The Harvey rebuild is now eight or nine years old

Hurricane Harvey flooded 154,170 homes in Harris County in 2017, and a little less than half of them sat outside the 100- and 500-year floodplains, according to Harris County Flood Control District figures. Many were gutted and rebuilt in 2018 and 2019, quickly and often to an insurance budget, and some of those rushed kitchens and baths are now due for a proper redo. A page or ad that speaks to “your post-Harvey kitchen, done properly this time” lands in a way a generic offer doesn’t. Be tactful; some of these owners lost a lot.

Young housing stock, old neighborhoods

Texas has the second-newest housing in the country, with a median owner-occupied home age of 28 years against 42 nationally, per NAHB’s analysis of 2024 Census data. But The Woodlands opened on October 19, 1974, with Grogan’s Mill as its first village (Visit The Woodlands), and Friendswood Development built Kingwood through the 1970s. Those original houses are around 50 years old: dated layouts, closed kitchens, small primary baths. Market specific projects to specific neighborhoods.

The Woodlands’ design review is a selling point

The Township’s Covenant Administration page says work on a lot with an existing home “must have the prior written approval of the Residential Design Review Committee or their designated staff.” Each village has its own committee (the Township lists nine), they meet at least monthly per the Residential Design Review Committees page, and only the Development Standards Committee can grant variances. Applications go through the Township’s online Civic Access Portal.

Most homeowners find this confusing. Put “We prepare and submit your RDRC application” on your Woodlands pages and build the meeting calendar into the timeline you quote. That line separates you from the out-of-town crew who learns about the committee after demo day.

Permits change at the city line

Inside Houston city limits, the Houston Permitting Center requires a residential permit with plan review for remodels, conversions and additions, and licensed contractors schedule the trade inspections. That includes Kingwood, which Houston annexed in 1996 (the City of Houston’s Kingwood page). Conroe, Shenandoah, Tomball and other cities run their own offices, and many unincorporated subdivisions answer to an HOA instead. Saying plainly who pulls the permit and who handles the HOA, on every service page, answers a question homeowners are already worried about.

How to get more remodeling jobs from realtors, designers and past clients

Referral partners send the best leads you’ll ever get, and most remodelers treat them as luck. Treat them as a channel with a process.

  • Listing agents. They need pre-listing fixes done fast and buyers who want changes after closing. Offer a two-week turnaround on paint, fixtures and small bath refreshes, and a one-page “what it would cost to open this kitchen” estimate their buyers can take to showings.
  • Interior designers and kitchen showrooms. Many have clients and no installer they trust. Offer to handle the RDRC paperwork and protect their design on site.
  • Restoration and water-mitigation companies. They dry out the house after a leak or storm and often don’t do the rebuild.
  • Past clients. Most renovators aren’t selling soon, so the family who loved their new kitchen is a good bet for the primary bath in two or three years. Put a reminder in the CRM.

The process: keep a list of partners in your CRM, send each one a short project recap with photos when you finish a job near them, and track which partner sent which signed contract. Jobber’s guide suggests a referral relationship takes months to pay off, and that matches what we see. Three partners sending four jobs a year each is a dozen remodels with no competing bids.

Is Houzz worth it for remodelers in Houston?

Houzz is where a lot of affluent homeowners save photos before they call anyone, so a complete Houzz profile with your best projects and reviews is worth an afternoon. It also gives you a second, independent review source that design-minded clients trust.

Houzz Pro’s paid advertising is a different question. Judge it like Angi: get your market’s pricing in writing, run it for a set period, and count signed jobs in your own CRM. Our view: the free profile pulls its weight, and the paid tier has to earn its renewal.

Home remodeling leads from paid ads: where to start

Once the owned pieces are in place, paid ads add speed. A sensible order for a remodeler new to paid ads:

  1. Local Services Ads in the kitchen and bathroom remodeling categories, if they’re available in your ZIPs.
  2. Google search ads on specific project searches (kitchen remodel, bathroom remodel, room addition plus your cities), sent to the matching service page, never the homepage.
  3. Retargeting everyone who viewed a project page, with more project photos and a consult offer.

Kitchen and bath projects deserve their own campaigns, keywords and follow-up, because the ticket is higher and the decision takes months. Tie each remodeling lead to the contract it became

Most remodelers know their lead count and their revenue and nothing in between, so they can’t tell which source actually signed. A few habits close that gap:

  • Use a call tracking number on your website, your Business Profile and each ad channel. Our call tracking guide shows the setup.
  • Record the source on every lead in your CRM, and update the status when it becomes a consult, a proposal, a signed job or a loss.
  • Once a quarter, divide what you spent on each source by the jobs it signed. Cut the bottom one. Feed the top one.

Start this week with the cheapest fix: post the last three finished projects to your Business Profile, each linked to its own page. If you’re also running Google or Meta ads, check what the account counts as a conversion. When a form fill from a $3,000 bath shopper and a booked design consult both register as one “lead,” the campaigns optimize toward the cheap one. Frequently asked questions

Where do remodelers get the best leads?

Referrals from past clients, realtors and designers usually close at the highest rate and the best margin, because the homeowner arrives already trusting you and is rarely collecting four bids. After that come calls from your Google Business Profile and project pages on your own site. Paid search and Local Services Ads add volume, and shared marketplace leads tend to close lowest and push hardest on price.

How much should a remodeling lead cost?

There is no fair single number, because a $4,000 bath refresh and a $90,000 kitchen are different businesses. Work backward instead. Decide what you can spend to sign one job, often 5 to 10 percent of its gross profit, then divide by how many leads it takes you to sign one. That gives you a ceiling per lead for each project type.

How do I get kitchen remodeling leads without Angi?

Build a dedicated kitchen remodeling page with five or more real local projects and a price range, post each finished kitchen to your Google Business Profile, and ask every happy client for a review on the day of the final walkthrough. Add Google Local Services Ads in the kitchen remodeling category, then search ads on kitchen remodel terms for your cities, and retarget site visitors with more project photos.

Do I need approval to remodel a home in The Woodlands?

For exterior work, yes. The Woodlands Township says improvements on a lot with an existing home need prior written approval from the village Residential Design Review Committee or its staff, applied for through the Township's online portal. Each village committee meets at least monthly. Interior-only remodels are a different question, so check with Covenant Administration before you start, and build review time into the schedule.

Are bathroom remodeling leads cheaper than kitchen leads?

Usually, because the jobs are smaller and more companies compete for them, including tub-to-shower specialists. Bathroom leads also tend to decide faster. That makes them good for keeping crews busy, but price pressure is higher, so lean on reviews, project pages and partner referrals instead of shared leads where four bids are the norm.
