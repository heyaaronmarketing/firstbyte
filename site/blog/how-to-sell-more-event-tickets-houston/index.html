How to Sell More Event Tickets Online: A Houston Promoter’s Playbook

How to sell more event tickets online in Houston: when to spend, Meta and Google settings, ticket pixels, Texas texting rules, all-in pricing and weather.

Three weeks out, the dashboard says 38% sold. The promoter refreshes Eventbrite every hour, the venue manager keeps texting, and somebody in the group chat suggests boosting a post. Almost every Houston event we’ve been around hits this exact moment.

The honest answer to how to sell more event tickets online is mostly timing. Spend hard at the announcement and again in the last ten days, keep past buyers warm in between, and make sure Meta and Google can actually see a purchase when one happens. Then give Google enough structured data to show your event to someone typing “things to do this weekend.”

Below are the settings, budget splits, Texas texting law, the FTC pricing rule, and the Houston weather problem nobody puts in the plan. It’s for whoever is on the hook for tickets at exhibitions, family shows, festivals, concert series and holiday light walks.

The ticket sales curve: why the middle weeks feel dead

Ticket sales spike when you announce, bump at the early bird deadline, go quiet for what feels like a month, then surge in the final week. Every promoter knows this. Every promoter panics in week four anyway.

That last-week surge keeps getting bigger. In Eventbrite data reported by Pollstar, 57% of US tickets sold from January to April 2024 were bought a week or less before the show. The gap between purchase and event shrank 26% from 2022 to 2024, and the on-sale window shrank 41%. It’s music data, but family events and exhibitions follow the same shape in our experience, since parents decide on Wednesday what Saturday looks like.

So a slow middle is normal. Spreading budget evenly across the run is where the waste comes from, because week-five money mostly reaches people who’ve already decided to wait.

Hold 40 to 50% of paid media for the last 10 to 14 days. The buyers are there, and so are your biggest retargeting pools.

Then create small spikes in the dead zone with real deadlines, like an early bird price that actually ends, a past-buyer presale, or a group rate that requires booking 14 days out. The lull is also for work that costs hours instead of media dollars: group sales calls, creator content, local press, community calendars.

Multi-week exhibitions run a mini-curve every weekend and a bigger “last chance” rush at the end. Budget them as a series of weekend pushes.

How much to spend on event ads, and when

Start with the event’s own math. Say you’re running a family exhibition in a Houston-area hall for two weekends, and the average order is three tickets at $28, so $84. Willing to spend 20% of gross ticket revenue on ads? Then you can afford about $17 in ad cost per order. If Meta is delivering orders at $25, more budget just loses money faster; fix the campaign first.

For that event we’d split an example $8,000 paid budget roughly like the table below. Your percentages will move. The shape shouldn’t.

Under roughly $1,500 in paid media, forget the channel spread and run Meta retargeting, Google search on your event name, and email. Spread $1,200 across five platforms and you get five half-trained campaigns.

How to promote an event on Facebook and Instagram so it sells tickets

Boosting the Facebook event post is the most common move and the weakest one. A boost optimizes for engagement, and people who will never buy a ticket love tapping “Interested.”

Build it in Ads Manager.

  1. Pick the Sales objective, with the Purchase event from your ticketing platform’s pixel as the conversion. (No Purchase event firing? Fix that first; the tracking section below covers it.)
  2. Turn on Advantage+ audience and feed it suggestions. As Jon Loomer’s breakdown explains, custom audiences and lookalikes are suggestions Meta tries first, while location, minimum age and excluded audiences are hard controls. Suggest your past-buyer list plus a 1% lookalike. Meta needs a source of at least 100 people from a single country and recommends 1,000 to 5,000 for a useful lookalike.
  3. For single-date shows, exclude people who already bought. For multi-date exhibitions, leave them in. Week-one families come back with the grandparents.
  4. Draw the map by drive time, using zip codes and towns since Meta can’t target drive time directly. A Saturday matinee at the Cynthia Woods Mitchell Pavilion pulls easily from Spring, Conroe, Magnolia, Tomball and Kingwood. A 7 p.m. Friday show there asks a lot of someone in Katy or Pearland. A 25-mile radius looks tidy and ignores I-45 at rush hour.

Small events hit a quieter problem. Meta’s guidance points to roughly 50 optimization events a week to exit learning, and a 400-seat show might sell 60 tickets through ads, total. We’ll often optimize for initiated checkout early and switch to Purchase for the last two weeks. A compromise, but better than a campaign stuck in learning forever.

Honestly, creative moves the needle more than any targeting setting. Vertical video of the real thing (the exhibit floor, the crowd at the chorus, a kid in the light tunnel) beats a flyer, and the date, venue and total price belong in the first two seconds. That’s the kind of work our paid social team does every week.

Google Ads for “[event] near me” and “things to do this weekend”

Google search mostly catches demand your other channels create. Someone sees a poster on Market Street or scrolls past a TikTok, then Googles the name. If a reseller shows up first, you paid to create that search and somebody else cashed it.

Start with an ad group on your event name and its misspellings. Cheapest clicks you’ll ever buy, and they keep resale sites from owning your name. Next, the category: “dinosaur exhibit Houston,” “jazz festival Houston,” “Christmas lights The Woodlands.”

Planner phrases like “things to do in Houston this weekend,” “indoor things to do with kids Houston” and “events near me tonight” get their own ad group. They’re pricier and broader, so we schedule them Wednesday through Saturday only.

Google’s location targeting help page explains “Presence” versus “Presence or interest.” For a concert, the broader option can help by catching the parent in Beaumont or College Station planning a Houston weekend. A neighborhood holiday market should use Presence. Radius targeting needs at least 1 km, so tiny geofences around one venue are out.

Bid on Maximize clicks with a max CPC cap until purchases are tracking, then move to Maximize conversions. We’d skip Performance Max for a one-off event; the show is over before it learns anything.

If you run a touring exhibition with daily time slots, ask your ticketing provider whether it connects to Google’s Things to do program, which shows bookable activities through connectivity partners. We haven’t confirmed which platforms qualify. Our search marketing page covers how we run Google Ads more broadly.

TikTok and streaming TV for events: when each is worth it

TikTok earns its spot for concerts, festivals, nightlife and anything aimed at people under 35. The ads that sell look like regular posts, say a creator walking the venue or a shaky clip of last year’s crowd. Spark Ads put paid budget behind a creator’s own post, which people trust more than your brand account.

Check whether your ticketing platform can pass purchases to TikTok. If it can’t, judge TikTok by the lift in branded searches and sales during flights. TikTok chews through creative fast, so plan new videos every week or two in the final month.

Streaming TV is worth a real look for family events. A 15-second spot on a streaming app reaches the whole household on the couch, targeted by zip code. Our full take is in whether streaming TV ads are worth it. For events, our rule of thumb: total paid media over about $10,000, and a run of two weekends or more so awareness has time to turn into searches. A single-night club show? Keep that money on Meta.

Whichever you run, check branded search volume every few days. It’s the cheapest read on whether either channel is doing anything.

Email and texts to past attendees (and the Texas consent rules)

Your best audience already bought from you once. Per dollar, a past-buyer list outsells every paid channel, yet most promoters email it twice per event. We’d send four.

  1. A presale invite with a code, 48 hours before the public on-sale.
  2. An early bird reminder the day before the price goes up.
  3. Something worth opening in the lull, like a behind-the-scenes video, set times or the parking plan.
  4. A final-week note. “Saturday is close to sold out” goes in only if it’s true, along with weather and parking details.

Texts convert even better, and they come with real legal risk. Federal law (the TCPA) generally requires prior express written consent before you send marketing texts using automated systems, so text only people who opted in to texts specifically. Buying a ticket isn’t the same as opting in.

Texas tightened things too. According to Paul Hastings’ write-up of Texas SB 140, the law took effect September 1, 2025, brought text messages under the state’s telephone solicitation rules, added a registration requirement with penalties up to $5,000 per violation, and lets consumers sue under the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act. The write-up notes an exemption for texts to current or former customers. Whether your list fits it is a question for your lawyer, and we’d ask before the first blast, not after.

People who visited the ticket page and didn’t buy can be followed up with retargeting ads, which carry no consent risk. Our explainer on how retargeting works covers the basics.

Tracking ticket sales: pixels on Eventbrite, Universe and Ticketmaster

Most promoters get this part wrong, and it quietly undoes everything above. If Meta can’t see purchases, it goes looking for the wrong people. Per their help pages, the major platforms support the following.

Then test it the boring way. Create a hidden $1 ticket type, buy one yourself through an ad click, and watch the Test Events tab in Meta Events Manager alongside Google’s Tag Assistant. Refund yourself after. Tedious, yes. Also the single most useful thing in this post.

Getting your event into Google’s event listings

Search “things to do in Houston this weekend” and Google often shows an events panel. Getting in is mostly a structured data job.

Google’s Event structured data guidelines require three properties: name, startDate (with date and time) and location with a venue name and address. Recommended properties include offers with price, currency, availability, a validFrom on-sale date and a ticket url, plus eventStatus, image, organizer and performer. A few rules trip people up:

  • Mark up one event per page, not a schedule page listing many.
  • The event must be open to the public and at a physical place. Invite-only and virtual events aren’t eligible.
  • If your listing lives on a ticketing site that already works with Google’s event search, Google says that listing can carry you. Mark up your own page anyway.
  • When plans change, update eventStatus: EventCancelled, EventPostponed (new date unknown) or EventRescheduled (with the new startDate and an optional previousStartDate).

While you’re at it, add an Event post with the ticket link to the venue’s Google Business Profile, and submit to the community calendars locals actually read. Both are free.

Pricing, scarcity and group sales (with the FTC’s all-in rule)

Since May 12, 2025, the FTC’s fees rule has required the total ticket price, mandatory fees included, upfront and more prominent than any partial price (Barnes & Thornburg’s summary). The FTC’s FAQ on the fees rule says it covers “any business that offers, displays, or advertises live-event tickets,” venues included, and that ads with pricing must give the total. You can advertise the lowest general admission total “only if people can actually buy tickets at that price.” Taxes may be left out of the upfront figure as long as they’re disclosed before payment.

If your ad says “Tickets from $25,” then $25 had better be the all-in price of a ticket someone can buy. Check ad copy and email subject lines first.

For family events we like published price tiers (early bird, general, final) better than algorithmic surge pricing. Surge can work for a hot concert. A parent who watches the price jump overnight posts about it in the neighborhood Facebook group.

Scarcity works when it’s true. “Only 60 tickets left for the 2 p.m. Saturday slot” is honest if your ticketing report says 60. Countdown timers that reset cost you the repeat crowd.

Group sales are the most underused tool in the lull. Churches, PTOs, scout troops, youth sports teams and company outings (Energy Corridor HR teams love a family day) buy in tens and twenties. Put up a group page with a 10-ticket rate and a short form, and answer within a day. Two afternoons of calls to youth sports leagues can outsell a week of ads.

Houston weather: plan for the storm before you sell the ticket

Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30. A summer afternoon can go from clear to lightning in an hour. Beryl knocked out power across the region in July 2024. Late buyers (that 57% again) check the forecast first, so your weather plan is a marketing document too.

The National Weather Service says people “should wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder before resuming outdoor activities,” that organized events should “have a lightning safety plan and follow it without exception,” and that organizers should consider canceling or postponing when thunderstorms are forecast.

Indoor events get to play the other side of this. Air conditioning in August is a selling point in Houston, and so is a roof on a rainy Saturday. Build a “rainy weekend” ad set around searches like “indoor things to do Houston” and leave it paused until the forecast turns.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should you start promoting an event?

For most local concerts and family events, six to eight weeks of promotion is plenty, with the heaviest spending in the final ten to fourteen days. Festivals and touring exhibitions can announce months out, but should still save a big share of budget for the end, when most tickets sell.

How much should I spend on ads to sell event tickets?

Work backward from your average order. Decide what share of gross ticket revenue can go to ads, often 10 to 25 percent, and apply it per order. A family show with $84 orders and a 20 percent ad share can afford about $17 per order. If ads cost more than that, fix tracking, creative or targeting before adding budget.

Why are my event ticket sales slow?

Usually it is normal. Sales spike at announcement, go quiet in the middle weeks and surge in the last one. If you are close to the date and still behind, check that your pixel records purchases, that ads show the all-in price, that past buyers got a presale, and that you target people within a realistic drive.

Is Facebook or Google better for selling event tickets?

They do different jobs. Meta and Instagram create demand by showing the event to people who were not looking for it. Google search catches people already searching your event name or things to do this weekend. Most events need both, with Meta getting more budget early and branded Google search running the whole time.

Can I text past attendees about my next event?

Only if they agreed to receive marketing texts from you. Federal rules generally require prior express written consent for automated marketing texts, and Texas SB 140, effective September 1, 2025, brought texts under state telephone solicitation rules, with registration and lawsuit risk. Email past buyers, text only opted-in contacts, and ask a lawyer about the Texas exemptions.
