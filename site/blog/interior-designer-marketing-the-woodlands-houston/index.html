Thursday afternoon, between a tile pickup in Spring and a walkthrough in Creekside Park. Three new inquiries came in today. One is a DM that says “love your work, how much for a living room?” One is a form fill from a couple who closed on a new build in The Woodlands Hills last month and want “help with everything.” The third wants you to pick paint colors for free and asked if you could stop by Saturday.

This guide is for independent designers and small studios, from a one-person e-design business in Magnolia to a five-person firm near Hughes Landing. The legal part comes first, since it decides what you can print on your own website.

“Registered interior designer” vs. “interior designer” in Texas: what you can say in your marketing

Texas has a title law for interior designers, and it’s narrower than a lot of people think. Under Chapter 1053 of the Texas Occupations Code (section 1053.151), a person who isn’t registered with the Texas Board of Architectural Examiners may not represent themselves as a “registered interior designer,” by using that title or words that imply it. A knowing violation is a Class C misdemeanor.

It used to be broader. Before 2009, only architects and registered designers could call themselves “interior designers” at all. HB 1484 changed that. Per the committee analysis of HB 1484, the bill restricted only the “licensed interior designer” and “registered interior designer” titles, removed the language covering the term “interior design,” and exempted anyone who doesn’t use those titles.

So, in plain marketing terms:

Registration has teeth beyond the title. TBAE’s Registered Interior Designers page says applicants need to qualify for and hold a CIDQ certificate (the NCIDQ exam route) before they apply. And the statute is clear that registration doesn’t authorize anyone to design architectural interior construction or practice architecture or engineering. That matters for how you describe space-planning and remodel work on your service pages.

If you are registered, that’s a real differentiator for commercial clients, medical offices and the builder design centers that care about credentials. Put “Registered Interior Designer” in your title tag and Google Business Profile description, list your registration number on the About page, and link to TBAE’s public lookup so a skeptical client can check it in ten seconds. If you aren’t registered, don’t apologize for it. Most residential clients in Sugar Land or Kingwood hire on portfolio, process and price. We’re marketers, not lawyers, so if your situation is unusual (say, you’re licensed in another state and taking Texas projects), call TBAE before you print the brochure.

Who’s hiring a designer in The Woodlands and Houston right now

The 2026 Houzz & Home study, covering 2025 projects, found 54% of homeowners renovated, with a median spend of $20,000 and the top 10% of projects at $150,000 or more. Ninety-one percent of renovating homeowners hired professionals. That’s from more than 20,000 U.S. respondents, per KBB’s summary of the 2026 Houzz & Home study. Your clients mostly live in that top slice, and they’re reachable.

Around here we’d split them into four groups, because each one searches and decides differently:

  • New-build buyers. Closing on a house in a master-planned community, with empty rooms and a deadline (usually a holiday or a family visit). Fast decisions, furnishing-heavy projects.
  • Relocation families. Energy and medical transfers landing in The Woodlands, Memorial and Katy. They need someone local who already knows the showrooms and the installers. They find you through their agent or a Google search the week after closing.
  • Remodel clients. A 2006 kitchen in Sterling Ridge or a dated primary bath in Cinco Ranch. Long timelines, contractor coordination, selections. Our kitchen and bath remodeling marketing post covers the contractor side of this buyer.
  • Second homes and lake houses. Lake Conroe and Montgomery County weekend places, often furnished from scratch, often for owners who live inside the Loop.

Pick one or two to build around. “We furnish new builds in The Woodlands, Spring and Conroe in eight to twelve weeks” is easier to hire than “residential and commercial design of all kinds.”

Portfolio and photography: the asset everything else runs on

Every channel in this post (Instagram, Houzz, Pinterest, your website, a pitch to a builder) runs on the same thing, which is professional photos of finished rooms. Phone photos at install are useful for Stories. They don’t sell a $90,000 project.

Hire an architectural or interiors photographer for your best two or three projects a year, and budget it like a marketing expense rather than a project cost. Houzz even runs a separate Best of Houzz award for photography, for architecture and interior photographers whose images are most popular on the platform, per KBB’s Best of Houzz 2026 announcement. Look at who wins locally and you’ll find your shortlist. Our photographer marketing post explains how those photographers think about bookings and licensing, which helps when you negotiate.

What to get from each shoot:

  • Wide, medium and detail shots of every room, plus a few vertical frames for Pinterest and Reels covers.
  • Before photos you took yourself, from the same angles. Before-and-after pairs outperform finished-only images almost everywhere we’ve tested them.
  • A license that covers your website, social, award entries and print. Ask in writing whether builders or vendors can use them too.

Then organize the portfolio by project type and area, with a budget band if the client agrees. “Whole-home furnishing, 3,800 sq ft, Woodforest, eleven weeks” tells a prospect more than any adjective.

Your website and Google Business Profile in the Houston market

Somebody typing “interior designer The Woodlands” on a Tuesday night has already decided to hire someone. The pages a studio needs:

  • One page per service: full-service design, new-build furnishing, remodel selections, e-design, styling or refresh. Each with process, timeline, a starting investment and three project examples.
  • Area pages for where you actually work (The Woodlands, Spring, Tomball, Montgomery, Memorial, River Oaks, Katy), each showing projects in that area. Our local landing pages guide covers how to make these useful instead of copy-pasted.
  • An inquiry page with a real form (more on the questions below) and a clear statement of what happens next.

On Google Business Profile, choose “Interior designer” as the primary category. Most independent designers work from a home office or meet clients at the job site, so per Google’s service-area help page, if you don’t serve customers at your address, you should remove it and set a service area instead. You can list up to 20 areas, and the whole thing shouldn’t extend much beyond about two hours’ drive from your base. A studio with a sign on the door and posted hours can keep its address. Upload project photos to the profile every month; it’s one of the few free places a homeowner sees your work next to your reviews.

One Houston-specific note. Google’s Local Services Ads do include an interior designer category, but Google’s Local Services Ads category list shows it as available in California and Florida only. Texas designers can’t buy it yet, so regular search ads and the map pack are the game. Our map pack guide goes deeper.

Houzz, Pinterest and Instagram: three visual channels doing three different jobs

Houzz is where homeowners in the middle of a project go to find and vet pros. Best of Houzz 2026 recognized about 2% of the platform’s more than 3 million home building, remodeling and design professionals, across Design, Customer Service, Innovator and Photography categories, per KBB’s Best of Houzz 2026 announcement. Customer Service winners are chosen on overall rating and client reviews, so a Houzz profile with twelve reviews earns more than one with a beautiful cover photo and none. A free profile with complete projects and reviews is worth maintaining. A paid Houzz Pro plan is an ad buy, and we’d judge it by consults booked over a full quarter.

Pinterest is a search engine people use months before they hire. Pinterest reported 661 million monthly active users globally and 107 million in the U.S. and Canada for the third quarter of 2026, per Pinterest’s third-quarter 2026 results. A pin from a 2024 project can still send traffic in 2027, which is something an Instagram post can’t do.

Instagram is where people decide whether they like you. Process Reels, sourcing trips to the trade showrooms, a sixty-second reveal, the ugly “before” on install day. It now does a bit of search work too: since July 10, 2025, public photos and videos from professional accounts of users over 18 can appear in Google results, per PPC Land. The setting is under Settings, Privacy, “Allow public photos and videos to appear in search engine results,” and it starts on. So write captions like a person searching would read them: “Whole-home refresh in Creekside Park, The Woodlands: new lighting, built-ins, white oak floors.”

Paid Instagram works best as retargeting for designers. Show finished projects to people who visited your website or watched most of a Reel, inside a radius around your service area. Cold Instagram ads for “hire a designer” rarely pay back at small budgets. See our Facebook and Instagram ads guide for the setup.

Builder and realtor partnerships in the new master-planned communities

North of Houston, the new-build pipeline is enormous. Community Impact counted about 51,000 homes expected across 27 developments in its Conroe and Montgomery coverage area over the next decade, in communities like Cielo, Chapel Run, The Woodlands Hills and Two Step Farm, with prices from $250,000 to $1 million. The same piece noted that RCLCO’s 2024 year-end report put 12 of the nation’s top 50 master-planned communities by sales in the Houston metro, with more than 7,500 sales.

Every one of those closings is a family standing in an empty house. The people who meet them first are the builder’s sales counselor, the design center and the buyer’s agent.

How we’d approach it:

  • Builders. Most production builders run their own design center for selections, so don’t pitch selections. Pitch what happens after closing: furnishing packages, window treatments, lighting upgrades the design center didn’t offer. Custom and semi-custom builders around Montgomery and Magnolia are different. Many will refer a designer who keeps their schedule on track and makes their homes photograph well.
  • Realtors. Agents selling relocation buyers in The Woodlands, Kingwood and Katy are asked “do you know a designer?” every month. Give them a one-page PDF with your new-build package, a starting price and a phone number. Offer vacant-home staging advice before listing photos. Ask their broker before offering anything of value for referrals; agents have their own rules on that.

Keep a partner sheet: who referred, what project, what it closed for. After a year you’ll know which three relationships matter.

Design consultation fees and project minimums: say them out loud

Back to the person who wanted free paint colors on Saturday. The fastest way to stop spending your weekends on unpaid consults is to publish what the first meeting costs and what projects usually start at.

A number on the website will scare some people off. Mostly the ones who would have taken two hours of your time and then bought the sofa at a big-box store.

What we’d put on the site:

  • “Initial design consultation: [your price], credited toward your design fee if we work together.” A credit makes a paid consult feel like a deposit instead of a toll.
  • “Full-service projects typically start at [your price] in design fees, plus furnishings.” Or a range by project type.
  • How you bill (flat fee, hourly, or a fee plus product markup) in one honest sentence. Clients are less bothered by markup than by finding out about it late.
  • What the consult includes: walkthrough, a short written summary, a rough budget range. Something they keep.

Minimums do the same filtering. If you don’t take single-room projects under [your price], say so on the inquiry page. Point smaller clients to an e-design package instead; some call back later for the whole house.

High-ticket nurture: the inquiry form and the eight-month client

A $60,000 furnishing project rarely closes in a week. Lots of clients are waiting on a closing date, a bonus, a remodel bid or a spouse. Your marketing has to stay useful the whole time.

Start with the inquiry form. Ask for project type, address or neighborhood, timeline, approximate budget range (as ranges, never a blank box), whether the home is new construction and the closing date if so, and how they found you. Five or six questions is plenty. More than that and good clients bail.

Then the follow-up:

  1. Reply the same business day, ideally within the hour, with a link to book the consult. Designers lose more projects to slow replies than to price. Our speed-to-lead post explains why.
  2. If they don’t book, send one short email a week for three weeks: a relevant project, your process, a client review.
  3. Then move them to a monthly email with one finished project and one useful note, like ordering lead times for custom upholstery before the holidays.
  4. Tag the closing date for new-build clients and reach out three weeks before it. Timing beats persistence.

Track inquiries, consults, proposals and signed projects in a spreadsheet or CRM. Your consult-to-signed rate tells you what a lead is worth.

Reviews that sell the next project

Designers get fewer reviews than a restaurant, so each one carries more weight.

Google’s rules are simple: merchants may not offer incentives such as discounts or free services for reviews, may not discourage negative reviews and may not “selectively solicit positive reviews,” per Google’s prohibited content policy. Ask every client, the same way.

Timing that works for designers: mention it at the reveal while they’re walking through, then send the link about two weeks later once they’ve lived with the rooms and had people over. A script you can copy:

“It was a joy working on your house. If you have two minutes, a Google review helps other families in The Woodlands find us. Here’s the link: [link]. Mentioning the kind of project helps people most.”

Ask for a Houzz review too, a month later, since Houzz recognition leans on reviews. Reply to every review with something specific about the project. Our complete guide to Google reviews has more templates.

A first 90 days for a Houston interior design studio

If you’re starting from a pretty Instagram and not much else, this order works:

  • Month one. Fix the title language on your site and profiles. Publish your consult fee and starting investment. Build service and area pages. Set up Google Business Profile properly and upload your best twenty project photos. Add the inquiry form with real questions.
  • Month two. Book a professional shoot of your best recent project. Pin every image to keyword-named Pinterest boards. Write one partner one-pager and hand it to five agents and two custom builders. Start the review ask with every finished client.
  • Month three. Launch a small Google Ads search campaign on your core local and new-build keywords, plus Instagram retargeting. Start the monthly email. Review the inquiry-to-signed numbers and cut whatever produced no consults.

Frequently asked questions

Can I call myself an interior designer in Texas without being registered?

Yes. Since HB 1484 took effect in 2009, Texas restricts only the titles registered interior designer and licensed interior designer, plus words that imply registration. You can call yourself an interior designer, decorator or stylist without registering with the Texas Board of Architectural Examiners. Using the registered title without registration is a Class C misdemeanor, so leave registered and licensed out of your bio unless you hold TBAE registration.

How do interior designers in The Woodlands and Houston get clients?

Most projects come from referrals, Google searches like interior designer The Woodlands, Instagram, Houzz and Pinterest. The designers who grow fastest pair a strong professional portfolio with clear pricing on their website, a properly set up Google Business Profile, steady Google and Houzz reviews, and referral relationships with realtors and custom builders in new master-planned communities around Conroe, Spring and Montgomery.

Should an interior designer charge for the first consultation?

In our view, yes, for most established designers. A published consultation fee filters out people looking for free advice and signals that your time has value. Crediting the fee toward your design fee if the client signs makes it feel like a deposit. Make the consult worth paying for with a walkthrough, a written summary and a rough budget range the client keeps.

Are Google Local Services Ads available for interior designers in Texas?

Not currently. Google lists an interior designer category for Local Services Ads, but only in California and Florida. Designers in Houston and The Woodlands should rely on regular Google search ads, a strong Google Business Profile and reviews to show up for searches like interior designer near me. Check Google’s category list from time to time, since it does expand.

Is Houzz worth it for interior designers in Houston?

A free, complete Houzz profile with project photos and reviews is worth keeping, because homeowners use it to vet pros and Best of Houzz service awards lean on reviews. A paid Houzz Pro plan is an advertising decision. Run it for a full quarter and judge it by paid consultations and signed projects, not by profile views or message counts, before you renew.
