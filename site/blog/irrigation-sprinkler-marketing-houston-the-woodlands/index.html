Monday morning in late July, after a weekend in the upper 90s. Fourteen voicemails. Three are broken heads the mow crew clipped on Friday. Two are controllers blinking after a power blip. One is a homeowner in Alden Bridge with a letter from the HOA about a brown front yard. One is a builder’s superintendent asking whether you can take six new installs in a Conroe subdivision next month.

And your Google Ads account spent $212 over the weekend on people searching “how to fix a sprinkler head yourself.”

Irrigation and sprinkler company marketing in Houston and The Woodlands comes down to three jobs. Win the repair call when something breaks. Turn that call into a yearly maintenance and backflow account. Build a short list of builders and landscapers who hand you installs. Most sprinkler companies we talk to are decent at the first job, accidental at the second and lucky at the third.

This post covers all three, starting with the Texas licensing rules that shape your ads before you write a word of copy.

Your TCEQ license number belongs on every ad

Texas is stricter about irrigation advertising than most owners realize. Under 30 TAC §344.70, every advertisement for irrigation services, in any medium, must include the license number. If the ad names a company or person other than the licensed irrigator, it has to name the irrigator and show that person’s license number too. Vehicles used for irrigation work need the license number with the “LI” prefix in block letters at least two inches high on both sides, and trailers that advertise irrigation services need the number as well.

Paperwork gets the same treatment. 30 TAC §344.71 requires bids, proposals, estimates and invoices for installing or repairing a system to show the irrigator’s name, license number, business address and phone, along with the TCEQ notice that begins “Irrigation in Texas is regulated by the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality.” Installation contracts must be written, signed, carry the irrigator’s seal and state the warranty dates.

TCEQ’s landscape irrigation licensing page explains who can do what. A Landscape Irrigator (LI) can sell, design, install, maintain, repair and service systems. An Irrigation Technician (IT) can connect systems to the water supply and do repairs, but works under an LI’s supervision. An Irrigation Inspector (II) checks systems for compliance and can’t work for an irrigation company at the same time. Licenses cost $111, run three years, and anyone can look one up on TCEQ’s license search.

We treat this as a marketing asset. Hardly anyone checks a plumber’s license before calling. Irrigation is different, because HOAs, builders and property managers do check, and a homeowner who just got burned by a mow-and-blow crew “fixing” their system often looks too. So we put the LI number in the Google Business Profile description, the website footer, every landing page, the truck wrap and the Meta ad image. Search ads have tight character limits, but a callout like “TCEQ Licensed Irrigator LI [your number]” fits, and the landing page carries the rest.

Backflow testing in Houston: the reminder that sells itself

Backflow testing is the most underrated revenue line in the business, because the customer doesn’t have to be persuaded. The utility persuades them.

In the City of Houston, testable backflow assemblies have to be tested at least once a year and again whenever they’re installed, repaired or relocated, according to Houston Public Works’ Cross Connection Control Program. Testers must hold a current TCEQ backflow prevention assembly tester license and be approved on the city’s testing company list, and results get uploaded through SwiftComply. Properties that fall out of compliance can face citations of up to $2,000 per day and possible suspension of water service. Many MUDs and suburban utilities in Cypress, Katy, Pearland and Montgomery County run their own programs with their own deadlines, so check each one you serve.

The marketing is mostly a calendar. Every test you run creates a due date 12 months out. Put it in your field software, send a reminder text 45 days ahead, then 21, then 7. Offer a combined “backflow test plus system check” visit, which is how a [your price] compliance call turns into the summer repair work you’d otherwise have chased with ads.

Landing pages help here too. Build one page per utility area you test in (“Backflow testing in the City of Houston,” “Backflow testing in The Woodlands”), each explaining that area’s process in plain English and linking to the official source. Those pages rank for low-competition searches all year, and property managers bookmark them.

Watering schedules by city and MUD are free content

Watering rules around Houston change by city, by MUD and by summer. That’s a headache for homeowners and an opening for you.

In The Woodlands, the Woodlands Water Agency manages the 10 municipal utility districts and has run a defined irrigation schedule since 2013, per Community Impact. Odd-numbered addresses run automatic sprinklers overnight starting Tuesday and Saturday evenings, and even-numbered addresses start Wednesday and Saturday evenings, with drip and hand watering allowed any time. The Woodlands Township’s water conservation page suggests about an inch of water a week in summer, half an inch in spring and fall, and none in winter, split across your two allowed days.

Other cities move in and out of stages. In August 2026, ABC13 reported that Tomball entered Stage 2, limiting automatic irrigation to 7 p.m. to 5 a.m. on two designated days by address. The same month, Katy moved to Stage 1, asking residents to water only overnight hours. As of this writing, Houston Public Works’ watering restrictions page shows Stage One was rescinded effective May 29, 2024, with no active stage listed. All of that can change in a dry week, so link to the official page rather than copying the rules word for word and forgetting to update them.

What we’d build: a short “watering schedule” page for each city or MUD you serve, with the current rule, a link to the source, and a line about how to program a controller to match it. Add a “Reprogram my controller” booking button. When a stage changes, update the page, post it to your Google Business Profile and send it to your customer list the same day. Nextdoor posts in the affected neighborhoods do well here because neighbors are already arguing about it.

Keep the tone helpful. Nobody books the company that scolds them about water waste.

Drought summers, wet springs and when to spend

Houston irrigation demand swings harder than most home services. Summer 2023 was the warmest on record for Houston, the city hit 109°F twice in August, and it went almost 50 days without measurable rain from early July into August, per ABC13’s 2023 weather recap. Sprinkler companies couldn’t answer the phone fast enough. Then a wet spring arrives and nobody thinks about their sprinklers for six weeks.

A rough seasonal plan for the north Houston area, which you should adjust once you have your own call data:

  • February and March. Spring startup and system checks. Push maintenance plan sign-ups to past customers, and quote installs for homeowners who want them in before summer.
  • April through June. Repair search volume climbs with the heat. Raise search budgets, and keep the controller and smart upgrade offers running.
  • July through September. Peak repair season. Your problem becomes capacity, not demand. Tighten your service radius in Google Ads so techs aren’t driving from Montgomery to Pearland for a single head replacement.
  • October and November. Fall audits, drainage work and commercial contract renewals.
  • December and January. Freeze prep and backflow insulation. Hard freezes in Houston are rare enough that people forget, and frequent enough that every few years they crack assemblies across the region. Have a freeze-repair page ready to switch on.

Rainy stretches are when you should be selling maintenance plans and rain sensors to the people who called you in August. They remember the bill.

Smart controllers, utility discounts and the tax holiday

Smart controllers are a good upsell because they sell on the customer’s own complaint. “My water bill is crazy” is the opening line of half your summer calls.

Some Houston-area programs help. Houston Public Works’ smart controller program offers residential customers who get their water bill from the city a discount on Rachio controllers: in 2026, the 8-zone model was $169.99 against a regular $199.99, with lower pricing during promotional weeks (May 4 to 11 and June 8 to 15). The program is built around DIY installation, which is exactly why there’s a market for “we’ll install and program the one you bought.”

The state chips in once a year. The Texas Comptroller set the 2026 water-efficient products sales tax holiday for May 23 to 25, covering WaterSense-labeled landscape irrigation controls, plus residential soaker and drip hoses, moisture controls for sprinkler systems, mulch and plants. Whether your installation labor qualifies is a question for your accountant, so we wouldn’t advertise “tax free installs.”

Utility programs change every year, and many MUDs and suburban utilities in Montgomery and Harris County don’t have one at all. Before you put any discount in an ad, confirm it’s current and that it applies where the customer lives. Then make it the headline, not a footnote.

Google Business Profile, Google Ads and Local Services Ads for sprinkler repair

Start with the Business Profile, because most repair calls come through Maps. Pick the primary category that matches sprinkler work as closely as possible (type “sprinkler” or “irrigation” into the category field and choose the nearest real match), and add landscaping or drainage categories only if you actually do that work. Fill the services list with plain entries like “sprinkler head replacement,” “zone valve repair,” “backflow testing,” “controller installation” and “leak detection.” Put the LI number in the description. If you work from home and don’t serve customers there, hide the address and set a service area. Our guide to ranking in the Houston map pack covers the rest.

For Google Ads, split campaigns by job type so you can control the budget for each. Repair and backflow campaigns should be call-focused with ad scheduling that matches when someone answers. Installs can lean on forms and a quote page.

Sample repair ad, for illustration:

  • Headline 1: Sprinkler Repair in The Woodlands
  • Headline 2: TCEQ Licensed Irrigator
  • Headline 3: Same-Week Repairs, Upfront Pricing
  • Description: Broken heads, leaking valves, dead zones. Licensed LI on every job. Call now or book online.

The negatives matter as much as the keywords. Start with: how to, diy, yourself, youtube, manual, parts, Home Depot, Lowe’s, Rain Bird or Hunter part numbers, wiring diagram, jobs, hiring, salary, license class, test prep and “free” (unless you do free estimates). Read the search terms report weekly for the first month. Our Houston cost-per-click benchmarks will help you set a starting budget.

Local Services Ads are less clear-cut. Google’s Local Services Ads category list has no standalone irrigation or sprinkler category. It does list lawn care services and landscaping services, and some irrigation companies run under those with matching job types. Start the sign-up with your address to see what’s offered. If the job types don’t match sprinkler repair, skip it and put the money into search. We compare the two in Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads.

Builder, landscaper and pool builder partnerships

The installs you want most rarely come from a Google search. They come from a superintendent who trusts you to show up before the sod goes down.

Builders have their own reasons to care about licensing. TCEQ’s guide for builders hiring irrigators tells them to check the irrigator’s name, license number and contact details on bids and invoices, look for the license number on the truck, confirm local permits on new installs, and hand the design, maintenance checklist and warranties to the homeowner at closing. It also warns that unlicensed irrigators often lack insurance, which leaves the builder exposed. That’s your pitch, written by the regulator.

So make it easy. A one-page builder sheet with your LI number, insurance certificate, typical lead time per lot, how you handle punch-list calls and warranty visits, and a sample closing packet. Builders in Conroe, Magnolia, Montgomery and the Grand Parkway corridor are adding lots fast; our home builder marketing post explains how they think about vendors and timelines.

Landscapers are the other half. Plenty of landscape companies in Spring, Tomball and Cypress don’t hold an irrigator license and can’t legally do the work. Under 30 TAC §344.71, a pass-through contract that includes irrigation has to name the licensed irrigator doing it, with license number and a way to reach them for warranty work. Offer those landscapers a clean subcontract arrangement and a referral fee policy in writing, and they’ll send you every bed renovation that needs drip.

Pool builders and outdoor living contractors tear up irrigation on every backyard job. A short list of them who know your number is worth more than another month of install ads.

Maintenance plans: one repair becomes a yearly account

The repair call is the cheapest customer you’ll ever acquire, because they found you. What you do in the next 30 days decides whether they’re worth $150 or $1,500 over five years.

A simple residential plan might include a spring startup and full-zone inspection, a midsummer audit and controller adjustment, a fall check, freeze prep in December, and the annual backflow test where it’s required. Price it at [your price] a year or monthly, include a discount on repairs, and give plan members priority scheduling in July. That last perk sells the plan better than any discount, because everyone has waited a week for a sprinkler tech in August.

How to sell it without a sales script:

  1. The tech shows the customer one or two photos on the invoice: a misaligned head watering the street, a zone running 40 minutes in a shaded bed.
  2. The invoice includes a one-line plan offer with a link.
  3. A text two days later asks how the yard looks, links the plan, and asks for a review.
  4. A follow-up email before the next season reminds them of the startup visit.

On reviews, follow Google’s rules: ask every customer the same way, never offer discounts for reviews, and never filter out unhappy customers before sending the link. What to track and a first 90 days

Track calls by job type. Total calls hide too much. A month with 120 calls that were mostly head replacements 40 minutes away is worse than 70 calls that turned into nine maintenance plans and two installs.

The short list: booked jobs by source, revenue by job type, maintenance plan sign-ups and renewals, backflow tests due versus completed, and builder or landscaper referrals by partner. Recorded calls and form tracking make this possible; our GA4 and Google Ads tracking guide walks through the setup.

A first 90 days we’d run for a sprinkler company in The Woodlands, Spring or north Houston:

  • Days 1 to 30. Put the LI number everywhere it’s required. Fix the Business Profile categories and services. Build repair, backflow and controller landing pages. Launch call-focused repair search ads with a full negative list.
  • Days 31 to 60. Load every past backflow test into a reminder sequence. Publish watering schedule pages for your top three areas. Launch the maintenance plan offer to past customers.
  • Days 61 to 90. Build the builder and landscaper one-pager and visit ten of each. Review search terms and cut the waste. Decide whether LSA job types fit your work.

Frequently asked questions

Do irrigation ads in Texas have to show a license number?

Yes. Under 30 TAC 344.70, every advertisement for irrigation services in any medium must include the irrigator's license number, and vehicles used for irrigation work must show it with the LI prefix in letters at least two inches high on both sides. Bids, invoices and contracts must also show the license number and the TCEQ notice statement. That rule covers websites, social media ads and yard signs too.

How often is backflow testing required in Houston?

In the City of Houston, testable backflow assemblies must be tested at least once a year and again when installed, repaired or relocated. Testers must hold a TCEQ tester license and be on the city's approved testing company list, and results are uploaded through SwiftComply. Many MUDs and suburban utilities near Houston and The Woodlands run their own programs, so check each utility's rules.

What is the watering schedule in The Woodlands?

The Woodlands Water Agency runs a defined irrigation schedule by address across the 10 MUDs it manages. Odd-numbered addresses water overnight starting Tuesday and Saturday evenings, and even-numbered addresses starting Wednesday and Saturday evenings. Drip irrigation and hand watering are allowed any time. Rules can change during drought, so check the Woodlands Water website for the current schedule before programming a controller.

Can a sprinkler company use Google Local Services Ads?

Google's Local Services Ads list has no standalone irrigation or sprinkler category. It does include lawn care services and landscaping services, and some irrigation companies run under those with matching job types. Start the sign-up with your business address to see what is offered in your part of Houston. If the job types don't fit sprinkler repair, regular search ads are usually the better choice.

How do irrigation companies get more customers in Houston?

Win repair calls through Google Maps and call-focused search ads with strong negative keywords. Then turn each repair into a maintenance plan and an annual backflow reminder. Publish helpful pages on local watering schedules, show your TCEQ license number everywhere, and build referral relationships with home builders, landscapers and pool builders, who send most of the installs worth having.
