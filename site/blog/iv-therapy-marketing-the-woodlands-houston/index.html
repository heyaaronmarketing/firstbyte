It’s 11:40 on a Sunday in late July. Someone at a brunch table on Market Street, still feeling Saturday night, types “iv hydration near me” into their phone. Whoever shows up first in the map pack, with a booking link and an RN who can be at their condo by 1:30, gets that client. Everyone else paid for a website.

IV therapy marketing in The Woodlands and Houston comes down to four jobs. You need to own the Google Maps results for “iv therapy near me” and turn one-time drips into memberships. You want corporate and event bookings that don’t depend on ad clicks. And every ad and page has to survive Texas’s 2025 IV law, Texas Medical Board rules, Google and Meta review, and the FTC.

Most drip bars skip that last one. It can shut down a campaign, or a clinic.

So we’ll start with the rules, since they decide which headlines you’re even allowed to run.

What changed for Texas IV clinics in 2025, and why your ads care

Two things landed in 2025. In January, the Texas Medical Board’s reorganized rules took effect, and the Texas Medical Board’s February 2025 bulletin spelled out that its medical spa standards “now apply to all delegated medical acts, including IV Hydration therapy.” Then House Bill 3749 took effect on September 1, 2025. It covers “elective intravenous therapy,” meaning IV fluids, nutrients or medications a patient seeks to relieve temporary discomfort or improve temporary wellness, given outside a physician’s office or licensed facility. That is the drip bar and the mobile IV van.

Under the law, a physician can delegate ordering elective IV therapy only to a PA or an APRN, and administering it only to a PA, APRN or RN, each under adequate physician supervision. Frier Levitt’s client alert reads that as shutting out LVNs, paramedics, medical assistants and aestheticians from administering elective IVs. If your mobile model was built on paramedics, that’s a staffing problem first and a marketing problem second.

The parts that touch your marketing directly:

  • Name the physician. The TMB bulletin says “all advertising materials must feature the supervising physician’s name.” Separately, 22 TAC §164.3 treats it as deceptive to hide medical directorship or delegation to non-physicians when care happens away from the physician’s primary practice under standing orders. Put the medical director’s name on the site footer, the booking page and the GBP description. Careful clients look for it, and they notice when it’s missing.
  • 22 TAC §169.28 requires the delegating physician’s name and Texas license number, plus how to file a complaint with the TMB, posted in public areas and treatment rooms, and name tags showing each person’s credentials. Your lounge and team photos will show all of this, so get it right before the photographer shows up.
  • Drop “walk in and drip in 10 minutes.” 22 TAC §169.26 requires the physician, PA or APRN to establish a practitioner-patient relationship and keep a medical record before the delegated act, with someone BLS-trained on site. “Same-day appointments, intake takes about 15 minutes” is honest. “No questions asked” is a problem.
  • One upside: “Every IV started by a Texas RN” is now a claim you can actually back up. Put it in the headline.

We’re marketers, not lawyers. Have your healthcare attorney read your medical director agreement against HB 3749 before you spend on ads, and keep a copy of the delegation protocols handy. Ad reviewers and the TMB both ask about this stuff after a complaint, never before.

IV therapy claims you can’t make (the FTC already sued a Texas drip bar)

This isn’t hypothetical. In the FTC’s 2018 case against iV Bars, the FTC went after A & O Enterprises, doing business as iV Bars, which ran clinics in north Texas, New Braunfels and Vail. The company had advertised its “iV Cocktails” as treatments for cancer, congestive heart failure and multiple sclerosis. The FTC described the product as “a simple mix of water, vitamins, minerals, and herbs injected directly into the bloodstream,” and the order barred health claims without competent and reliable scientific evidence. Then-Chairman Joe Simons put it in one line: “Health claims must be supported by competent and reliable scientific evidence.”

Most Houston drip bars won’t claim to cure MS. The everyday risks are softer, and you’ve probably seen them on a sandwich board this week. “Boosts immunity.” “Detox.” “Cures your hangover.” “Anti-aging glutathione.” “Clinically proven.” Every one of those is a health claim, and if you can’t hand a regulator the study behind it, rewrite it.

What tends to be safer is describing the service, the ingredients and the experience: fluids and electrolytes, B vitamins, the 45 minutes in a recliner, who starts the IV, what intake involves. “Feel ready for Monday” is a mood. “Eliminates hangover symptoms in 30 minutes” is a claim. We’d also keep testimonials honest; 22 TAC §164.3 treats testimonials as deceptive without a note on the person’s credentials and a disclosure of any compensation, and models have to be identified as models. Can you run Google Ads for IV therapy?

Yes. There’s no IV-specific certification in Google Ads, and most drip bars run search campaigns without much trouble. Where they do hit friction, it traces back to Google’s healthcare and medicines policy.

Prescription drug names. Some add-ons are prescription drugs, such as ondansetron (Zofran) for nausea. Per Google’s restricted drug terms page and the October 2025 policy update, US advertisers no longer need certification to mention prescription drug terms in ads and landing pages, but you “must be certified in order to keyword-target these terms.” So “anti-nausea add-on” in your ad text is fine, while bidding on “zofran iv near me” can get disapproved. We just don’t bid on drug names.

Audiences. Google lists “invasive medical procedures, such as cosmetic surgery, surgery, or injections” under its Health sensitive-interest category. If your ads land in that bucket, advertiser-curated audiences, including Customer Match and your own remarketing lists, can’t be used. So the account runs on search intent and location, and your client list stays in your email and text tools where it belongs.

Telehealth is the third wrinkle. If you sell virtual consults that end in a prescription, Google treats you as a telemedicine provider, which in the US needs LegitScript accreditation plus Google certification. Most in-person drip bars aren’t in that group.

IV spa advertising on Facebook and Instagram

Meta works for IV, but mostly for awareness, offers and events. Someone who needs a drip in the next two hours is on Google, not scrolling Reels.

The policy that kills the most IV ads is Meta’s personal attributes policy, which bans ads that assert or imply a personal health attribute. Its own rejected examples include “Do you have diabetes?” So “Hungover?” or “Feeling run down?” aimed at the viewer is the classic rejection, and we still see agencies submit it every summer. Describe the service: “Hydration drips in The Woodlands, started by an RN, about 45 minutes.”

The health and wellness ad policy doesn’t mention IV at all. It does require 18+ targeting for weight loss and cosmetic offers and bans “negative self-perception” tactics, so if you bundle a beauty or slimming drip, those rules follow that ad. For anything that references drinking, we set the age floor at 21 by choice.

Then there’s data. Starting in early 2025, Meta began classifying health advertisers’ data sources and limiting which pixel events they can send; Cardinal Digital Marketing’s write-up describes tiers where some providers lose lower-funnel conversion events and custom conversions entirely. Meta instant forms and on-platform engagement carry more weight now. Before you assume your “Booked” event is still reaching Meta, open Events Manager and look.

A typical IV campaign from us is a short video of the actual lounge and the actual RN (no stock nurse in a lab coat), an intro offer for new clients and a lead form or booking link. We swap the creative every few weeks in summer because people tire of it fast. Our paid social team handles the policy back-and-forth so you don’t have to.

How to get more clients for an IV hydration business from Google Maps

Most first-time IV clients search, scan the map pack, check reviews and book. Your Google Business Profile is the cheapest ad you’ll ever run.

  • Primary category. Google added “IV therapy service” as a category in October 2024, according to Sterling Sky’s category change log. If you still sit under “Medical spa” or “Health and beauty shop” from when you opened, test switching the primary to IV therapy service and keeping the others as secondary.
  • Mobile IV setup. If you don’t see clients at an address, run as a service-area business. Google’s service area rules allow up to 20 areas and an overall area no more than about two hours’ drive from your base. A Woodlands-based van might list The Woodlands, Spring, Conroe, Shenandoah, Oak Ridge North, Tomball, Magnolia and Kingwood.
  • List each drip as a service with a plain description and a starting price. People compare, and the bar that hides prices loses to the one that doesn’t.
  • Put the medical director’s name and “IVs started by Texas-licensed RNs” in the description. Those are the two things careful clients want to know.

For photos, shoot the lounge, the team in name tags, the van and the bags (with brand-name drug labels turned away from the camera). Ask every client for a review by text about an hour after the session, with no incentive attached.

A GBP won’t rank you in Katy if you’re based off Woodlands Parkway. Distance still counts, and no amount of keyword stuffing changes that. When IV demand spikes around The Woodlands and Houston

IV demand up here is predictable. You can usually see the busy weeks coming a month out.

Heat is the big one, and July and August are the long stretch. The National Weather Service’s August 2023 summary called August 2023 the warmest August on record at Bush Intercontinental, with an average high of 102.7°F and a peak of 109°F. Outdoor workers, golfers, weekend soccer parents and anyone who spent Saturday at a pool party end up searching for hydration.

Race calendars come next. The 2027 Chevron Houston Marathon weekend is January 16 and 17. Ironman Texas is The Woodlands’ big one; Community Impact reported proposed race dates through 2030, including April 24, 2027, with athletes from all 50 states and 25 countries. Race weeks bring visitors in hotels near the Waterway, which is mobile IV territory. Be careful with athlete marketing, though: say “hydration and recovery drips,” not “perform better.”

Market Street and Waterway Square brunch traffic is steady every weekend. The group-booking peaks are December office parties, New Year’s Day, wedding weekends and bachelorette groups at the hotels.

For a typical Woodlands bar we’d raise search budgets in late June and hold them through Labor Day. Race-week mobile offers go live two weeks before each event, and group packages start getting pushed around mid-November, which feels early until you watch the December calendar fill. Pull your own booking history by month before you set any of this. It beats our chart.

IV membership pricing that keeps clients coming back

One-off drips make for a jumpy calendar. A membership smooths it out, and the membership page is the one page on your site we’d fight hardest for.

Keep the structure simple. One drip a month at a member rate, a discount on add-ons and extra drips, priority mobile booking on weekends, and a guest pass every quarter (which is your referral program in disguise). Members should be able to see on one page what they pay, what they get, when they’re billed and how to cancel. The FTC’s recent weight-loss telehealth case, which we cover in our weight loss clinic marketing guide, turned largely on hidden recurring charges and slow cancellations. Don’t give anyone that opening.

A hypothetical to size it: a drip bar near Hughes Landing signs 120 members at $149 a month. That’s $17,880 a month before a single walk-in, with a known staffing load. Your price will differ. But “120 members” is a number you can plan ads around. “More clients” isn’t.

Rebooking matters as much as signing. We’d text clients about three to four weeks after each visit and check in when a member skips a month. A short note before the heat arrives in June works well too, as long as it doesn’t drift into health claims.

Corporate, event and mobile IV: the channels that don’t need ad clicks

The drip bars we’d bet on get a third of their revenue from somewhere other than paid search. What these channels cost is phone calls and coffee meetings.

  • Corporate wellness. The office parks along Research Forest, Lake Robbins and the Springwoods Village campuses near I-45 and the Grand Parkway are full of HR teams planning health fairs and summer events. Offer an on-site hydration day with a set number of chairs and a per-person price. Check whether the employer’s benefits or legal team wants anything in writing first.
  • Hotels and concierges. Business travelers and wedding parties staying near the Waterway ask the front desk. A one-page sheet with your RN credentials, medical director’s name and response time does more than a discount code.
  • Race expos, golf tournaments and charity runs. Get a booth at the expo. Skip the recovery tent at the finish line; medical tents are a different conversation.
  • CrossFit boxes, Pilates studios and run clubs will cross-promote if you bring them something, even just a member discount. Our sports and fitness page shows how we market that crowd.
  • Bachelorette and wedding groups. Build a group page with a minimum headcount, a travel fee and a deposit. These book months out.

For mobile, publish your travel zones and minimums plainly. A flat travel fee inside The Woodlands, Spring and Shenandoah and a higher one for Conroe or Kingwood avoids awkward calls. And staff it to the law: under HB 3749 the person starting the IV at a client’s house has to be a PA, APRN or RN.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more clients for my IV hydration business?

Start with Google Maps: choose the IV therapy service category, list every drip with a price, name your medical director and ask every client for a review. Then run tight Google search ads on near-me and mobile terms, sell a monthly membership, and build corporate, hotel and gym partnerships. Memberships and partnerships usually add more steady revenue than extra ad spend.

Can you advertise IV therapy on Google Ads?

Yes. There is no IV-specific certification. You can describe your drips and mention prescription add-ons in ad text and landing pages, but keyword-targeting prescription drug names requires Google certification. Health-related ads also can't use Customer Match or your own remarketing lists, and virtual prescribing services need LegitScript accreditation. Avoid disease or cure claims in every ad.

Do I need a medical director for an IV therapy business in Texas?

Yes. Texas treats IV hydration as the practice of medicine, so a Texas-licensed physician must delegate and supervise it. Under HB 3749, effective September 1, 2025, only PAs and APRNs can be delegated ordering, and only PAs, APRNs and RNs can administer elective IVs. The Texas Medical Board also expects advertising to name the supervising physician.

Can paramedics or LVNs start IVs for a mobile IV company in Texas?

Not for elective IV therapy under HB 3749. The law lets a physician delegate administration only to physician assistants, advanced practice registered nurses and registered nurses under adequate supervision. Healthcare attorneys read that as excluding LVNs, paramedics and medical assistants. Check your staffing and medical director agreement with a Texas healthcare attorney before you advertise mobile service.

What can't you say in IV therapy ads?

Avoid claims that a drip treats, cures or prevents a disease, boosts immunity, detoxes the body or is clinically proven unless you have solid scientific evidence. The FTC took action against a Texas IV chain over claims like these in 2018. On Meta, also avoid copy that implies the viewer has a condition, such as asking if they are hungover or sick.
