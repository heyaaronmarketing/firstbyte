Kitchen Remodeling Marketing for High-End Jobs in The Woodlands

Kitchen remodeling marketing for high-end projects comes down to proof and patience. Show finished local kitchens and baths in video and on detailed project pages, buy Google searches from homeowners who are ready to talk, stay in front of them for the six to twelve months they take to decide, and measure what each signed project cost you, not each lead.

That’s different from marketing a $9,000 tub-to-shower conversion, and it’s why generic “10 kitchen remodeling marketing ideas” lists don’t help much. Below is how we’d market a kitchen and bath company selling to homeowners in The Woodlands, Kingwood and the Memorial area, with real cost data, ad settings and the follow-up that closes long decisions. If you’re after more leads of every kind first, start with our guide on how to get more remodeling leads in Houston.

What kitchen and bath remodels cost, and why that shapes your marketing

According to the 2025 Cost vs. Value Report for the West South Central region (the region that includes Texas), an average midrange minor kitchen remodel cost $26,762, a midrange major kitchen $77,742 and an upscale major kitchen $151,557. A midrange bath remodel came in at $22,995 and an upscale bath at $73,185. Custom work in places like Carlton Woods or Bunker Hill Village can run well past those averages.

Now look at resale. In the same regional report, the upscale major kitchen recouped just 34.6% of its cost at sale. People spending $150,000 on a kitchen aren’t doing it for the appraisal. In the 2025 Houzz & Home Study, 61% of renovating homeowners planned to stay at least 11 more years, and the median spend on large kitchens (200 square feet or more) was $55,000.

So the marketing has to sell living in the house, not return on investment. “Add value to your home” is the message of a flipper. “Finally cook for twelve at Thanksgiving without bumping into each other” is the message of the person who’s about to sign.

Who you’re marketing to in The Woodlands, Kingwood and Memorial

High-end kitchen and bath buyers north and west of Houston fall into a few groups, and each one needs a slightly different pitch:

  • Original owners in 1970s and 1980s villages. Grogan’s Mill, Panther Creek and Cochran’s Crossing in The Woodlands, older sections of Kingwood. Closed kitchens, small primary baths, soffits. They want walls opened up.
  • Owners of 2000s builder homes. Sterling Ridge, Creekside Park and newer Kingwood sections. The layout works, but the finishes are builder grade and tired. These buyers want a design refresh more than demolition.
  • Memorial and the Villages. Bunker Hill, Hunters Creek, Piney Point and the other Memorial Villages are separate small cities with their own permit rules, and larger lots. More full-gut and addition work.
  • Empty nesters planning to age in place. Curbless showers, wider doors, better lighting. They often decide faster than families.

According to the Houzz data, Baby Boomers and Gen X homeowners did most of the renovating, Gen X was the most likely to take on projects of $150,000 or more, and 56% of renovating homeowners owned their homes outright. That’s a buyer with equity and time to deliberate, which is exactly why the consideration cycle runs long.

Kitchen remodeling marketing ideas built for a long decision

A kitchen buyer usually moves through the same stages: saving photos, shortlisting three or four remodelers, a design consultation, proposals and financing, then a signature. That can take a few months or more than a year. Most remodelers market only to the last stage and lose the people who weren’t ready the day they clicked.

Marketing ideas that fit each stage:

  • Inspiration stage: short before/after videos on Instagram and YouTube, a Houzz profile full of finished local projects, and project pages on your site that Google can index.
  • Shortlist stage: Google search ads and a strong Business Profile, so you’re one of the names they write down.
  • Consult stage: a design-consult offer with a clear deliverable, like “a floor plan and a budget range in 10 days,” rather than “free estimate.”
  • Proposal stage: retargeting and email that answer the questions people sit on: timeline, what living through a remodel is like, how RDRC approval works in The Woodlands, financing.

Before/after video and project pages that sell a $90,000 kitchen

Nobody signs a six-figure contract from a paragraph. They sign because they saw a kitchen like theirs, in a house like theirs, finished by a crew that looked like it knew what it was doing.

A simple video plan that a small company can actually keep up:

  1. Shoot the “before” every time, from the same three spots, before demo. Phone, horizontal and vertical. This is the step everybody forgets.
  2. Capture one or two mid-project clips: the wall coming out, cabinets going in, a tile detail.
  3. Shoot the “after” from the same three spots in good daylight, plus a slow 60-second walkthrough.
  4. Cut three versions: a 15 to 30 second vertical before/after for Reels and Shorts, a 60 to 90 second walkthrough for YouTube and your site, and stills for Houzz and Google.
  5. Ask the homeowner for a one-minute on-camera review at the final walkthrough. Video reviews from a real neighbor beat any script.

Then give every project its own page with the video embedded, the scope, the budget range, the timeline and who handled permits and HOA approval. Settings we’d check in any kitchen and bath account:

  • Negative keywords from day one: ideas, pictures, cheap, diy, ikea, home depot, lowes, cabinets for sale, jobs, salary, rental, plus “refacing” and “countertops only” if you don’t sell them on their own.
  • Location set to “Presence: people in or regularly in your targeted locations,” so you aren’t paying for someone in another state researching kitchens.
  • Separate budgets for kitchen and bath. Bath campaigns usually get more, cheaper leads and will eat the whole budget if you let them share.
  • Exact and phrase match first. Broad match on “kitchen” finds a lot of people shopping for toasters.
  • Ad copy with a price frame and your process: “Most kitchens $60K to $150K. Design, RDRC approval and build, one team.”

Bathroom remodeling marketing: a smaller ticket and a faster decision

Bathroom remodeling marketing runs on the same pieces with a shorter clock. A midrange bath in the region averaged $22,995 in the Cost vs. Value data, and a universal design bath (wider doors, a curbless shower, grab bars built in) averaged $42,183 in the national Cost vs. Value figures. Homeowners often decide on a primary bath in weeks, not months.

A few adjustments for bath campaigns:

  • Lead with timeline (“most primary baths finished in four to six weeks”), because living without the main shower is the biggest fear.
  • Split hall-bath refreshes from primary-bath remodels in your ads and forms. They’re different budgets and often different buyers.
  • Use aging-in-place as its own campaign and page. These buyers search differently, respond well to video of a finished curbless shower, and care about safety more than tile.
  • Expect more competition from tub-to-shower franchises on price. Your answer is design, reviews and finished local work, not a lower number.

Financing messaging that helps without breaking ad rules

Financing helps close kitchens, but most of these buyers don’t lead with it. In the Houzz study, 84% of renovating homeowners paid with savings, 29% used credit cards and 12% took out a secured home loan, per Contractor magazine’s summary of the Houzz study. Higher-end projects were the most likely to use loans of $50,000 to $200,000.

So mention financing, but don’t make it the headline for a Woodlands kitchen campaign. “Financing available” on the project page and in follow-up email is enough for most of this audience.

If you do advertise payments, the wording has rules attached:

  • Truth in Lending. Under Regulation Z’s “triggering terms”, phrases like “payments as low as $299 a month” or “terms up to 120 months” require nearby disclosure of the APR and repayment terms. Get the exact wording from your finance partner, and use “financing available” in ads if you can’t fit the disclosure.
  • Meta’s special ad categories. Per Jon Loomer’s summary of Meta’s categories, Meta’s housing category lists “housing repairs” and its credit category lists “long-term financing.” Ads in those categories can’t target by ZIP code, age or gender and need at least a 15-mile radius, per Meta’s developer documentation. A financing-led ad aimed only at affluent ZIP codes is the kind Meta may reject. Our pool builder guide covers how to work within this.

Retargeting and nurturing for six- to twelve-month decisions

Retargeting is how you stay in the room while a homeowner thinks it over. Google Ads lets you keep a website visitor in an audience segment for up to 540 days, but the default is 30. For a kitchen buyer who takes nine months, a 30-day list forgets them before they’ve finished collecting bids. Change it. For the basics of how it works, see retargeting explained.

A nurture sequence doesn’t need to be fancy. A monthly email with one finished project, one useful explainer (“How RDRC approval works in Alden Bridge,” “What it’s like to live through a kitchen remodel”), and an invitation to a showroom visit or a design consult will keep you on the shortlist. Showroom events and open houses at a just-finished project, with the homeowner’s blessing, are some of the best offline conversion points this business has.

The number that matters: cost per signed project

A kitchen and bath company can have great-looking lead numbers and an awful cost per signed project, or the reverse. Measure the whole chain. Say you spend $4,000 a month on Google Ads and get 40 leads at $100 each. Fourteen book a design consult, six get a proposal and two sign kitchens at $85,000. Your cost per signed project is $2,000, about 2.4% of the contract value. That’s a healthy number, even though $100 a lead might have looked expensive to you.

To see it in Google Ads, mark consults and signed contracts as offline conversions and import them, so Google learns to find people who sign, not people who fill out forms. Use call tracking so phone leads aren’t invisible. Our guide to cost per lead covers the math in more detail.

Before next month’s budget meeting, pull your last ten signed kitchen and bath projects, write down where each one came from, and set that list beside where the ad money went. They rarely match on the first try. Two settings are worth a look while you’re in there: how long your website audiences last, and whether any Meta ads that mention remodeling or financing are running under the right special ad category. Frequently asked questions

What is the best marketing for a kitchen remodeling company?

For high-end kitchens, the best mix is finished local projects shown in video and on individual project pages, Google search ads on project and city terms, a Google Business Profile updated with weekly photos and reviews, and retargeting plus monthly email for the months homeowners take to decide. Measure cost per signed project, because cheap leads that never sign cost more than expensive leads that do.

How much should a remodeling company spend on marketing?

Many remodelers budget a few percent of revenue, but a better approach is to work from signed jobs. Decide what you can afford to spend to sign one kitchen or bath, then multiply by the number of projects you want each month. A company signing four $80,000 kitchens a month can justify far more ad spend than one doing four $15,000 hall baths.

Does Google Ads work for kitchen remodelers?

Yes, when the campaigns are split by project type, use exact and phrase match keywords with a strong negative list, and send clicks to a kitchen page with real local projects. It works poorly when one broad remodeling campaign sends everyone to the homepage. Import signed contracts as offline conversions so Google learns to find buyers instead of browsers.

Is Houzz or Instagram better for kitchen and bath marketing?

They do different jobs. Houzz is where design-minded homeowners compare remodelers and read reviews, so a complete free profile matters. Instagram keeps you in front of people during a long decision with before and after Reels and retargeting ads. Most kitchen and bath companies should keep a strong Houzz profile and spend their time and ad money on Instagram and Google.

Should I advertise financing for kitchen remodels?

Mention it, but don't lead with it for high-end buyers. The 2025 Houzz study found most renovating homeowners paid with savings. If an ad states a monthly payment or loan term, federal Truth in Lending rules require APR and repayment disclosures nearby, and Meta may require a special ad category with limited targeting. Financing available on the page and in follow-up email is usually enough.
