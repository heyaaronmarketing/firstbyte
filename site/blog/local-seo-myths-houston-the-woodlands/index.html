Ten myths. All of them came up in a sales call, a client meeting or a networking lunch in The Woodlands or Houston this year. Several came from people who had paid someone to tell them.

Local SEO attracts myths because the results are visible and the reasons aren’t. You can see that the HVAC company in Spring is in the map pack and you aren’t. You can’t see why. So people fill the gap with whatever they heard last.

For each myth below we’ve gone to Google’s own documentation (Business Profile Help, Search Central and Google Ads Help) and linked the page. Each one gets three parts: the myth, what’s actually true, and what to do about it. Where something is our experience rather than Google’s word, we say so.

Myth 1: Putting “Houston” or “The Woodlands” in your Google business name helps you rank

Myth. Rename “Juniper Plumbing” to “Juniper Plumbing The Woodlands Water Heater Repair” and you’ll climb the map pack.

What’s actually true. It might work for a while. That’s why the myth survives. But Google’s guidelines for representing your business say your name should reflect your real-world business name, and “including unnecessary information in your business name isn’t permitted.” Google’s own examples ban taglines, service info and location details, such as “Holiday Inn (I-93 at Exit 2).” Search Engine Land lists keyword-stuffed names among the top causes of profile suspensions, and competitors can suggest edits to your name whenever they like.

What to do. Use the name on your sign, your filing and your invoices. If you want “The Woodlands” in your name, it has to be in your actual brand. (And in The Woodlands, putting it on your sign has its own trademark review; see our sign rules guide.) Put the keywords in your primary category, your services list and your website instead.

Myth 2: You have to post on Google Business Profile every day

Myth. Daily posts tell Google you’re active, and active profiles rank.

What’s actually true. Google’s posts help page describes three post types (updates, offers and events), explains that posts older than six months are archived unless a date range is set, and makes no claim about posting frequency or ranking. Google’s page on local ranking, which is Google’s own list of what affects local results, doesn’t mention posts at all.

What to do. Post when you have something a customer would care about: a seasonal offer, an event, new hours for the holidays, photos of a finished job in Kingwood. In accounts we manage, two to four useful posts a month is plenty. Spend the time you save on photos and review replies, which customers actually look at.

Myth 3: Review count is everything

Myth. Whoever has the most reviews wins, so the plan is to get to 500.

What’s actually true. Reviews matter. Google says “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking,” per Google’s page on local ranking. But reviews feed one of three factors. The other two are relevance (how well your profile matches the search) and distance (how far you are from the searcher). A business with 900 reviews in Katy will usually lose a “near me” search in Tomball to a business with 90 reviews in Tomball.

What to do. Ask every customer, steadily, the same way. Don’t buy reviews, don’t offer discounts for them and don’t only ask happy customers. Google Maps’ prohibited content policy prohibits incentives and selective solicitation. Reply to reviews too; Google’s review management page notes that helpful replies show you’re responsive. Our Google reviews guide has scripts.

Ranking is only half of what reviews do. The other half happens after a customer finds you.

Myth 4: You need an office in every suburb you want to rank in

Myth. To show up in Katy, Cypress, Sugar Land and The Woodlands, you need an address in each. A virtual office will do.

What’s actually true. Distance is a real ranking factor, so a physical location does help near that location. But a rented address where you don’t operate “isn’t eligible for a Business Profile,” per Google’s guidelines for representing your business, and P.O. boxes and remote mailboxes aren’t acceptable. Service businesses can list up to 20 service areas, covering no more than about 2 hours of driving from where they’re based, per Google’s service area help page.

What to do. If you have real, staffed locations, give each its own profile. If you don’t, set your service area honestly, build a genuine page for each area you actually work in, and use paid search to cover suburbs where you won’t win the map pack. Our explainer on The Woodlands, Spring and Conroe addresses covers the north-side version of this problem.

Myth 5: SEO is a one-and-done project

Myth. Pay for SEO once, during the website build, and you’re set for years.

What’s actually true. Google’s SEO Starter Guide says some changes “might take effect in a few hours, others could take several months,” and that “there are no secrets here that’ll automatically rank your site first.” Rankings move because competitors keep publishing, Google keeps updating, and your own information goes stale: hours, services, staff, photos.

A composite of what we see in Houston audits: a business pays for a site in 2021, ranks decently in 2022, and by 2025 the hours on Google are wrong, the photos show a storefront that’s been remodeled, the newest review reply is two years old, and three competitors in Cypress have added real service pages. Nothing “broke.” Everyone else just kept going.

What to do. Set up a small monthly routine and keep it going. It doesn’t need to be expensive. Before hurricane season and before the holidays, add a quick check of special hours, since those are the weeks people search “open now.” For how long the early work takes to show up, see how long SEO takes in Houston and The Woodlands.

Myth 6: Running Google Ads boosts your organic rankings

Myth. Spend on Google Ads and Google will reward you in the free results, or stop spending and it will punish you.

What’s actually true. Google Ads help puts it in one sentence: “Google’s search results are independent of Google’s advertising programs.” On the local side, Google’s page on local ranking says “there’s no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google.”

The myth usually comes from a real pattern. When a business runs ads, its name shows up more often, more people search for it by name, and branded traffic rises. That’s demand, not a ranking favor from Google. And if an ad and an organic listing show for the same search, some of the clicks you used to get for free will go to the ad. That’s worth measuring before you assume ads are “helping SEO.”

What to do. Treat ads and SEO as two separate tools. Ads buy visibility now, for searches where you can’t rank yet. SEO builds visibility you don’t pay for per click. Many Houston service businesses need both for a while. We compare them in SEO vs. paid ads, and our search marketing team runs both.

Myth 7: You need a page for every suburb and ZIP code

Myth. Publish “Plumber Katy,” “Plumber Cypress,” “Plumber Spring” and 40 more pages with the town name swapped, and you’ll rank everywhere.

What’s actually true. Google’s spam policies list “multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page” as doorway abuse, and “blocks of text that list cities and regions that a web page is trying to rank for” as keyword stuffing. Mass-produced pages with little value fall under scaled content abuse.

What to do. Build area pages only where you have something real to say: jobs you did there, local photos, reviews from customers in that town, specifics like “we see a lot of slab leaks in older Meyerland homes.” Five strong pages beat fifty thin ones. Our local landing pages guide shows the difference.

Myth 8: Keywords in the domain name, meta keywords and word count still move rankings

Myth. Buy bestplumberhoustontx.com, fill the meta keywords tag and make every page 2,000 words.

What’s actually true. Google’s SEO Starter Guide is blunt on all three. “Google Search doesn’t use the keywords meta tag.” Keywords in a domain name “have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs.” And “the length of the content alone doesn’t matter for ranking purposes.” There’s no magic heading count either.

What to do. Pick a domain that matches your brand and is easy to say on the radio. Write pages as long as they need to be to answer the customer’s question, and no longer. A clear price range and a phone number above the fold usually do more than another 800 words.

Myth 9: Duplicate content gets you penalized

Myth. If your service description appears on two pages, or matches your franchisor’s site, Google will penalize you.

What’s actually true. Per Google’s SEO Starter Guide, duplicate content “is not a violation of our spam policies” and isn’t something that causes a manual action. It’s inefficient: Google picks one version to show, which may not be the one you want.

What to do. Don’t panic over a repeated paragraph. Do write unique copy for your most important pages, so they have a reason to rank on their own. Franchise owners in Sugar Land or The Woodlands should make their location page clearly local rather than a copy of corporate.

Myth 10: Once you’re in the map pack, you’re there for everyone

Myth. “We’re number one on Google.” Usually said by the owner, searching from their own office.

What’s actually true. Distance is one of Google’s three local factors, and Google says it uses the searcher’s location, or what it knows about it, per Google’s page on local ranking. Results for “dentist near me” in Hughes Landing look different from results on the Grand Parkway at 249.

What to do. Check rankings from several points across your service area, not just your desk. A grid-based rank tracker does this well. Then judge local SEO on calls, direction requests and booked jobs, which you can see in your profile’s performance report and your call tracking. Our map pack guide explains how to read those numbers.

What actually moves local SEO in Houston and The Woodlands

Strip out the myths and what’s left is unglamorous. Google’s own list in Google’s page on local ranking comes down to complete and accurate information, the right categories, reviews you reply to, photos, and a website that other sites link to.

We’d add two from experience. First, answer the phone. Ranking well and sending calls to voicemail is the most expensive local SEO mistake we see. Second, measure with real numbers. If you can’t say how many calls came from your Google profile last month, start there; our tracking guide shows how.

Frequently asked questions

Does adding Houston or The Woodlands to my Google business name help ranking?

It can appear to help briefly, but Google’s guidelines say your Business Profile name must match your real-world business name and that unnecessary information, including location details and service keywords, is not permitted. Keyword-stuffed names are a common cause of suspensions, and competitors can suggest edits. Use your real name and put keywords in your categories, services and website.

How often should I post on my Google Business Profile?

Google does not say posting frequency affects ranking, and its list of local ranking factors does not mention posts. Posts older than six months are archived unless they have a date range. Post when you have something useful, such as an offer, an event, holiday hours or photos of recent work. For most Houston and The Woodlands businesses, two to four good posts a month is enough.

Does Google Ads improve my organic or map rankings?

No. Google Ads Help states that Google’s search results are independent of its advertising programs, and Google Business Profile Help says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Ads and SEO are separate tools. Many local businesses run ads for searches where they do not rank yet while building organic visibility over time.

Do I need an office in each Houston suburb to rank there?

Not to appear at all, but distance is a ranking factor, so a real location helps most near that location. Virtual offices and mailbox addresses are not eligible for Google Business Profiles. Service businesses can set up to 20 service areas within about two hours of driving. Build genuine area pages and use paid search for suburbs where you cannot win the map pack.

Is local SEO a one-time project?

No. Google’s SEO Starter Guide says changes can take anywhere from a few hours to several months to have an effect, and that no secret will automatically rank a site first. Competitors keep publishing, Google keeps updating and your own details go stale. A modest monthly routine of profile updates, review replies, photos and page improvements keeps results from slipping.
