Monday morning at a plumbing company in Katy. The office manager scrolls last week’s Local Services Ads leads. A man selling “Google Maps ranking.” A woman asking for a water heater install in Beaumont. A call about sprinkler repair, which they don’t do. Two calls from the same number, both charged. Somewhere in there are four real jobs, and she’d like the other money back.

She goes looking for the “dispute” button. It isn’t there anymore.

The short version: Google stopped taking manual Local Services Ads disputes in July 2024 and replaced them with automatic credits. You can still rate every lead, which feeds Google’s models and can earn a bonus credit, but you can’t file a case and argue for a refund. Since August 2026, LSA accounts have been moving into Google Ads as Performance Max campaigns with pay-per-lead goals, and the same rules carry over. So getting credited now comes down to three habits: rate every bad lead, set your account up so fewer bad leads arrive, and watch the charge status column so you know what Google already refunded.

Here’s how each piece works, with the actual menu paths for both the old dashboard and Google Ads. If you’re still deciding whether LSA is worth it at all, start with Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads.

What Google charges for, and what it doesn’t

Google’s rules on charges are more specific than most owners realize. Per Google’s “How leads work” page, you’re charged for a valid lead, which means one of these:

  • You answer a call, or call the customer back and talk to them.
  • You miss a call during business hours and the caller stays on the line more than 20 seconds.
  • The customer sends a text, email or voicemail.
  • You receive a booking request (U.S. and Canada).

And you shouldn’t be charged for these:

  • Leads Google’s systems flag as spam or duplicates at first contact.
  • Missed calls under 20 seconds.
  • Callers who hang up on your phone menu without pressing a key.
  • Unanswered calls outside your business hours or ad schedule.
  • Repeat contact from the same phone number or email within 15 days of the last interaction. After 15 days of no contact, a new call becomes a new lead.

Two details trip people up. The phone-menu timer starts at the first key press, so a long greeting before “press 1” isn’t counted against you. And “business hours” means the hours in your Google Business Profile, so if your profile says 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. and you stop answering at 5, those two hours of missed calls can be charged.

Why the dispute button disappeared

Until mid-2024, you could dispute a charged lead within 30 days and a person or a model would review it. In July 2024, Google switched to automated credits: its models review every lead and credit invalid ones, typically within 30 days, per Search Engine Land. Google said the new system would credit the same number of leads or more on average.

Not everyone agreed it worked. In February 2025, Search Engine Roundtable reported complaints from agencies about out-of-area and out-of-industry leads they could no longer dispute. Google’s Ads Liaison, Ginny Marvin, said Google credits more leads overall than under the manual process, and that manual disputes had been underused by most advertisers and overused by a few.

Then came the bigger change. Google removed credits for two categories outright: “job type not serviced” and “geo not serviced,” per Google’s lead credit page. Those used to be the most common disputes. Now they’re your problem to prevent with settings, which is the most important thing in this whole post.

Credits also aren’t available at all for health care verticals, tax specialists or advertisers in EMEA, per Google’s automated credits page. If you’re a dentist or a chiropractor in The Woodlands running LSA, every charged lead is final.

How automatic credits work now

Per Google’s automated credits page, Google checks a lead when the customer first makes contact, and anything it judges invalid or low quality isn’t charged. Charged leads are then reassessed over time, and Google’s “How leads work” page describes that reassessment running over 30 days. Some poor-quality leads are charged and then credited within the first 24 hours.

When a credit happens, it goes to your account balance, usually within 30 days. Your invoice still shows the original charge. That catches bookkeepers off guard: the invoice won’t match your spend, and the credit shows up as a separate balance adjustment.

To see where each lead stands, look at the charge status. It reads one of four values: Charged, Not charged, In review or Credited. “In review” means Google hasn’t decided yet; per Google’s invalid-lead troubleshooter, if the lead isn’t a good match a credit will be applied.

How to rate a lead (old dashboard and Google Ads)

Rating is the closest thing left to a dispute. It doesn’t guarantee money back, but it’s the only feedback channel Google built for this, and it can trigger a bonus credit.

In the old Local Services Ads dashboard: sign in, open the menu, select Leads, open the lead, and select Rate this lead in the top right to open the survey, per Google’s automated credits page.

In Google Ads, after your account migrates: go to Goals, then Conversions, then Leads, per Google’s transition guide. That page replaces the LSA inbox. You can read and reply to message leads there, download a CSV, and, according to PPC Land, it keeps a lead feedback form on each lead.

The survey runs from very satisfied to very dissatisfied. According to PPC Land’s breakdown of the rating survey, the dissatisfied reasons are geo mismatch, job type mismatch, not ready to book, spam, duplicate and solicitation, and “other” needs a written comment. Satisfied reasons include booked customer and high-value service. The same source says a rating can produce a bonus credit up to an unpublished cap, and that autocrediting runs on its own whether you rate or not. Agencies with many accounts can also send ratings through the Google Ads API.

Our advice is to rate every lead, good and bad, within a few days. The good ratings matter too: they tell the model what a booked job looks like for you. And when you choose “other,” write something specific: “Caller wanted irrigation repair; we only do residential plumbing; service type is off.” Vague comments teach the system nothing.

What not to do: don’t mark a real customer as spam because they didn’t book. “Not ready to book” exists for that. Misusing ratings doesn’t get you more money, and it can make your lead matching worse.

Fix the settings that create unpaid-for junk

Since geo and job-type mismatches aren’t credited anymore, your settings are your refund policy. In Google Ads these live under Campaigns, then Settings, per Google’s campaign settings page.

  • Service types. Each business category has service type chips you can switch on or off. A plumber who doesn’t do septic or irrigation should turn those off today. You need at least one active per category.
  • Locations. Target cities, counties or ZIP codes you actually serve. Google’s Google’s pay-per-lead best practices say not to set service areas more than two hours of driving from your business. In Greater Houston, that limit is far wider than most companies should go. A Conroe company that lists all of Harris County will get calls from Pearland it doesn’t want.
  • Ad schedule. Run all the time, use a custom schedule, or sync to your Business Profile hours. If nobody answers after 6 p.m., don’t run after 6 p.m.
  • Business categories. These come from your Business Profile. Opt out of any category that brings the wrong work.
  • Direct business search. When it’s on, your ads can show for searches of your own business name, and Google says it screens out returning customers. If existing customers keep calling through your ad, check this setting.
  • Contact information. Make sure call leads route to a phone a person answers, not the owner’s cell under a sink.

Houston adds one twist: storms. After a hurricane or a hard freeze, volume spikes and so do out-of-area calls, because people from Galveston, Beaumont or Baytown search for anyone who can come. Decide in advance whether you’ll widen your area for those weeks, and change it back when the surge ends.

Call recordings: your best evidence

In the U.S. and Canada, Google records inbound calls that come through the Google forwarding number on Local Services Ads, and callers hear a pre-recorded notice before you hear the “Call from Google” announcement, per Google’s lead management page. Listening to recordings is available in the U.S. Recordings and messages aren’t available for health care or tax specialists. Older recordings carry over into Google Ads when your account migrates, per Google’s transition guide.

Use them. Before you rate a lead as spam or solicitation, listen to 20 seconds. You’ll find that some “bad” leads were real customers who got a bad first answer, which is a training problem, not a lead-quality one.

Regular Google Ads campaigns now handle recording a bit differently. Google turned call recording on by default so its AI can judge whether calls are qualified, and only qualified calls count as conversions, per its page on AI-qualified call leads. Recording stays off if you turned it off before, or if Google identifies you as health care or financial services. To change it, go to Admin, then Account settings, open Call ads, and set Call recording, per Google’s call recording settings page. If you turn it off, conversions fall back to call duration. Callers hear an automated notice, and you’re expected to tell your staff calls are recorded.

Budgets and bidding: old modes and new

Bidding affects which leads you get as much as how many. The old LSA dashboard had three modes, per Google’s LSA bidding page:

ModeHow it workedAfter migration to Google Ads
Maximize leadsGoogle set bids to get the most leads for your budget; Google suggested a budget that supports at least 10 leads a weekBecomes Maximize Conversions, the default
Target cost per leadAutomated, with an optional targetOne campaign-level Target CPA replaces per-category targets
Max per leadYou set the most you’d pay for any leadGone. Manual bidding isn’t supported.

Budgets change too. Weekly budgets become a daily average (the weekly number divided by seven), and monthly spend won’t exceed that daily average times 30.4, per Google’s transition guide. Because you pay per lead, daily spend swings with demand. Google’s Google’s pay-per-lead best practices recommend considering a Target CPA only if you consistently get more than 15 leads a month.

The single Target CPA is a real issue for multi-trade companies. If you do HVAC and plumbing, an AC replacement lead is worth far more than a faucet repair, and one target across both will under-bid one and over-bid the other. Google’s answer is separate campaigns per vertical, which you set up through the Local Services page, since new pay-per-lead campaigns can’t be created inside Google Ads yet. Splitting gives each campaign less data, so only do it when the value gap is large.

Ranking: reviews and responsiveness

Per Google’s ad ranking page, LSA ranking comes down to your bid, how likely your ad is to produce a lead, and profile quality. “Likely to produce a lead” includes your responsiveness to calls and messages, what the customer searched for, how relevant your services and bio are, and whether you offer message and booking leads. Profile quality covers your rating, number of reviews, average response time, photos and completed verification. If you have several locations serving the same area, only the highest-ranking one shows.

The new Google Ads guidance says the same thing in plainer words: “Missed calls and slow response times can lower your ranking and visibility,” and aim for five or more reviews on your Business Profile, per Google’s pay-per-lead best practices. Review ratings now pull straight from your Business Profile into the ad, per Google’s pay-per-lead goals page.

  • Answer the phone. A missed call can cost you twice: a possible charge if they hold past 20 seconds, and a ranking hit. If you can’t staff the line, narrow the ad schedule.
  • Reply to messages in minutes. If you opted into message leads, they count toward responsiveness too. Our speed-to-lead guide covers how to set up alerts.
  • Ask every customer for a review. Same link, every time, sent by text after the job. The complete guide to Google reviews has scripts, and the fake reviews rule covers what not to do.
  • Keep verification current. The Google Verified badge carries over when you migrate. If you’re unsure which badge you have, see Google Guaranteed vs. Google Verified.

Before and after your migration date

Google emails the account administrator 14 days before migration, then a reminder seven days before, per Google’s transition guide. On the day, the old dashboard stops working and logins redirect to Google Ads, per PPC Land. Search Engine Journal notes historical reports don’t transfer, so anyone who needs year-over-year numbers has to export them first.

After it moves, check the daily budget, Target CPA, service types and locations, ad schedule, lead phone number, photos and callouts. Better Business Bureau callouts are no longer supported, and Google recommends at least six other callouts. Business name, address and hours sync from your Business Profile and can only be edited there; a significant name or address change can trigger a 24 to 48 hour review that pauses the campaign. Google lists migration support at +1-844-205-0011, weekdays 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. Eastern.

Frequently asked questions

Can you still dispute Local Services Ads leads?

Not in the old sense. Google ended manual disputes in July 2024 and replaced them with automatic credits. Its models review every lead, don’t charge for leads judged invalid at first contact, and credit charged leads later found to be low quality, usually within 30 days. You can still rate each lead, which feeds Google’s models and may earn a bonus credit, but there’s no case to file.

Why won’t Google credit leads outside my service area?

Google no longer offers credits for leads marked job type not serviced or geo not serviced. The fix is in your settings: turn off service types you don’t perform, target only the cities, counties or ZIP codes you serve, and keep your area within a realistic drive. Google’s guidance says not to set service areas more than two hours of driving from your business.

How long does it take to get an LSA credit?

Credits usually appear in your account balance within 30 days, and some poor-quality leads are credited within the first 24 hours. The original charge still appears on your invoice, so the credit shows as a separate balance adjustment. Check the charge status on each lead, which reads charged, not charged, in review or credited, to see where it stands.

Where do I rate Local Services Ads leads in Google Ads?

After your account migrates to Google Ads, go to Goals, then Conversions, then Leads. That page replaces the old LSA inbox, and each lead keeps a feedback form. In the old dashboard, open Leads, choose the lead and select Rate this lead. Rate every lead, good and bad, and add a specific comment when you choose other.

Does answering calls affect my Local Services Ads ranking?

Yes. Google ranks Local Services Ads on your bid, how likely your ad is to produce a lead and your profile quality. Responsiveness is part of that, and Google says missed calls and slow response times can lower your ranking. Your rating, number of reviews and average response time also count, so answer during your ad schedule and reply to messages quickly.
