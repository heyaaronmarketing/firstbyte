Saturday, 11:40 p.m. A woman is standing outside her townhouse off Market Street in The Woodlands, keys on the kitchen counter, phone at 14%. She searches “locksmith near me” and taps the first thing that says “24/7, $19 service call.” The man who answers says “locksmith service,” not a company name. Your shop is six minutes away with a tech on call. Your phone never rings.

That’s locksmith marketing in Houston: you’re competing against other locksmiths, and also against call centers pretending to be locksmiths.

What works here: get through Google’s Advanced Verification so you can run ads at all, keep a Google Business Profile that’s clean enough to survive suspicions, and put your Texas DPS license number everywhere the law says to (which is everywhere). After that, price the job honestly on the phone and answer every call at 2 a.m. like it’s 2 p.m. Treat commercial rekeys and access control as a separate business with its own marketing, because it pays better and doesn’t wake you up.

We haven’t worked with a locksmith company. We have run Google Ads and local SEO for home service businesses across Greater Houston, and that’s where the experience here comes from.

The fake-listing problem is your marketing problem

Locksmiths have been the poster child for fake Google listings for over a decade. In Google’s own 2017 research with university researchers, the company said fewer than 0.5% of local searches led to fake listings, and that locksmiths, plumbers, electricians and other contractors made up roughly 2 of every 5 fake listings. It also said abusive listings had fallen 70% from their June 2015 peak. Small share of searches, big share of locksmith searches.

The pattern hasn’t changed much. Search Engine Land summarized the New York Times reporting: listings with fake local addresses route calls to a remote call center, which quotes $35 to $90 by phone and dispatches a subcontractor who often demands three or four times that on site. Houston has its own versions. KPRC covered a Katy driver who found a “$19 locksmith” through Google and paid about $185.

Two things follow for a real shop.

First, Google is suspicious of you by default. In our experience with restricted home service categories, profiles get extra verification, edits get flagged, and suspensions hit legitimate businesses too. Keep your profile boringly accurate. Our guide to Google Business Profile video verification walks through what Google wants to see.

Second, trust is the product. Every piece of your marketing should answer the question the customer is silently asking: is this a real local company, and will the price on the phone be the price at the door?

Texas locksmith licensing, and what it means for your ads

Locksmiths in Texas are licensed by the Department of Public Safety under the Private Security Act (Occupations Code chapter 1702). The regulator changed shape in 2019: SB 616 abolished the Texas Private Security Board as of September 1, 2019, moved its functions to DPS, and turned the board into an advisory committee. If an old competitor’s website still says “licensed by the Texas Private Security Board,” that tells you how often they update it.

The pieces that matter for marketing:

  • Company license. Per DPS’s list of company license types, locksmith work falls under the Class B Security Contractor company license, which can cover locksmith, electronic access, alarm and other services. If you’re selling access control, confirm your license covers electronic access too.
  • Individual licenses. DPS’s individual license page lists Locksmith as an individual license, and individuals have to be affiliated with a licensed company. Each tech you send out should be licensed.
  • Using the word “locksmith” makes you a locksmith company. Under Texas Occupations Code 1702.1056, advertising services with the term “locksmith,” or having it in the company name, counts as acting as a locksmith company. DPS has told tow companies that an unlicensed outfit opening cars for owners can’t advertise that work with the word, which is one more reason to keep your license number visible.
  • License number in every ad. 37 TAC 35.9 requires licensee advertisements, including business cards, to show the company name and address as in DPS records (residential addresses excepted) and the company license number.
  • Consumer notice and trucks. 37 TAC 35.8 requires you to give clients your license number and DPS contact information for complaints, and any vehicle that shows the company name must show the license number in letters at least one inch high on each side.

Qualifying for the company license, per 37 TAC 35.123, takes either two consecutive years of full-time locksmith experience or a set of approved courses, an exam and one year of experience. That bar is real. Put it to work in your marketing: “Texas DPS licensed company, every tech individually licensed, license numbers on our trucks” is a sentence a call center can’t truthfully say. Link to the DPS license search so customers can check.

We’re marketers, not lawyers. Read the current rules or ask DPS before you print anything new.

Google Advanced Verification: the gate before any ad runs

You can’t just open a Google Ads account and start bidding on “locksmith near me.” Google’s local services ad policy restricts locksmith ads in the U.S., and Google’s Advanced Verification page says all locksmith businesses in the U.S. and Canada must pass Advanced Verification.

The process, per Google:

Google says verification typically takes around 6 to 8 weeks, and appeals take about the same. So plan your launch two months out, and don’t promise your partner a busy December if you’re starting the paperwork in November.

A few things we’d have ready before applying. Your DPS license paperwork with the business name exactly as it appears in Google Ads. A real storefront or office with signage, since the agent confirms your business exists and where it operates over video. Someone at the location at the scheduled time. And the customer ID of the account you’ll actually use, so you don’t verify one account and run ads from another. Agencies that “own” your ad account create problems here, so set it up under your own login and give your agency access.

Local Services Ads for locksmiths

Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes Locksmiths, so you can run pay-per-lead ads in the box at the very top of Google. Google also notes that pre-badge ads (the ones that start before checks finish) aren’t available for locksmiths. You wait for full screening.

For a locksmith, LSA has two real advantages. The Google Verified badge answers the trust question before the customer calls. And you pay per lead, not per click, so a curious tap on a $40 keyword doesn’t cost you anything. You can dispute leads that weren’t real customers, which matters in a category full of wrong-number calls. Our guide to LSA lead disputes covers how.

The catch is ranking. LSA leans hard on reviews, responsiveness and distance. If you miss calls after midnight, Google notices, and your ads show less. Read our Google Guaranteed vs. Google Verified explainer if you’re still seeing the old badge in competitors’ screenshots.

Google Ads for locksmiths: costs, structure and negatives

Locksmith clicks are expensive because emergency intent is valuable and the call centers bid too. For context, WordStream and LocaliQ’s 2026 benchmarks put the median cost per click for the Home and Home Improvement category at $8.33, with a median cost per lead of $90.92. Emergency lockout terms in large metros run well above that category median in accounts we’ve seen for urgent home services. Only say “background checked” if it’s true, and only list a street if your customers can actually visit it.

Emergency lockouts and commercial work are two businesses

Most locksmiths market like they only do lockouts, because that’s what the phone rings for. But the lockout customer is a one-time buyer in a panic. The property manager with 14 buildings in the Energy Corridor needs rekeys every time a tenant moves, master key systems, door hardware and access control. That customer calls for years.

Commercial marketing looks different:

  • A commercial page with real photos of installed hardware, panic bars and access control panels, plus the brands you service.
  • LinkedIn and direct outreach to property managers, facilities directors, HOA management companies and churches in Sugar Land, Katy and north Houston. Our property management marketing post shows how those buyers think.
  • Service agreements with response-time promises.
  • A dedicated form that asks the number of doors, locations and whether they want a site visit.

The emergency side still pays the bills and feeds reviews. Just don’t let it be the only thing your website talks about.

Price transparency beats bait-and-switch

The scam model is built on a low phone quote. BBB’s locksmith scam alert describes it plainly: a very low price to win the call, then extra charges once the tech arrives, often after claiming the lock has to be drilled. The BBB says most consumer complaints concern fees that weren’t disclosed on the call, and that it isn’t routine for a locksmith to insist on drilling. InvestigateTV reported in 2025 that one BBB region logged 48 locksmith complaints in 2024, with examples like a customer expecting about $150 and billed $1,217.29. And the FTC told consumers back in 2008 to research a locksmith before an emergency and save the number.

That gives a legitimate shop an obvious position: say the real price first.

  • Publish a price table on your website: residential lockout from [your price], car lockout from [your price], rekey per cylinder [your price], after-hours fee [your price]. Ranges are fine. A number beats “call for a quote.”
  • On the phone, quote the total range including the trip fee, and say what would change it.
  • Write “We quote before we roll, and the price at your door matches” on the site, the ads and the truck. Then make it true.
  • Explain when drilling is actually needed. Customers who’ve read the BBB warning will ask.
  • Send a text confirmation with the quote, the tech’s name and photo, and the license number before arrival.

Some callers will hang up when they hear an honest $150 after seeing a $19 ad. Let them go. A good share of them call back 40 minutes later when the $19 guy hasn’t shown up, and those are often your five-star reviews.

Reviews and the after-hours phone

Reviews are how a real locksmith separates from a call center in the map pack. Ask every customer by text right after the job, with the same link for everyone and nothing offered in exchange. Lockout customers are often grateful in a way few other service customers are, so the ask rarely feels awkward. Reply to every review, and to negative ones with specifics. Our Google reviews guide has templates.

Then the phone. Emergency calls come at night, and missed calls cost you the job, the review and some LSA ranking. Options, from cheapest to best:

  1. Owner’s cell on forward after hours. Free, and it burns people out by March.
  2. A rotating on-call tech with calls forwarded and a backup if they don’t pick up in four rings.
  3. A 24/7 answering service trained on your price ranges and dispatch rules, which books the job and texts the tech.

Whatever you choose, test it. Call your own number at 1 a.m. once a month from a phone they don’t recognize. If it goes to voicemail, your ads shouldn’t be running at 1 a.m. Pause the late-night schedule until coverage is real.

A first 90 days and a starting budget

For a Houston locksmith with a shop, two or three techs and little marketing today, this is the order we’d use. Start the Advanced Verification application on day one, since it takes the longest.

  1. Weeks 1-2: apply for Advanced Verification and LSA. Audit the Google Business Profile for accuracy. Put the DPS license number on the website, invoices, trucks and profiles.
  2. Weeks 3-6: build service pages (lockouts, car keys, rekey, commercial, access control) with prices. Set up call tracking and review texts. Sort out after-hours coverage.
  3. Weeks 7-10: once verified, launch emergency and car key campaigns in a tight radius with the full negative list. Turn on LSA.
  4. Weeks 11-13: add commercial campaigns and outreach to property managers. Review cost per booked job by campaign and cut what doesn’t pay.

Treat those as examples for a single-location shop, not quotes. The number that matters is cost per completed job by channel, and you only get it with tracked calls tied to invoices.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more locksmith customers in Houston?

Pass Google’s Advanced Verification so you can run ads, keep an accurate Google Business Profile, and collect reviews after every job. Show your Texas DPS license number on your website, trucks and ads, publish starting prices, and answer every call around the clock. Then build a commercial side with rekeys, master key systems and access control for property managers, which brings repeat work.

Do locksmiths need Advanced Verification for Google Ads?

Yes. Google requires all locksmith businesses in the United States and Canada to pass Advanced Verification before their ads can run. You submit business, owner and license details, then complete a video call from your business location so an agent can confirm the business is real. Google says it typically takes about 6 to 8 weeks, so start well before you need the calls.

Who licenses locksmiths in Texas?

The Texas Department of Public Safety licenses locksmith companies and individual locksmiths under Occupations Code chapter 1702. The Texas Private Security Board was abolished in 2019 and its functions moved to DPS, with an advisory committee in its place. Locksmith companies hold a Class B Security Contractor license, and each individual locksmith must be licensed and affiliated with a licensed company.

Does a Texas locksmith have to show a license number in ads?

Yes. DPS rules require licensee advertisements, including business cards, to show the company name and address as listed with the department and the company license number. Vehicles that display the company name must also show the license number in letters at least one inch high on each side. Licensees must also give clients their license number and DPS complaint contact information.

Are Local Services Ads worth it for a Houston and The Woodlands locksmith?

Often, yes. Locksmiths are an eligible Local Services Ads category, and the Google Verified badge helps answer the trust question in a category full of fake listings. You pay per lead rather than per click and can dispute bad leads. Pre-badge ads aren’t available for locksmiths, so you wait for full screening, and ranking depends on reviews and answering calls quickly, including at night.
