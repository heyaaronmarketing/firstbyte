Marketing Agency Contract Red Flags: What to Check Before You Sign

Agency contract red flags: auto-renewal, termination, who owns accounts and creative, ad spend markups, guarantees and non-solicits. Not legal advice.

Thursday afternoon, a dental office in Spring. The proposal looked great in the meeting: a clean deck, a friendly account lead, a promise to “own page one.” Now the practice manager has a 14-page PDF on her screen and a DocuSign deadline of Friday at 5. She skims it. Twelve months. Something about a “platform fee.” Section 9 says the agency keeps “all work product.” She’s not sure whether that’s normal.

It might be. Most agency contracts are written by people who have been burned by clients that didn’t pay, so they protect the agency. That isn’t sinister. But a few clauses decide whether you can leave cleanly in a year, and those are worth twenty minutes before you sign.

The short version: before you sign a marketing agency contract, check the term and renewal, how much notice you owe to cancel, who owns the ad accounts, data, creative and website, whether ad spend is billed straight to you or marked up, what the setup fee actually buys, what’s promised as a result, what reports you’re owed, and whether a non-solicit clause stops you from hiring someone later.

We’re a marketing agency in The Woodlands, and we sign these from the other side every month. This is how we’d read one if we were the client. It isn’t legal advice. For anything large or unusual, a Texas business attorney is worth the hour.

Read it in this order

Agency contracts usually have a short statement of work up front and a long block of standard terms at the back. The standard terms are where the trouble hides, because nobody reads them in the meeting.

We read in this order: term and renewal, termination, ownership, money (fees, markups, setup), promises, reporting, then the restrictive clauses (non-solicit, exclusivity, confidentiality). If the first three are clean, the rest is usually fine. If ownership is murky, nothing else in the contract really matters, because you’re renting your own marketing.

Before any of that, check that the statement of work names the actual deliverables. “Digital marketing services” is not a scope. “Management of Google Search campaigns, up to $8,000 monthly spend, two new ad variations per month, monthly report and call” is a scope. If the scope is vague, every disagreement later turns into “that wasn’t included.” Our questions to ask before hiring an agency cover the sales conversation; this post is about the paper.

Term length and auto-renewal

Minimum terms are normal. Paid search needs a few weeks to gather data, and SEO takes months before it shows anything (we wrote about that in how long SEO takes). An agency that commits people and setup time to your account wants some assurance you won’t walk after week three.

What we consider reasonable for a small or mid-size Houston business, from the contracts we see:

  • Paid ads: three months, then month-to-month.
  • SEO: six months, then month-to-month.
  • Website build: a project contract tied to milestones, with no ongoing term unless you choose hosting or maintenance.

The real flag is automatic renewal for another full term. A 12-month contract that renews for another 12 months unless you cancel in a 30-day window, 60 days before the anniversary, is built to be missed. Owners don’t put that date on a calendar, and the renewal letter (if there is one) gets lost in a shared inbox.

Ask for it to roll to month-to-month after the first term. Most agencies will agree, because a good agency doesn’t want a client who feels trapped. If they won’t, put the cancellation window on your calendar the day you sign, with a reminder two weeks before it opens.

Termination: notice, cause and what happens on the way out

Look for two kinds of exit. Termination for cause (they miss deliverables, you don’t pay) and termination for convenience (either side just wants out). A fair contract has both, with the same notice period for each side.

Thirty days’ notice is common and fine. Sixty is livable. Ninety days for a monthly retainer is a lot of money to spend on a relationship that’s already over.

Then read what happens after notice is given. This is the part almost no one checks, and it’s where most ugly exits come from. A good termination clause says the agency will:

  • Hand over admin access to every account it created for you, within a set number of days.
  • Export any data held in its own tools (call recordings, lead lists, reports) in a usable format.
  • Keep campaigns running, unchanged, through the notice period unless you ask otherwise.
  • Not pause, delete or restructure anything during the handover.

If the contract also has an early termination fee, check if it’s a reasonable fee or simply every remaining month due at once. A fee equal to one month is a negotiation point. “All remaining fees become due” on a 12-month term is a walk-away clause for most small businesses.

Ownership of accounts, data, creative and the website

This is the clause that matters most, and the one most often left vague. We have a whole post on who owns your Google Ads account when you switch agencies, asset by asset, so here we’ll stick to what the contract should say.

Ad accounts. The contract should say the accounts are yours and created in your name, with the agency given access. In Google Ads, ownership in practice means Admin access. Per Google’s access level page, Admins can add and remove users, change access levels and unlink manager accounts, while Standard users can edit campaigns but can’t do those things. You want your own Admin login, not a promise. Google’s third-party policy also requires agencies to use “a separate account for each end-advertiser,” so if an agency proposes running your ads inside its own shared account, that’s a policy problem as well as an ownership one.

Billing. Check whose card or invoice pays Google. If your account runs on monthly invoicing through the agency’s manager account, Google’s unlinking rules say the account “will stop serving” once it’s unlinked from that paying manager. That isn’t a reason to refuse agency billing, but the contract should commit the agency to help you move billing before any unlink.

Data. GA4, Tag Manager, Search Console, call tracking, CRM records. The contract should say data generated for your business belongs to you and will be exported on exit.

Creative and the website. This one surprises people. Paying for a logo or a website does not automatically make you the copyright owner. Under U.S. Copyright Office Circular 30, a commissioned work from an outside contractor is only a “work made for hire” if it falls into one of nine specific categories and both parties sign a written agreement saying so. A website or a logo usually doesn’t fit those categories neatly. The safer route is a plain assignment, and under 17 U.S.C. § 204(a), a transfer of copyright ownership isn’t valid without a written, signed document. So look for words like “assigns to Client all right, title and interest” in the deliverables, effective on payment.

Agencies fairly keep ownership of their own templates, internal tools and stock photo licenses (which are licensed, not owned, by anyone). That’s fine as long as you get a license to use what’s on your site forever. What you don’t want is a site built on an agency’s proprietary platform that can’t be moved, with “all work product” retained by the agency.

Ad spend handling and markups

There are two common ways ad spend gets paid. Your card goes on file in Google Ads and Meta and the agency bills you only its fee. Or the agency pays the platforms and invoices you for spend plus fee. Both are legitimate. The second can hide a markup.

A markup isn’t automatically wrong. Some agencies float the spend and charge for the cash flow and risk. The problem is when it isn’t disclosed. Google’s advertiser guide to working with third parties says that, at a minimum, you have the right to know “the number of clicks, impressions, and the total cost” of your Google ads, and it encourages advertisers to understand total cost including any fees the partner charges. If your invoice says “Google Ads: $6,000” and the Google Ads account says $5,100, you’re owed an explanation.

The contract should state which billing method applies, the exact management fee, and that any markup or “platform fee” on media is written as a percentage. Better still, ask for read access to billing in the ad accounts so you can match the invoice yourself. Our post on agency pricing models runs the math on percentage-of-spend fees if you’re comparing proposals.

Setup fees: what they should buy

Setup fees are normal for ad accounts, SEO and websites. The first month of an account is real work: tracking, structure, negative keywords, landing page fixes. The test is whether the setup produces something you keep.

A setup fee should be tied to a short written list, for example:

  • Conversion tracking for calls, forms and bookings, tested, with the right actions set as primary.
  • Call tracking numbers assigned by channel, in an account you own.
  • Campaigns built or rebuilt, with location settings and negative keyword lists documented.
  • A one-page account map you could hand to the next agency.

“Onboarding fee” with no list is an ask. A setup fee that’s refundable if the agency never launches is a nice sign. A setup fee that’s charged again if you pause and restart is a little greedy.

Performance guarantees, and why “guaranteed rankings” is a red flag

Everyone wants a guarantee. Some agencies sell one because it closes deals.

For SEO, Google is unusually blunt. Google’s own “Do you need an SEO?” guidance says “No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google,” and if an SEO guarantees their changes will put you in first place, “find someone else.” The same page warns about anyone who claims a “special relationship” with Google or advertises a “priority submit.” For paid ads, Google’s third-party guide says “it’s not possible to guarantee a specific ad position” on search results, because position is set by an auction on every search, and it lists guaranteed placement as a red flag.

So a guaranteed ranking clause tells you one of three things. The agency doesn’t understand how search works, or it plans to rank you for a phrase nobody searches (“best family dentist Spring Cypress Creek Parkway Saturday”), or the guarantee has so many exits it’s meaningless. Read the fine print of any guarantee and you’ll usually find that it’s a refund of the management fee after 12 months if you stayed and did everything they asked.

What a reasonable agency can commit to in writing: the work (a number of pages, ad tests, posts or reports per month), response times, and that tracking will be set up and verified so you can judge results yourself. Some will agree to a performance review point, like “if cost per lead isn’t under $X by month four, either side may exit with 15 days’ notice.” That’s a useful clause and a fair one.

Reporting obligations

A contract that says “monthly reporting” and nothing else lets the agency send a PDF of impressions forever. Spell out what’s in it.

We’d ask the contract to commit to:

  • Spend, clicks and impressions per platform (the minimum Google says you’re entitled to).
  • Conversions by type: calls over a set length, form fills, bookings. Not one blended “conversions” number.
  • Cost per lead, and cost per booked job if you share sales data back.
  • What changed this month and what’s planned next month.
  • A standing call, monthly at least, with the person who actually runs the account.

And read-only access to everything, all the time, so the report is never your only view. If you’re already in a contract and these reports feel thin, our companion post on signs your marketing agency isn’t working walks through a 30-minute check you can run yourself.

Non-solicit clauses, plus the Texas notes we could verify

Many agency contracts say you can’t hire the agency’s employees during the contract and for a period after, often 12 months. Agencies include this because training an account manager is expensive and clients do sometimes poach them. It’s a reasonable clause in moderate form.

Watch for three things. The length (12 months after the end is common; 24 or 36 is a lot). Whether it covers contractors and freelancers the agency used, who may have worked for you directly before. And whether the penalty is a fixed “placement fee” such as a year of the person’s salary. A placement fee is often fairer than a flat ban, because it gives you a way to hire someone who wants to come work for you.

In Texas, restrictive covenants are governed by the Covenants Not to Compete Act in the Business and Commerce Code, section 15.50. In Marsh USA v. Cook (2011), the Texas Supreme Court said covenants restricting solicitation of a former employer’s customers and employees “are restraints on trade and are governed by the Act,” and the statute requires reasonable limits on time, geographic area and scope of activity. The court also noted a trial court can reform an overbroad covenant. That case involved an employee rather than an agency client, so how it applies to your contract is a question for a lawyer, but it tells you Texas courts look at reasonableness.

A few other clauses worth a glance: exclusivity (you can’t use another agency for anything, even an unrelated billboard buy), venue (disputes heard in another state, which makes small claims impractical), and auto-approval of creative if you don’t respond in 48 hours.

Frequently asked questions

What should I look for in a marketing agency contract?

Check the minimum term and whether it auto-renews, the notice needed to cancel, and who owns the ad accounts, data, creative and website. Confirm whether ad spend is billed to your card or marked up, what the setup fee buys, what reports you are owed, and whether a non-solicit clause limits hiring. Ownership and termination terms matter most because they decide how cleanly you can leave.

Is a 12-month marketing agency contract normal?

It happens, but for most small businesses we think it is longer than needed. Three months for paid ads and six for SEO, then month-to-month, covers the agency's setup time without locking you in. If you do sign 12 months, ask for a performance exit and make sure it rolls to month-to-month rather than renewing automatically for another full year.

Can an SEO agency guarantee first-page rankings?

No. Google's own guidance says no one can guarantee a number one ranking on Google, and tells site owners to find someone else if an SEO guarantees first place. Google also says nobody can guarantee a specific paid ad position because ads are ranked in an auction on every search. A reasonable agency commits to the work it will do and to tracking you can check, not to a ranking.

Do I own the website my agency built?

Not automatically. Under U.S. copyright law, commissioned work is only a work made for hire in nine specific categories with a signed written agreement, and a website usually does not fit. The safer approach is a written clause assigning the website, design and content to you on payment. Agencies can reasonably keep their own templates and tools as long as you have a permanent license.

Is a non-solicitation clause in an agency contract enforceable in Texas?

It may be, depending on the details. Texas treats non-solicitation covenants as restraints governed by its Covenants Not to Compete Act, which requires reasonable limits on time, area and scope, and courts can reform overbroad terms. How that applies to a client-agency contract is a question for a Texas business attorney. Practically, ask for a 12-month limit or a placement fee.
