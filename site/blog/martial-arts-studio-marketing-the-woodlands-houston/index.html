Tuesday, 5:10 p.m. The after-school van is back from two elementary schools in Spring, and fourteen kids in white uniforms are lining up for the Little Dragons class. The lobby is full of parents on their phones. The front desk line rings twice and nobody picks up, because the one person at the desk is tying a six-year-old’s belt. Somewhere in a CRM there’s a Facebook lead from yesterday afternoon that nobody has called yet.

Most martial arts studios in Houston don’t have a traffic problem. They have a desk problem, an offer problem and a retention problem, roughly in that order.

The short version: to get more students at a martial arts school in The Woodlands or Houston, sell the kids program to parents, because they’re the ones paying. Use a simple trial offer that books into real class times online. Run Meta ads aimed at adults (Meta won’t let you target the kids anyway) and win the Google Maps results within a 10 to 15 minute drive. Push hardest in August and January. Then keep students through belt tests and events, since a student who stays two years is worth far more than any lead you’ll buy.

This guide is written for independent karate, taekwondo, jiu-jitsu and mixed-style schools with one or two locations.

The kids program is the business, and parents are the buyer

In most suburban schools we look at, kids and teens make up the majority of active memberships. Adult classes matter for culture and for the instructors’ sanity, but the rent in Shenandoah or Katy gets paid by the 5-to-12-year-olds.

That changes who you’re marketing to. A parent isn’t buying “martial arts.” They’re buying one of a few outcomes:

  • Focus and confidence. The kid who won’t make eye contact, or who melts down at homework.
  • Safety and anti-bullying. A real worry, and one that spikes in the first month of school.
  • Activity that isn’t a team sport. Plenty of kids don’t love soccer or baseball and their parents want something structured anyway.
  • Logistics. Classes at 4:30 or 5:15, close to home, with pickup from school if you offer it.

Cost sits under all of it. the Aspen Institute’s Project Play reported in its State of Play 2025 that youth sports costs have risen 46% since 2019. Parents comparing your monthly tuition to rec league soccer will notice. So your marketing has to show what the monthly fee buys: structure, small classes, a clear belt path, and instructors who know their kid’s name.

Write every page and ad for the parent, who reads it at 9 p.m. on a phone after the kids are in bed.

Back-to-school and New Year: the two enrollment peaks

Martial arts enrollment in Houston follows the school year. In the accounts and studios we’ve seen, two windows produce a big share of the year’s new students, and a couple of smaller bumps fill in around them.

Back-to-school. Conroe ISD’s approved 2026-27 calendar has students starting Wednesday, August 12, 2026, and Cy-Fair ISD’s calendar uses the same start date, with a new week-long fall break October 12 to 16. Parents start planning fall activities in late July, so that’s when the campaigns should start. The real decisions happen in the two weeks after school starts, once the family’s schedule settles and parents see whether their child has a free afternoon.

New Year. January is the second peak. Parents set goals for their kids too, and adults sign themselves up. Start ads the week after Christmas, not January 15.

Smaller bumps. Spring break (March 15 to 19 in both Conroe and Cy-Fair this school year) is a good time for a camp or a “bring a friend” week. Summer camp sells in March and April.

Plan your budget around this calendar instead of spending the same amount every month. We go deeper on the school calendar in our back-to-school marketing guide.

Trial class offers that turn into enrollments

Every school runs some version of a trial. The difference between a trial that fills classes and one that fills the mat with no-shows is mostly in the details.

The common options:

  • One free trial class. Easy to sell, high no-show rate, and you’re selling the membership at the end of a single 30-minute class.
  • Paid intro program. Something like two weeks of classes plus a uniform for a set fee. Fewer leads, more show-ups, and the family has already paid you once. In the studios we’ve talked to, paid intros usually convert to memberships at a higher rate than free classes.

Whatever you choose, the trial needs three things to work:

  1. A real booking page. Show the next available class times by age group and let the parent pick one. “Fill out this form and we’ll reach out” loses half the people who were ready that night.
  2. A reminder sequence. Text the day before and two hours before, with the address, where to park, what to wear and how early to arrive.
  3. A planned conversation after class. The instructor gives the parent two minutes of specific feedback about their child (“she listened well, we’ll work on her stance”), then the desk walks through the options. Don’t let the parent leave with “we’ll think about it” and no next step booked.

And answer the phone, or call back within minutes. A parent who filled out a form at 8:45 p.m. is often calling the next studio at 9 the next morning. Our post on speed to lead covers how to set that up without hiring another person.

Meta ads for martial arts: target the parent, never the kid

Facebook and Instagram are usually the biggest lead source for a kids program, and the rules shape everything.

Start with minors. According to Meta’s announcement, since February 2023 advertisers can only use age and location to reach teens, gender is gone as a teen targeting option, and teens’ activity on Meta’s apps no longer shapes the ads they see. Interest targeting for teens was removed even earlier. Your teen program can still advertise, but the ad has to reach the parent or the teen through broad age and location only.

For parents, the targeting toolbox keeps shrinking. Meta has removed or merged detailed targeting options several times since 2022, including a round of consolidation announced in June 2025, and it removed detailed targeting exclusions from Ads Manager in 2025, per Linear Design’s running list of Meta targeting changes. Parent-related demographic options still show up in many accounts. Check what’s actually there before you build a plan around it.

In practice we’d run it like this:

  • Adults 25 to 54, a radius of roughly 5 to 10 miles around the studio (tighter inside the Loop, wider out toward Magnolia or Montgomery), and either broad targeting or a light parent layer if your account still has it.
  • Let the creative do the sorting. “Kids karate in Kingwood, ages 5 to 12, classes at 4:30 and 5:15” finds parents because everyone else scrolls past.
  • Use real video from your mat. A 20-second clip of a class with the instructor explaining what kids learn in week one beats any stock footage.
  • Retarget website visitors and video viewers with the trial offer and a few short testimonials from parents.

Sample ad copy:

“Looking for an after-school activity that builds focus? Our Little Ninjas program in The Woodlands is for ages 4 to 6, 30 minutes, twice a week. Two-week intro with a free uniform. Pick a class time online.”

One rule we hold firm on: never post a child’s face in an ad or on social media without written permission from a parent. Keep a photo release on file for every student, and keep a “no photos” list at the desk so instructors know whom to keep out of frame.

Google Business Profile and search ads

Parents who search are further along than parents who scroll past an ad. “Karate for kids near me,” “taekwondo The Woodlands,” “kids jiu jitsu Cypress” and “martial arts classes Katy” are people who have already decided on the activity and are now choosing a school.

Business Profile. Use Martial arts school as the primary category unless you really are a single-style school, then add the style-specific categories Google offers that match what you teach. Add each program under Services with ages and class length. Upload photos of the mat, the lobby, the parking lot and the front door, and video of a real class. Put your class schedule in the business description and keep holiday hours current. Our map pack guide covers the ranking side in full.

Reviews from parents, inside Google’s rules

Parents trust other parents, and a studio with 180 detailed reviews wins the map pack and the phone call.

The rules, per Google’s prohibited content policy: no incentives for reviews (no free week, no gear discount), no discouraging negative reviews, and no review gating where only happy families get the link. In April 2026 Google added language against having staff collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content, which can include asking families to name a particular instructor, per PPC Land. Drop any instructor review contests.

The best moments to ask are built into your calendar:

  • Right after a belt test, when the family just took photos with the new belt.
  • At the end of the first month, when the parent has seen a change at home.
  • After a tournament, camp week or birthday party at the studio.

A text script that works: “Congrats to Mason on his yellow belt today! If you have a minute, a Google review helps other parents in Spring find us: [link]. Thank you for being part of the family.” Send that to every family that tested, not just the ones you think loved it.

After-school pickup programs: market them carefully

A pickup program can be the most profitable thing a studio does. A van loop through three or four elementary schools, two hours of classes and supervised time, and a monthly fee well above regular tuition. It also fills the 3 to 5 p.m. slot that’s otherwise dead.

It also carries a licensing question that affects your marketing copy. Texas exempts some programs from child care licensing. Under Texas Human Resources Code § 42.041(b)(18), a program that gives children direct instruction in a single skill can be exempt if it meets every condition: it offers no services unrelated to that skill, it doesn’t advertise or present itself as a child-care facility, day-care center or licensed before- or after-school program, it tells parents it isn’t licensed by the state and describes the physical risks of the activity, and it runs background checks on all employees and volunteers who work with children.

The marketing implications are direct. Words like “after-school care,” “child care” and “daycare” on your website, flyers or ads can work against the exemption. Adding homework time, snacks and general supervision may push you outside it. Texas HHS Child Care Regulation’s handbook notes that a program that doesn’t meet every requirement of the single-skill exemption may meet the definition of a school-age program, which is regulated under Chapter 744, and it lists separate exemption forms for school-year and summer single-skill programs.

We’re marketers, not lawyers. What we’d do: decide what your program actually is with your attorney or a call to Texas HHS Child Care Regulation, then write every page and ad to match. “After-school martial arts program with pickup from these schools” is a different claim from “after-school care.”

Summer camps have their own question. After the 2025 Hill Country floods, Texas passed new youth camp safety laws, and day camps are covered too, per KPRC, which reported only 18 camps fully licensed weeks before summer 2026. Check with the Department of State Health Services before you advertise a summer camp.

Retention: belt tests, events and the second year

Lead generation gets the attention, but retention decides whether a school grows. A kids program that loses students in month four is filling a leaky bucket.

What keeps families, in the schools we’ve seen do it well:

  • Visible progress. A clear belt path with stripes in between, so a six-year-old earns something every few weeks. Post the curriculum and testing schedule for parents.
  • Belt testing as an event. Invite grandparents, take photos at a branded backdrop, and send the family a photo and a short video that evening. This is your best social content and your best review moment.
  • Buddy weeks. Students bring a friend to class for a week, twice a year. Referrals from current families convert better than any ad.
  • Birthday parties and parents’ night out. Both bring non-students onto the mat.
  • Attendance alerts. When a student misses two weeks in a row, the instructor calls. Most cancellations start as quiet absences.

Texting is how most of this reaches parents, so get consent right. Class reminders are different from promotional texts. Get clear opt-in for marketing messages and keep the record. Texas brought marketing texts under its telephone solicitation law through SB 140, effective September 1, 2025, with some exemptions for current customers and businesses that sell from a physical location, per Morgan Lewis’s summary of SB 140. Cost per enrolled student: the number that matters

Cost per lead is the number ad platforms show you. Cost per enrolled student is the number that runs your business. Track every step:

Below is an illustrative month for a single-location school spending on Meta and Google. The numbers are an example, not a benchmark.

StepCountCost per step
Ad spend$2,500
Leads (forms, calls, messages)100$25 per lead
Trials booked45$56 per booking
Trials attended30$83 per show
Enrolled15$167 per student

Now the other side. If tuition is $159 a month and the average kid stays 14 months, each student is worth about $2,226 in tuition before testing fees and gear. Against that, $167 to enroll a student is a good trade. Even $300 can be.

Two consequences. First, the biggest gains usually come from the middle of the funnel: booking more of your leads and getting more of your bookings to show up. A school that moves show rate from 50% to 70% gets 40% more students from the same ad spend. Second, retention changes what you can afford. Add three months to average student tenure and you can outbid every studio in the area for the same parents.

Count enrollments by source in your studio software and tie them back to ad platforms where you can. Our post on tracking forms and calls explains the setup.

Mistakes we see at martial arts schools

  • Pricing hidden behind a phone call. You don’t have to publish everything, but parents want a ballpark. “Programs start at $X a month” keeps more of them.
  • Ads with no schedule. If a parent can’t tell whether there’s a class at a time that works, they move on.
  • One page for every program. A four-year-old’s parent and a 35-year-old wanting jiu-jitsu need different pages.
  • Nobody owns the follow-up. If leads go into an inbox that three people check “when they can,” the ads aren’t the problem.

A first 90 days for a studio starting fresh

  1. Days 1 to 30: fix the Business Profile, build a page per program with ages, times and the trial offer, add online trial booking, set up text reminders and a photo release process, and start asking for reviews after every belt test.
  2. Days 31 to 60: launch Meta ads to parents with real class video and a Google Search campaign on kids and style keywords. Track leads, bookings, shows and enrollments by source.
  3. Days 61 to 90: fix whichever funnel step is weakest, run a buddy week, and plan budget for the next peak, whether that’s August or January.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more students for my martial arts school?

Market the kids program to parents, since they make the decision. Offer a simple trial that books into real class times online, call leads back within minutes, and run Facebook and Instagram ads to adults within a few miles of the studio. Win Google Maps for searches like kids karate near me, ask every belt-test family for a review, and push hardest in August and January.

Can I target kids or teens with Facebook ads for my martial arts studio?

Only in a very limited way. Since February 2023 Meta lets advertisers reach teens under 18 only by age and location, with no gender or interest targeting. Most studios get better results aiming ads at parents, usually adults 25 to 54 near the studio, with creative that names the age group, class times and neighborhood. Never use a child's photo without written parent permission.

Is a free trial class or a paid intro offer better for a martial arts school?

It depends on your follow-up, but paid intro programs usually produce fewer leads and better show rates, because the family has already committed a small amount. A free class is easier to sell and fills the calendar but brings more no-shows. Whichever you choose, use online booking, text reminders, and a planned conversation with the parent right after the class.

Does a martial arts after-school pickup program need a child care license in Texas?

It can. Texas exempts single-skill programs only if they meet every condition, including offering no unrelated services, not advertising as child care or an after-school program, notifying parents the program is unlicensed, and running background checks. Adding homework time or general supervision may make it a regulated school-age program. Confirm with Texas HHS Child Care Regulation or an attorney, then write your marketing to match.

What is a good cost per enrolled student for a martial arts school?

There is no universal benchmark, so work it out from your own numbers. Multiply monthly tuition by how many months the average student stays to get student value, then decide what share you can spend to acquire one. A studio charging $159 a month with 14-month average tenure can comfortably spend a few hundred dollars per enrollment. Improving show rates and retention lowers the cost fastest.
