Most med spa ad problems in Texas start with one word: Botox.

It’s the treatment patients search for, the word owners want on every ad, and a prescription drug brand that Google won’t let most advertisers bid on. Get that wrong and half your search campaign sits in “limited” while a competitor down Woodlands Parkway books the consults.

The short answer: med spa marketing in The Woodlands and Houston works when it’s built on the treatment, not the spa. One page per treatment with provider credentials and price ranges, a Business Profile full of treatment-specific reviews, search ads that respect Google’s drug-term rules, Meta ads aimed at adults 18 and up, local creators, and memberships that bring patients back.

This guide puts the Texas rules next to each channel, because in this category the two can’t be separated.

Who regulates a med spa in Texas, and why your marketing should care

Texas has no single “med spa license.” Different services fall under different agencies, and your marketing has to match what each person on staff is allowed to do.

ServiceWho regulates itWhat it means for marketing
Facials, waxing, lash extensions, light peelsTDLR (esthetician license and establishment license)Fine to market by esthetician name. Estheticians can’t inject, use needles for fillers, or use lasers for hair removal or resurfacing
Neuromodulators, fillers, medium and deep peelsTexas Medical Board (medical acts, physician order and delegation)Name the supervising physician and the injector’s credentials on the treatment page
Laser hair removalTDLR certificate program; DSHS for device radiation safetyNo claims that it’s free from risk or has a medical benefit
Elective IV therapyHB 3749 (Jenifer’s Law), since Sept. 1, 2025Administered only by a physician, PA, APRN or RN under physician supervision. Say who places the IV
Tanning, permanent makeupDSHSSeparate rules; check before advertising

The first two rows come from TDLR’s medical spa page, which says plainly that TDLR “does not have jurisdiction over all the services provided at a medspa,” and that Botox and fillers must be provided under the order and delegation of a licensed physician. For laser hair removal, TDLR’s laser hair removal FAQ requires a separate facility application for each location, a consulting physician contract and an individual certificate for anyone performing the procedure. Section 401.518 of the Health and Safety Code bars a facility from advertising that laser hair removal is free from risk or provides any medical benefit, and from false or misleading advertising generally. For IV therapy, see Polsinelli’s summary of HB 3749.

Why care, beyond staying out of trouble? Patients are checking. In September 2025, ABC13 reported that a Montgomery County woman who ran a med spa in Magnolia was arrested on felony charges of practicing medicine without a license after giving Botox and filler at in-home parties. Stories like that make credentials a selling point. A treatment page that names the medical director, the injector and their license type converts better than one with a stock photo and the word “experts.”

What can a med spa say in Google and Meta ads?

Google. In the U.S., Google’s restricted drug terms policy lets advertisers use prescription drug terms in ad text and on landing pages without certification, but keyword-targeting those terms requires certification. Google’s prescription drug services policy is aimed at online prescribing, dispensing and pharmacies, which most med spas aren’t. In practice, that means you can mention a brand name in copy and on the page, while keywords built on the brand name tend to get limited or disapproved.

So we build search campaigns around the treatment and the outcome instead:

  • “wrinkle relaxer The Woodlands,” “forehead lines treatment Spring TX,” “lip filler near me,” “laser hair removal The Woodlands,” “microneedling Conroe,” “med spa near Market Street”
  • Negatives from day one: training, course, certification, jobs, salary, school, DIY, at home, kit, wholesale, “how to become,” free

Meta. Meta’s cosmetic procedures policy requires ads for cosmetic procedures to target people 18 and older. Meta’s personal health and appearance policy also bars ad content that implies or tries to generate negative self-perception, and its examples flag before-and-after images in weight-loss ads. In aesthetics accounts, we treat before-and-afters as something to test carefully rather than lead with, and keep copy away from “fix your…” framing.

If you offer GLP-1 weight loss, the rules tighten further on both platforms; our weight loss clinic guide covers that separately. Same for IV drips in our IV therapy marketing post.

What treatments drive med spa revenue?

Injectables. Neuromodulators made up 50% of minimally invasive cosmetic procedures in the American Society of Plastic Surgeons’ 2025 data, and hyaluronic acid fillers another 30%, per AmSpa’s summary of the ASPS 2025 statistics. Laser skin treatments, IPL and laser hair removal were among the other most-performed procedures.

That shapes where marketing money goes. Neuromodulator patients come back every few months. A first injectables visit is the start of a relationship, so the real cost per patient you can afford is much higher than the price of one appointment. Laser hair removal and facial packages are good entry points for people nervous about needles, and a natural path to injectables later.

Treatment pages that book consultations

Patients rarely search for “med spa” alone. They search for the treatment, the problem, or the treatment plus a place. Each core treatment needs its own page, and each page should answer what a nervous first-timer actually wants to know.

Price is the one owners resist. Patients compare. A “from” price or a per-unit range on the page filters out people who’d never book and builds trust with the ones who will. If you won’t publish a price, at least publish what the consultation costs and if it’s credited toward treatment.

Connect each page to the matching service on your Google Business Profile and link the booking button straight into your scheduler, preferably without a phone call. Our landing pages guide and web and SEO service cover the build.

How do med spas get more clients in The Woodlands and Houston?

Four channels do most of the work. In rough order of what we’d build first:

1. The Business Profile and reviews. “Med spa near me” and “Botox near me” searches pull from the map. Add every service with a description, upload real photos of the rooms and providers monthly, and keep the booking link current. Ask every patient for a review and nudge them to mention the treatment and provider; reviews that say “lip filler with Sarah” help the profile show for “lip filler.” Never trade discounts for reviews. Our Google reviews guide and FTC fake reviews rule post cover the lines.

2. Search ads for treatment terms. As above, built around treatments and outcomes, with tight location targeting.

3. Local creators. Aesthetics is a trust purchase, and a creator who lives in The Woodlands showing her own appointment does more than any brand ad. Disclose every gifted treatment, get permission to run the content as ads, and send creator traffic to a page with a code so you can count it.

4. Memberships and rebooking. AmSpa’s 2024 State of the Industry recap put the average spend per visit at $527 and the average share of repeat patients at 73%. The money is in the second through tenth visit. A membership, a rebook prompt at checkout, and a text when they’re due do more for revenue than another ad campaign.

Geography matters more than owners expect. Patients will drive a bit farther for a provider they trust, but a Market Street patient isn’t driving to the Galleria for a touch-up.

Why med spa leads go cold, and the reply that saves them

Here’s a scene we’d bet happens at most med spas around Houston every week. A woman in Spring fills out a lip filler form at 9:40 p.m. on a Tuesday, after the kids are in bed. The front desk sees it at 10 the next morning, calls, gets voicemail, and leaves a message. She already booked with the place that texted her back at 9:43.

Aesthetics leads are impulse-adjacent. The interest is real, but it cools fast, and the person is usually comparing two or three spas at once. Speed matters more than the perfect script. Our speed-to-lead guide has the broader research; for med spas, we’d set it up like this:

  • An automatic text within a minute of any form, from a real local number, with the booking link and a question that invites a reply.
  • A human follow-up during business hours the same day, by text first. Many patients would rather not take a call at work.
  • Online booking with a deposit for first visits, credited toward treatment. Deposits cut no-shows without scaring off serious patients.
  • A reminder the day before, with parking directions and what to expect.

A first reply that works:

“Hi Jess, this is Maria at [your spa] on Market Street. Thanks for asking about lip filler! First visits start with a quick consult with our nurse injector. Want to grab a time this week? Here’s the link, or just text back a day that works.”

Only text people who gave you their number for that purpose, and keep marketing texts to people who opted in. Texas updated its telephone solicitation law in 2025, and our Texas SMS rules post explains what changed.

One more Houston-specific note on timing. Laser hair removal and resurfacing patients are usually told to stay out of the sun around treatment, which is a tough ask in a Texas summer. Many spas push laser packages in the fall and winter and lean on injectables, facials and memberships in the hottest months. Plan your ad calendar around that instead of running the same offer all year.

Frequently asked questions

Can a med spa advertise Botox on Google Ads in Texas?

You can mention prescription drug brand names in ad text and on landing pages in the U.S., but Google requires certification to keyword-target prescription drug terms, and most med spas don’t fit its certification categories. That’s why brand-name keywords often get limited. Build campaigns around treatment and outcome terms like wrinkle relaxer, forehead lines or lip filler plus your city, and name the brand on the page.

Do med spas need a license in Texas?

Texas doesn’t issue a single med spa license. TDLR licenses estheticians and the establishment for cosmetology services, and runs the laser hair removal certificate program with a separate application for each facility. Injectables and medium or deep peels are medical acts under the Texas Medical Board, done under a physician’s order and delegation. Elective IV therapy has had its own rules since September 1, 2025.

Can med spas post before and after photos on Instagram ads?

It’s risky. Meta requires cosmetic procedure ads to target people 18 and older and bars ad content that implies or generates negative self-perception, with before-and-after images flagged as non-compliant in its weight-loss examples. Organic posts with patient consent are a different matter. For ads, lead with the provider, the experience and the treatment process, and test before-and-afters carefully if you use them at all.

How do I get more med spa clients in The Woodlands?

Build one page per treatment with provider credentials and price ranges, fill your Google Business Profile with every service and treatment-specific reviews, run search ads on treatment terms around your location, and use local creators who live in The Woodlands. Then push rebooking and memberships, since AmSpa reports most med spa patients are repeat patients and the revenue is in return visits.

How much do med spas spend on marketing per month in Houston?

There’s no reliable Houston benchmark, so work backward from patient value. AmSpa reports an average of $527 per visit and 73% repeat patients, which makes a retained injectables patient worth well over $1,000 a year. Use your show rate and consult-to-treatment rate to find what you can pay per booked consult. Many single-location med spas end up spending in the low thousands a month on ads.
