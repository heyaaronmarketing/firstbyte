Thursday, 6:40 p.m., in Cypress. A loan officer has bought 14 internet leads since lunch. She’s called each one twice. Two picked up, one already locked with someone else, one wanted to know the rate on a $90,000 cash-out on a house with a 580 score.

Meanwhile, the buyer a Bridgeland agent sent over on Saturday has already uploaded pay stubs and asked when he can get a pre-approval letter.

Anyone who has originated in Houston for more than a year knows which of those leads closes. Mortgage loan officer marketing works when it builds more of the second kind (warm referrals from agents, builders, past clients and people who trust you before they call) and treats the first kind as a numbers game with tight rules. It also has more compliance tripwires than almost any local business we write about. Your ads touch RESPA, Texas licensing rules, Regulation Z and special ad restrictions on both Google and Meta.

This guide is for individual loan officers, small brokerages and branch managers in Greater Houston. It isn’t legal advice; your compliance department or attorney has the final word on anything here.

Rate-shopper leads vs. referral leads

The two lead types behave so differently that they need separate budgets, separate scripts and separate success metrics.

Rate-shopper leads (purchased internet leads, rate-table placements, broad “mortgage rates” search ads) arrive in volume and close at low rates. They reward speed above all else: the first loan officer to have a real conversation usually wins. If you buy them, plan on calling within five minutes, six or more touches across two weeks, and a contact rate that will disappoint you. Our speed-to-lead guide has the follow-up cadence we’d use.

Referral leads come from agents, builders, financial planners, CPAs and past clients. Fewer of them, and they close at several times the rate, because someone the borrower trusts already vouched for you. Most of your marketing budget should make referral partners look good and make past clients remember you. Paid ads can fill gaps, but they shouldn’t be the whole business.

Realtor and builder partnerships under RESPA Section 8

Every loan officer wants more agent referrals, and RESPA Section 8 is the rule that decides how you can go after them. Under 12 CFR 1024.14, no person shall give and no person shall accept any fee, kickback or other thing of value under an agreement to refer settlement service business. Paying for goods or services actually furnished is allowed, but anything above market value is treated as not being for services actually performed. So are “normal promotional and educational activities” that aren’t conditioned on the referral of business.

The CFPB’s 2020 Section 8 FAQs added some practical color, per Consumer Finance Monitor’s summary of the CFPB’s 2020 Section 8 FAQs. A gift to a consumer for doing business with you isn’t prohibited, but an incentive for referring other business is. Gifts to referral sources in exchange for referrals are prohibited, and there’s no exception based on how small the gift is. Marketing services agreements aren’t automatically illegal, but they aren’t presumed legal either.

How that plays out in Houston co-marketing:

  • Joint ads and flyers. If you and an agent split a Facebook ad or an open-house flyer, pay your share of the actual cost based on your share of the space, and keep the invoice. Paying for the whole thing while the agent gets the exposure is the classic problem.
  • Open houses. Showing up to answer financing questions is fine. Paying for the agent’s signs, food or listing photos is the agent’s expense you’d be defraying.
  • Lunch-and-learns and CE classes. Education open to agents generally, not conditioned on referrals, is the textbook promotional activity. Keep the agenda, sign-in sheet and costs.
  • Builders. Preferred lender arrangements in master-planned communities like Bridgeland, Towne Lake or the Conroe MPCs raise their own Section 8 and affiliated business questions. Get counsel involved before you sign anything that ties marketing spend to a builder’s buyer traffic.

What reliably works and stays clean is boring: be the lender who answers on a Sunday, closes on time and keeps the agent updated without being asked. Then give agents tools they can use (a fast pre-approval turnaround, a one-page program summary in English and Spanish, a quick text when the appraisal comes in). Texas license and NMLS disclosures in every ad

Texas puts specific identifying requirements on mortgage advertising. For residential mortgage loan companies under the Texas Department of Savings and Mortgage Lending (SML), 7 TAC §56.203 requires an ad to include the company’s name and NMLS ID, its website address if it has one, and the sponsored originator’s name and NMLS ID. Mortgage bankers have a parallel rule at 7 TAC §57.203. Ads made directly by the company to the public don’t need the individual originator’s details.

Social media gets a practical exception. You can meet the requirement by prominently displaying the required information on your home page, profile page or similar, and it has to be easy to find without scrolling through old posts. Per the SML’s social media guidance, social accounts should show the company name and NMLS ID, your name and NMLS ID, and the corporate website.

Team names trip up a lot of Houston loan officers. “The Bayou City Home Loans Team” is allowed for advertising only, and the legal company name and NMLS ID have to appear with it in substantially equivalent prominence. You can’t bury the company name in light gray 6-point type under a big logo, and team names can’t appear on applicant documents.

If you originate second liens, home equity or manufactured housing loans under a company licensed by the Office of Consumer Credit Commissioner instead, your license comes from the OCCC, per the OCCC’s originator page, and you should confirm your advertising requirements with that agency and your compliance team.

Our practical checklist: NMLS IDs in every social profile bio and every paid ad, the company name at equal weight next to any team logo, and the NMLS Consumer Access link in your website footer.

Reg Z trigger terms: the words that force more disclosure

Regulation Z’s advertising rule, 12 CFR 1026.24, is why so many mortgage ads read like legal documents. State certain terms and you have to state a lot more. Per Consumer Compliance Outlook, the triggering terms for closed-end credit are the down payment (only if one is actually required), the number of payments or repayment period, the amount of any payment, and the amount of any finance charge. When an ad uses one, it must also disclose the down payment, the repayment terms and the APR, plus whether the APR may increase.

Also, if you state a rate, the APR has to appear too, and the interest rate can’t be more conspicuous than the APR. On a 125-character Facebook headline, that’s very hard to do well. Our approach is to keep paid ads trigger-free and move the numbers to a landing page that can carry every required disclosure.

Trigger-free headlines that still work:

  • “Buying your first home in Katy? See which Texas down payment programs you may qualify for.”
  • “Pre-approved in 24 hours. Local loan officer, answers on weekends.”
  • “VA loans explained by a Houston veteran. Book a 15-minute call.”

Facebook and Instagram ads under the Financial Products and Services category

Meta replaced its old Credit special ad category with a broader Financial Products and Services category, and U.S. ads for loans and other financial products must declare it or get rejected, per Jon Loomer. The restrictions, as his special ad categories guide describes them: age fixed at 18 to 65+, all genders, some detailed targeting unavailable and no exclusions, location targeting that must include a 15-mile radius around any point, no ZIP code targeting and no lookalike audiences.

So on Meta, your creative does the targeting. A stock photo of a smiling couple holding keys reaches everyone and converts no one. A 30-second phone video of you standing in front of a new build in Elyson explaining one specific program finds the right people because everyone else scrolls past.

What we’d run:

  • Short educational videos, one topic each: the Houston HAP, TSAHC programs, VA loans, buying with student debt, what pre-approval actually means.
  • Instant forms with two or three qualifying questions (timeline, whether they’ve talked to a lender, whether they’re working with an agent). Harder forms mean fewer leads and better ones; our guide to fixing bad Facebook leads has the details.
  • Retargeting of website visitors and video viewers with a calendar link.
  • Your NMLS ID in the page profile and, where your compliance team wants it, in the ad text.

Google Ads for mortgages: targeting limits and verification

Google applies similar limits. Under Google’s housing, employment and credit policy FAQ, credit ads in the U.S. can’t target or exclude by age, gender, parental status or marital status, and ZIP code targeting is off the table, so you’ll target by city, county or radius. Search keywords carry most of the intent anyway.

More verification is coming. On Oct. 20, 2026, Google announced it will bring its Financial Services Verification program to the U.S. and Canada in early 2027, requiring certain financial advertisers to show they’re authorized by relevant regulators, per PPC Land. Google has only named some product types so far, and it hasn’t said whether NMLS-licensed lenders will be covered or what the deadlines are. We’d make sure your Google Ads account name, website and business details match your NMLS records now, so there’s nothing to untangle later.

Keywords we’d build around in Houston:

  • Program searches: “first time home buyer programs houston,” “houston down payment assistance,” “tsahc lender near me,” “houston hap lender.”
  • Loan types: “fha loan houston,” “va loan lender cypress,” “jumbo loan the woodlands,” “self employed mortgage houston.”
  • Broker and local intent: “mortgage broker the woodlands,” “local mortgage lender katy.”

And negatives: jobs, salary, “loan officer license,” “nmls course,” “pay my mortgage,” “mortgage login,” servicer names, “reverse mortgage” if you don’t do them, and “calculator” on campaigns where budget is tight. First-time buyer programs make the best content

The most useful content a Houston loan officer can publish is a clear explanation of the assistance programs buyers have heard about and don’t understand. Two are worth a page each.

City of Houston Homebuyer Assistance Program (HAP). Per the city’s HAP guidelines (version 8.0), effective June 23, 2026, HAP offers up to $75,000 as a forgivable loan. Buyers generally need household income at or below 80% of area median income (up to 120% for some TIRZ-funded assistance), the home must be within the City of Houston’s incorporated areas, buyers must finish an 8-hour homebuyer course from a HUD-approved counseling agency, and the mortgage must be a fixed-rate loan from a participating lender. That city-limits rule matters: plenty of buyers searching “Houston” are shopping in Cypress, Spring or Pearland.

Texas State Affordable Housing Corporation (TSAHC). Homes for Texas Heroes serves full-time teachers, school staff in certain roles, police, firefighters, EMS, certain corrections officers, nursing faculty and veterans. Home Sweet Texas serves low- and moderate-income buyers. Both offer down payment assistance of up to 5% of the loan amount, as a grant or a forgivable second lien, and a Mortgage Credit Certificate for first-time buyers worth a federal tax credit of 15% of the mortgage interest paid each year. Buyers apply through a TSAHC-approved participating lender.

Only build content around programs you can actually originate. If you aren’t an approved lender for TSAHC or HAP, either get approved or don’t publish the page. A buyer who finds out on the second call that you can’t do the program they came for won’t give you a third.

Spanish-language versions of these pages pay off in Houston. Our Hispanic marketing guide covers Spanish SEO and ads, and the same Reg Z and NMLS rules apply in either language.

Follow-up, texts and the past-client database

Your past clients are the cheapest source of your next loan, and most loan officers barely talk to them.

  • Send a short annual check-in on the loan anniversary with the client’s rough equity picture and a reminder that you’re around.
  • Mail a holiday card and a hurricane-season prep list in June. Houston homeowners will open the second one.
  • When rates move, call the clients who benefit. Use a list, not a blast.
  • Ask for referrals by name after closing, when the client is happiest. A gift to the client for doing business with you is fine; rewarding them for referring others is where Section 8 bites.

Texting needs consent and care. Loan status updates differ from marketing texts, and Texas brought marketing texts under its telephone solicitation law through SB 140, effective Sept. 1, 2025, per Morgan Lewis. Our Texas SMS rules post covers what to check with counsel.

What to track, and a first 90 days

Judge every channel by cost per funded loan. Cost per lead tells you almost nothing in this business.

  1. Days 1 to 30. Audit every profile and ad for NMLS and company disclosures. Fix your Google Business Profile with your name, company and NMLS ID. Build pages for HAP and TSAHC (if you’re approved) and your top two loan types. Set up call tracking and a CRM stage for each referral partner.
  2. Days 31 to 60. Launch one search campaign on program and loan-type keywords and one Meta campaign of educational videos. Book two agent education sessions. Start the past-client anniversary sequence.
  3. Days 61 to 90. Compare cost per application and cost per funded loan by source. Shift budget toward whatever is producing funded loans, and drop purchased leads if they aren’t pulling their weight after real follow-up.

Frequently asked questions

How do mortgage loan officers get more leads?

The leads that close best come from real estate agents, builders, financial professionals and past clients, so most of your effort should go into being easy to refer and staying in touch with past borrowers. Add educational content about local programs, Google search ads on program and loan-type keywords, and Meta video ads under the Financial Products and Services category. Purchased rate-shopper leads can work, but only with very fast follow-up.

Can a loan officer pay for a realtor’s marketing?

Not in exchange for referrals. RESPA Section 8 bars giving or accepting anything of value under an agreement to refer settlement business, and there is no exception for small gifts. Joint advertising is generally handled by each party paying its fair share of the actual cost for the space it gets. Normal educational events not conditioned on referrals are permitted. Have your compliance team review any co-marketing arrangement.

Do Texas mortgage ads need an NMLS number?

Yes. Texas SML rules require mortgage advertising to include the company name and NMLS ID, the company website if it has one, and the sponsored originator’s name and NMLS ID, with an exception for ads the company runs directly. On social media, you can meet the requirement by displaying the information prominently on your profile. Team names must appear with the company name and NMLS ID at substantially equivalent prominence.

What are Reg Z trigger terms in mortgage advertising?

Under Regulation Z, stating a required down payment, the number of payments or repayment period, a payment amount or a finance charge amount triggers extra disclosures: the down payment, the repayment terms and the APR. Phrases like no down payment, low monthly payments or no closing costs do not trigger. If you state an interest rate, the APR must also appear and be at least as prominent.

Can I target by age or ZIP code in Facebook mortgage ads?

No. Meta requires loan ads in the U.S. to run under the Financial Products and Services special ad category, which fixes age at 18 to 65+, includes all genders, removes ZIP code targeting, requires at least a 15-mile radius and blocks lookalike audiences. Google has similar limits for credit ads. Write specific creative, such as a video about one Houston program, so the right buyers recognize themselves.
