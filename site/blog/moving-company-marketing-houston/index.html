The 30th of the month, a Saturday, 7:10 a.m. Three crews are already out: a two-bedroom leaving an apartment in Katy, a four-bedroom in Kingwood, and a downsizing couple in The Woodlands headed to a patio home in Conroe. Your dispatcher is juggling a truck with a sticky liftgate. The office line rings and it’s a woman in Spring whose “movers” never showed up after she paid a $400 deposit to a website. She wants to know if you can do it today.

Meanwhile, a lead site has sold the same quote request to you and five competitors overnight. By the time anyone in your office looks at it, two of them have already called.

That morning has most of moving company marketing in it. Demand piles up on the same weekends. Trust is low because of the scammers. And the company that answers first with a believable price wins the job.

To get more moving customers in Houston, put your TxDMV number (and your USDOT number if you go interstate) everywhere a customer looks, win the Google Maps results for your part of town, run tight search ads and Local Services Ads, and answer quote requests in minutes, not hours. Then build the partnerships with apartments, agents and relocation managers that fill your calendar without bidding wars. Judge every bit of it on cost per booked move, never cost per lead.

Start with the license, because Texas makes it part of your advertising

Most marketing advice for movers skips this, and it’s the cheapest trust signal you have.

In Texas, anyone who transports household goods for compensation has to register with TxDMV, regardless of vehicle weight, per TxDMV’s registration requirements page. Carriers under 26,000 pounds need at least $300,000 in liability coverage, plus cargo coverage of $5,000 per vehicle and $10,000 aggregate. The fees are small: a $100 application, $10 per vehicle per year and $100 for each insurance filing.

The advertising rule is what matters for marketing. Under 43 Texas Administrative Code §218.52, a household goods carrier can’t run false or misleading ads, and its print ads aimed at a local Texas market must show the business name as it appears on the certificate, a Texas street address and the registration number in the form “TxDMV No. ______.” Any website operated by or for the carrier has to show the name, TxDMV’s toll-free consumer help line and that “TxDMV No.” on its home page. Trucks need the name and number on both sides in letters at least two inches high.

If you do interstate moves, federal rules add one more line. 49 CFR 375.207 requires every ad for interstate household goods service to include your name as it appears on your USDOT registration and your number in the form “U.S. DOT No. ______.”

We’d go further than the minimum. Put both numbers in the header of the site, on every quote page, in the Google Business Profile description, on the crew shirts and in the email signature. Link the TxDMV number to the state’s lookup tool. A nervous customer who checks your number and finds “Active” is halfway to booking. One who can’t find it moves on, and honestly, they should.

The trust gap: selling against moving scams

Movers start every conversation in a hole they didn’t dig. the BBB’s “Know Your Mover” study found the bureau receives an average of about 13,000 complaints and negative reviews about movers each year. In 2019, FMCSA logged 4,780 complaints, and 57% involved overcharging. The same study describes the pattern: a low quote by phone, a deposit, a job passed to an unlicensed crew, and a higher price once the goods are on the truck. It also notes some scammers work with online sites that “find movers” and post phony reviews.

The federal crackdown is real and Texas is in the middle of it. FMCSA’s 2024 Operation Protect Your Move report lists 128 hostage-goods complaints addressed in that operation, and Texas had 11 investigations, the third-highest of any state. In Houston, KPRC reported in May 2026 that the BBB was warning families about scams as peak season started, telling them to check registration, avoid large deposits and be wary of phone-only estimates.

So your marketing has to answer the scam questions before the customer asks them. Here’s what that looks like on a page and on the phone:

Texas already requires much of this. Per TxDMV’s consumer page for people hiring movers, a licensed mover has to give a written proposal before loading that’s either binding or not-to-exceed, and hand over the “Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move in Texas” brochure. Say so on your site in plain words: “You’ll get a written not-to-exceed price before we load a single box.” Scammers can’t copy that line without lying, and most of them won’t bother with a real address and real truck photos either.

Use photos of your actual lettered trucks and uniformed crews. Customers have learned that stock photos of smiling couples with boxes are what fly-by-night sites use.

Google Business Profile and reviews for movers

Most local moves start with a search like “movers near me” or “moving company Sugar Land,” and the map pack takes up a big piece of that screen. Choose Moving company or Mover as your primary category (match what your top-ranking local competitors use), and add Moving and storage service or Storage facility only if you actually run storage. If you have a yard or warehouse customers can visit, show the address. If you don’t, set it up as a service-area business with the towns you really serve.

Fill the Services list with what people search for: apartment moves, packing, piano moves, senior moves, office moves, labor-only loading, long-distance to Dallas, Austin and San Antonio. Put the TxDMV number in the description. Post photos every week from real jobs, with the customer’s permission and without house numbers in the shot.

Reviews do double duty for a mover. They feed the map ranking and they’re the main way a scared customer decides you’re real. The rules from Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps are short: no incentives, no discouraging negative reviews and no selectively asking only happy customers. In April 2026, Google also added language against telling staff to collect a set number of reviews or reviews with specific content, per PPC Land, so skip the crew contests.

The ask that works: a text from the office the evening of the move, after the last box is in, with the same link for everyone.

“Hi Dana, it’s Marcus from [Company]. Thanks for trusting us with today’s move to Conroe. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other families find a licensed mover: [link]. And if anything arrived damaged, reply here and we’ll start a claim tomorrow.”

That last sentence matters. It sends unhappy customers to you first, and you still sent the same link to everyone. Reply to every review, and to the bad ones with specifics and a phone number. Our complete guide to Google reviews has more templates.

Local Services Ads and Google Ads for movers

Google’s Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes “Moving services” and “Storage.” LSAs are the pay-per-lead boxes at the top of the results. You pay for a call or message instead of a click. Screening varies by category and location, and per Google’s LSA screening page it can include license checks, a certificate of insurance and background checks for owners and the people who do the work. Have your TxDMV certificate, insurance certificate and crew list ready before you start.

Two other policy points. Google currently requires advanced verification for locksmith and garage door ads in the U.S. but doesn’t list movers there, per Google’s local services ad policy, so a mover’s regular search ads go through the standard advertiser verification every account eventually faces. And LSA itself is moving into Google Ads. Search Engine Journal reported that starting in August 2026, a first group of U.S. advertisers sees LSA run as pay-per-lead Performance Max campaigns inside Google Ads, with daily budgets and a single target CPA, and the separate LSA dashboard going away after migration. If your LSA account flips over, check the budget settings the same week.

Regular search ads still matter, because LSA gives you almost no control over which jobs you get. How we’d set up a local mover:

Sample ad copy that answers the trust question in the ad itself:

  • Headline: “Licensed Houston Movers, TxDMV No. [number]”
  • Headline: “Written Not-To-Exceed Price”
  • Headline: “Quote by Video in 15 Minutes”
  • Description: “Local crews, our own trucks, no large deposit. Apartments, homes and offices across Katy, Cypress and The Woodlands.”

Set location targeting to people in or regularly in your area. Then add a separate campaign targeting people in other states searching “movers Houston,” since out-of-state transferees often book the destination crew before they fly in. Lead aggregator sites: why they feel cheap and end up expensive

Moving lead sites are the first thing most new movers buy, and the first thing most established movers complain about. The model is simple: a customer fills out one form, and the site sells that lead to several movers. What follows is a race to call, a race to the bottom on price, and a customer who now has five companies texting them.

In accounts we’ve looked at, the problems repeat:

  • Shared leads. You’re paying for the same person four or five competitors are paying for. Your close rate is capped before you dial.
  • Stale or fake contacts. Wrong numbers, people who already booked, people who were just curious about a price in March for a move in August.
  • Price anchoring. When five movers quote, the customer remembers the lowest number, and the lowest number is often the one that turns into a hostage situation later. The BBB study mentions scammers working with “find a mover” sites.
  • No asset. When you stop paying, the leads stop. Nothing you built carries over.

We wouldn’t tell every mover to quit them. A young company with no reviews may need them for a season. But treat them like any other channel: track booked moves per 100 leads and revenue per lead, call within five minutes, use the dispute process for bad contacts, and cap the spend. The money usually does more on your own Google Business Profile, search ads and partnerships, where the customer only talked to you. Quote speed decides a lot of moves

A customer asking for a moving quote usually asks two or three companies at once. The first credible, specific price tends to set the bar for everyone after it. Customers say they choose on price. A lot of the time they actually choose the first mover that felt organized.

A few things that make the speed possible:

  • A real quote form. Ask for move date, origin and destination ZIPs, home size, stairs or elevator, and big items (piano, safe, treadmill). Skip “tell us about your move” free-text boxes.
  • Video surveys. A five-minute FaceTime or video walkthrough beats a phone guess and is faster than an in-home visit. It also answers the BBB’s warning about phone-only estimates.
  • Text-back for missed calls. On a Saturday when the office is half-staffed, an automatic “Sorry we missed you, here’s a link to get a quote” keeps the lead warm.
  • Evening coverage. Many quote requests come in after work. Someone should answer until 8 p.m. in peak season, even if it’s an answering service that books a callback.

Seasonality: summer, month-end and Houston weather

Moving demand isn’t smooth, and your ad budget shouldn’t be either. Summer is the peak (KPRC’s May 2026 report treated May as the start of the busy season), driven by school calendars and lease cycles. Inside every month there’s a second peak: the last few days and the first of the month, when apartment leases turn over. Weekends book first.

What we’d do with that pattern:

  • Raise search budgets from April through August, and shift spend toward the second half of each month, when month-end movers are booking.
  • Sell the off-peak on purpose. A midweek, mid-month discount in January or February fills trucks that would otherwise sit. Put it in the ads and on the quote page.
  • Stop paying for clicks you can’t serve. When the last weekend of June is full, pause the campaigns that target that weekend’s searches or switch the message to “booking July now.”
  • Plan for the weather. Houston crews in August need earlier start times, and customers like hearing that you plan around the heat. Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30; have a storm-related page ready for packing out flood-damaged homes and moving contents to storage, if you do that work.

Apartments, agents and corporate relocation: the partnerships that fill trucks

The best moving leads are the ones nobody else got. Those mostly come from people who see moves before they happen.

  • Apartment leasing offices. Communities in Katy, Cypress, Spring and The Woodlands turn over units every month. Leasing staff get asked “do you know a good mover?” constantly. A one-page flyer, a tenant discount (paid to the tenant, never a kickback to staff, which can create its own problems), and showing up reliably for their residents is the whole program.
  • Real estate agents. Agents want their clients’ closing week to go smoothly. Give them a referral card, a priority slot for closings and a quick text update when their client’s move is done.
  • Senior living and downsizing. Retirement communities and senior move managers send steady work that’s often midweek, exactly when you have capacity.
  • HR and relocation managers. Corporate moves are bigger and pay on time.

That last group deserves attention right now. Chevron is moving its headquarters and all corporate functions from California to Houston over five years, per ABC13. the Greater Houston Partnership reported in February 2026 that Expand Energy planned to move its headquarters to Houston later in 2026 and that the combined Devon and Coterra would be headquartered here. And the Houston metro added 126,720 people between July 2024 and July 2025, the largest gain of any U.S. metro, according to Census estimates reported by ABC13.

Not every transferee gets a full relocation package, and plenty get a lump sum and pick their own movers. Build a “Relocating to Houston” page that explains neighborhoods and commutes in a paragraph each, offers destination-side services (unloading a container, unpacking, storage), and lets someone in California book a video survey. Then run search ads to people outside Texas searching for Houston movers.

Cost per booked move: the only number worth arguing about

Cost per lead is easy to measure and easy to fool yourself with. A $15 shared lead that books 1 time in 20 costs $300 per move. A $90 search lead that books 1 time in 3 costs $270. The cheap lead lost.

To get the real number, you need four things wired together:

  1. A tracked phone number per channel (Google Ads, LSA, Business Profile, lead sites, partners) with calls recorded. See how to track calls and forms in GA4 and Google Ads.
  2. A CRM or moving software where every lead keeps its original source.
  3. A “booked” status that means a deposit or signed proposal, not “sounded interested.”
  4. Revenue per move attached to each booking, so a $600 apartment move and a $6,000 corporate relocation don’t count the same.

Frequently asked questions

How do moving companies get more customers?

Most local movers grow through Google: a complete Business Profile with real photos and steady reviews, search ads on town and move-type keywords, and Local Services Ads in the Moving services category. Partnerships with apartment leasing offices, real estate agents, senior communities and relocation managers add jobs that nobody else is bidding on. Answering quote requests within minutes often matters as much as any ad.

Do Texas movers have to show their TxDMV number in ads?

Yes. Texas rules require household goods carriers to show the registration number in the form TxDMV No. followed by the number in print ads aimed at a local Texas market, along with the business name and a Texas street address. Websites must show the name, TxDMV’s consumer help line and the number on the home page. Interstate movers also need U.S. DOT No. in their ads.

Are moving lead sites worth it?

Sometimes, for a new company that needs volume quickly. The catch is that many lead sites sell the same request to several movers, so you compete on speed and price for every job, and contacts are often stale. Track booked moves and revenue per lead, call within five minutes, dispute bad leads, and compare the cost per booked move with your own search ads and referrals.

Can moving companies use Google Local Services Ads?

Yes. Google’s Local Services Ads list includes Moving services and Storage. You pay per lead rather than per click, and screening can include license checks, insurance certificates and background checks depending on category and location. Google began moving LSA into Google Ads as pay-per-lead Performance Max campaigns in August 2026, so check budget settings if your account migrates.

When is peak moving season in Houston?

Summer is the busiest stretch, with May usually treated as the start, because school calendars and lease renewals line up. Within each month, the last few days and the first are busiest because apartment leases turn over then, and weekends book first. Movers can fill slow periods by promoting midweek and mid-month pricing in winter and raising ad budgets from April through August.
