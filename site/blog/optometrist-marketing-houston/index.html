There’s no optometry category in the big Google Ads benchmark studies, but the closest one, Physicians & Surgeons, runs a median of $4.76 per click and $40.04 per lead, per WordStream and LocaliQ’s 2026 Google Ads benchmarks. That’s cheaper than dental. The hard part in Houston and The Woodlands is who you’re bidding against: retail optical chains, big-box vision centers, and online eyewear brands that now have storefronts with exam lanes.

A Tuesday in late August at an independent practice in Spring: four open exam slots tomorrow. A mom in Cypress types “eye doctor that takes VSP near me” because her kid failed the school vision screening. The first three results are a mall chain, a warehouse-store vision center and a directory site. Your practice is fourth on the map, and she never scrolls that far.

Winning that search means showing up for exam and insurance queries, making it obvious you take her plan, letting her book at 9:40 p.m., and giving her reasons to buy glasses from you instead of a website. This guide is for independent ODs with one to five offices.

Rank for eye exam and eye doctor searches

The searches that fill exam chairs are local and specific, so you need a page for each service and each office, plus a Google Business Profile complete enough to win the map pack.

  • One page per service: comprehensive exams, contact lens fittings, pediatric exams, dry eye, myopia control, diabetic exams, red-eye and urgent visits.
  • One page per office with address, hours, the doctors who see patients there, and the plans accepted at that location.
  • Doctor bio pages with the licensed name, school and any certifications, named correctly (see the Texas rules below).
  • Answers to pre-booking questions: how long an exam takes, whether dilation is included, what a contact lens fitting costs.

The map-pack basics are the same as any local practice: “Optometrist” as the primary category, real photos, steady reviews, accurate hours. Our guide to ranking in the Google map pack in Houston has the checklist, and our web design and SEO team builds these pages.

Build vision plan pages that answer “do you take my insurance?”

Lots of routine exam searches start with a vision plan, so a patient-friendly page for each plan you accept is often the best-converting page an independent practice can add.

Texas law is on your side. §351.155 of the Optometry Act bars the Texas Optometry Board from restricting truthful statements about accepted insurance or health plans, office hours, languages spoken or installment payments.

  • A page per in-network plan (VSP, EyeMed and the rest) explaining what’s typically covered and what to bring.
  • Vision vs. medical insurance. A short explainer on why a dry eye visit bills to the medical plan heads off billing-day surprises and bad reviews.
  • A plain “plans we don’t take” note. It saves your front desk a dozen calls a week.

The plans sell glasses too. VSP’s own consumer guide points members to Eyeconic, its in-network online store, and EyeMed’s list of online in-network stores includes Glasses.com, ContactsDirect and LensCrafters.com, with a footnote that EyeMed and those stores share a parent, EssilorLuxottica. “We take VSP” gets a patient in the door. The exam, fitting and follow-up are what keep them.

Market medical eye care, not just glasses

Medical eye care is the easiest way for an independent practice to stand apart from chains, because a website can’t do it.

  • Dry eye. A dedicated page and a symptom checklist, written the way patients describe it after a day in cold office air followed by Houston humidity.
  • Myopia control. The FDA authorized Essilor Stellest spectacle lenses for kids 6 to 12 on Sept. 25, 2025, and MiSight 1 day contacts were approved in 2019 for kids 8 to 12, per Myopia Profile. A page on options, costs and how progress is measured will get found by parents.
  • School screenings. Texas requires vision screening at age 4, kindergarten, for new entrants and in grades 1, 3, 5 and 7, per the Texas DSHS screening schedule. Run a pediatric push from late July through September around Katy, Cy-Fair, Conroe and Humble ISDs (timing in our back-to-school guide).
  • Same-day visits. “Pink eye doctor near me” is an urgent search. If you hold same-day slots, say so.

One catch: Google treats health as a sensitive interest category, and its personalized advertising policy bars advertiser-curated audiences like Customer Match and remarketing lists there. Assume dry eye campaigns fall under it and build them on keywords.

Set up Google Business Profile and online booking

Patients book eye exams at night and on weekends, so a booking button that opens a real calendar is worth more than most ads.

  • Add the link. In Search choose “Booking” on your profile (in Maps, “Edit profile”), then “Add link,” paste the URL and save, per Google’s help page on booking links.
  • Show real slots. A “request” form your staff returns tomorrow loses to a competitor that confirms instantly.
  • One profile per office, with the right suite number.
  • Update hours the morning a storm or freeze closes you. Nobody forgets driving to a dark office.

And answer the phone. Our post on speed to lead explains why the first practice to respond usually gets the appointment.

Use Local Services Ads for optometrists

Google offers Local Services Ads for optometrists: “Optometrist” is on Google’s Local Services Ads category list, so they’re worth testing for exam bookings.

  • Verification takes paperwork. Google’s screening requirements for optometrists include provider and practice background checks and state licenses, plus insurance and NPI checks for some accounts.
  • Leads are calls and messages. Google says booking leads aren’t available for health care, so someone has to pick up.
  • You pay per lead, not per click, and can dispute invalid ones.

Run LSAs next to search ads for a quarter and compare cost per booked exam. Our LSA vs. Google Ads comparison was written for home services, but the trade-offs carry over.

Follow Texas Optometry Board advertising rules

Texas optometry ads must follow the Texas Optometry Act and Texas Optometry Board rules, and price-ad disclosures are where practices slip most.

  • Nothing false or misleading. Texas Occupations Code §351.403 bans deceptive statements about ophthalmic services or materials in any medium.
  • Prescription notice. Ads for prescription glasses or contacts must say a prescription is required to buy them.
  • Price disclosures. A price ad must say whether the exam and follow-up are included, plus any time limit under 30 days, quantity or per-customer limits, and for contacts the number of lenses.
  • “Board certified” needs a name. Texas Optometry Board Rule 279.9 requires naming the certifying organization.

Under the FTC’s updated Eyeglass Rule, effective Sept. 24, 2024, prescribers who sell eyewear must get a signed confirmation that the patient received their prescription and keep it three years. Patients leave with it, so plan on earning the sale.

Check current wording on the Texas Optometry Board’s rules page. This isn’t legal advice; have counsel review price promotions.

Keep tracking pixels away from patient data

HIPAA still limits ad pixels on eye care websites, especially on patient portals and booking flows.

  • What the court changed. In June 2024 a federal court in Fort Worth vacated part of HHS’s tracking bulletin (IP addresses plus visits to public health-condition pages) and left the rest, per Holland & Knight’s summary of AHA v. Becerra. HHS later withdrew its appeal.
  • Keep Meta and TikTok pixels off the portal and scheduler. Record bookings as conversions with no health details.
  • Meta has its own limits. In early 2025 Meta started restricting the data health advertisers can send; check your categorization in Events Manager.
  • Get a BAA from any vendor that touches patient data.

Build reviews the right way

Patients weigh how recent your reviews are as much as the stars.

  • The bar is high. In BrightLocal’s 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, 31% only use businesses rated 4.5 stars or higher, 74% only care about reviews from the last three months, and 47% won’t use one with fewer than 20.
  • Ask at pickup, when the patient can suddenly read the sign across the street, then text a direct link.
  • Never offer anything for a review.
  • Never confirm someone is a patient in a reply. A California dental practice paid $23,000 to settle with HHS after its owner disclosed patient details answering Yelp reviews.

Script (illustrative): “Thanks for coming in today, Maria. If our team took good care of you, a quick Google review helps other Pearland families find us: [link].” More in our guide to Google reviews.

Sell more glasses in your own optical

Your optical can’t beat the internet on price, so sell fit, adjustments and repairs. The competition is local now too: an online-first brand opened its 18th Texas store, with exams, at LaCenterra in Katy in January 2023, per Community Impact.

  • Show your frame brands online. Patients search them by name.
  • Advertise the warranty and lifetime adjustments.
  • Track capture rate (exams that end in an optical sale) by doctor and month. We don’t know of a reliable benchmark, so watch your own trend.
  • Send recall and contact lens reorder texts. They’re the cheapest marketing you have.

Mistakes we see in eye care marketing

  • Bidding on “glasses” and “contacts.”
  • Price ads with no disclosures, like “2 pairs $99” with nothing about the exam.
  • A Meta pixel on the booking page.
  • One location page for offices in Tomball, Magnolia and Conroe.
  • Judging by clicks instead of kept exams.

Measure what matters

Track booked exams, show rate, cost per kept exam by channel, new vs. returning patients, and optical capture rate. Clicks and calls are leading indicators; kept exams are the result.

Frequently asked questions

How much should an optometrist spend on Google Ads in Houston?

Many single-office practices can start with $1,500 to $3,000 a month in ad spend, focused on exam, insurance and medical eye care searches. The closest benchmark category, Physicians and Surgeons, has a median of $4.76 per click and $40.04 per lead nationally. Houston often runs higher, so measure cost per booked exam and adjust.

Are Local Services Ads worth it for optometrists?

They’re worth testing. Optometrist is an eligible Local Services Ads category in the US, and you pay per lead instead of per click. Google says booking leads aren’t available for health care, so leads arrive as calls and messages. They only pay off if your front desk answers quickly and you compare them against regular search ads.

Do Texas optometrists have advertising rules?

Yes. The Texas Optometry Act bans false or misleading ads, requires ads for prescription eyewear to say a prescription is required, and requires price ads to disclose whether the exam is included, time limits and, for contacts, the number of lenses. Board Rule 279.9 requires naming the organization behind any board certified claim.

Can an eye care practice use the Meta pixel on its website?

Carefully, and not everywhere. Keep pixels off patient portals, scheduling flows and pages where patients enter health information, and get a business associate agreement from vendors that handle patient data. A 2024 court ruling narrowed HHS’s tracking guidance but left most of it in place, and Meta now limits data from health advertisers too.

How can an independent optometrist compete with online glasses and retail chains?

Compete on what a website can’t do. Market medical eye care like dry eye treatment and myopia control, make online booking fast, show which vision plans you accept on dedicated pages, and stand behind fittings, adjustments and warranties in your optical. Steady recent reviews help you win the map pack against chain locations.
