Pest control Google Ads work when you split campaigns by pest (termite, general pest, mosquito, rodent), use exact and phrase match with a long negative keyword list, target only the ZIP codes your trucks serve and count phone calls as conversions. Skip any one of those and most of your budget goes to people looking for bug spray at Home Depot.

Below is the account we’d build for a residential pest company in The Woodlands, Spring or Conroe, in the order we’d build it, with the cost figures we could actually verify and the ad copy limits Texas puts on pest companies.

What pest control Google Ads cost in Houston

Real pest control numbers are hard to find, so here’s what we could verify.

  • LocaliQ’s home services benchmarks put pest and rodent control search ads at a $6.55 average cost per click, a 4.31% click-through rate and a $39.25 cost per lead, with a typical spend of $1,250 a month. That data is from 2020 and 2021, so treat it as a floor. Costs have climbed since.
  • An agency that runs pest control accounts, writing in PCT Online, reported a $5.99 average CPC across its clients and an average cost per lead of about $98 in 2025, with mosquito leads as low as $30 in some months.

Neither source breaks out Houston, and we wouldn’t assume our suburbs come in under those figures during swarm weeks. Keyword Planner’s top-of-page bid ranges for your own ZIP codes are a better starting point than any national average. For broader context, see our Houston Google Ads cost per click benchmarks.

Campaign structure: termite, general pest, mosquito, rodent

The single best structural decision is giving termite work its own campaign. Termite jobs are worth far more than a general pest visit, the season is short and the cost per click can spike in swarm weeks. Lumped in with “roach exterminator” keywords, termite searches compete for the same daily budget and lose.

The PCT Online piece warns against splitting every pest into its own campaign, because most searchers use general phrases like “pest control near me.” We agree. Separate campaigns only for pests that have their own season, price or budget.

Pest control PPC keywords: match types and negatives

Use exact and phrase match. Broad match on a small pest budget tends to find every DIY search in Texas. The agency writing in PCT Online reported that six words (spray, repellant, trap, killer, bait and poison) were eating more than a quarter of its clients’ budgets before they were blocked.

Starter negative keyword list

Add these as broad match negatives at the account level, then read your search terms report weekly and keep adding.

ThemeNegatives to add
DIY intentdiy, how to get rid of, home remedy, homemade, yourself
Productsspray, killer, trap, bait, poison, repellent, repellant, fogger, bomb, dunks, granules
Retailhome depot, lowes, walmart, amazon, ace hardware

Researchpictures, identify, life cycle, what do, symptoms
Wrong servicebee removal (if you don’t do it), car, plants, garden, chickens

Careful with “spray.” As a broad negative it also blocks “mosquito spraying service,” which is exactly what your mosquito campaign sells, so use a phrase negative like “mosquito spray” there instead. And leave “cost” and “price” alone. Someone searching “termite treatment cost Spring TX” is close to buying.

Pest control ads examples that follow Texas rules

Before you copy a template, read the Texas advertising rule for pest control licensees (4 TAC § 7.152). Its list of prohibited claims includes “safe,” “harmless,” “non-toxic to humans and pets,” “environmentally friendly” and “organic,” and it requires every ad to show your business name as it appears on your TDA license. The rule’s definition of advertising covers digital ads, so a Google headline counts the same as a truck wrap. A lot of national pest ad templates break it in headline one.

Some headlines that stay within the rules (swap in your name and towns):

  • Termite Inspection in The Woodlands
  • Formosan Swarmers? Book Today
  • Licensed by the Texas Dept. of Ag
  • Quarterly Pest Plans From $125
  • Same-Week Service in Spring & Conroe
  • Rodent Exclusion and Attic Sealing
  • Locally Owned in Montgomery County
  • Call Now, Real People Answer

A description line might read: “Formosan termites swarm at night in late May and June. Our licensed techs inspect, photograph and explain your options. Book online or call.” Specific, local and nothing the state would object to.

Responsive search ads take up to 15 headlines. We like a mix of roughly a third service, a third location and a third call to action, and we pin only the headline that carries the licensed business name so the rule is met every time the ad shows. Test “Schedule an inspection” against “Get a free estimate” on termite ads; the PCT Online piece describes a call-to-action change like that paying off. Prices in ads only work if they match your landing page and what the phone team quotes.

Assets that make the phone ring

Assets (Google’s new name for extensions) are where pest ads win the click.

  • Call asset: your tracking number, scheduled for hours someone actually answers. Extend it into the evening during swarm season.
  • Location asset: link your Google Business Profile so your address and rating can show.
  • Sitelinks: Termite Inspections, Mosquito Program, Quarterly Plans, Service Areas, Reviews.
  • Callouts: TDA licensed, locally owned, free re-service between visits, online booking.
  • Structured snippets: Services header listing termites, ants, roaches, rodents, mosquitoes, bed bugs.

Each sitelink should go to its own page. Sending everything to your homepage is one of the most common leaks we see. Our landing page guide covers what those pages need.

Location targeting by ZIP across Montgomery and Harris counties

Google’s location targeting help confirms that in the U.S. you can target postal codes, and it describes two options: “Presence or interest,” which also reaches people who have shown interest in your area, and “Presence,” which limits ads to people physically there. For pest control, set it to Presence. Someone in Dallas reading about Houston termites won’t book you.

Then target by ZIP rather than a big radius. A 25-mile ring around The Woodlands pulls in north Houston neighborhoods you may never drive to. ZIPs let you add 77380, 77381 and 77382 for The Woodlands, 77375 for Tomball, 77354 for Magnolia, 77301 for Conroe and 77339 and 77345 for Kingwood, and then compare cost per lead by area.

After 30 days, check the location report. Raise bids in ZIPs that book well and are close to your routes. Cut ZIPs where the drive eats the profit. If you serve both sides of the county line, keep Montgomery County and Harris County ZIPs in separate campaigns or ad groups so you can see each clearly.

Google ads for exterminators vs. Local Services Ads

Pest control is a Local Services category, listed in Google’s Local Services category list, and LSA charges per lead. On many “exterminator near me” searches it shows above regular search ads. Most pest companies should run both. LSA is often the cheaper lead, while search ads let you push termite season, choose your landing pages and run specific offers.

Watch for overlap. If LSA is already eating the generic “pest control near me” calls at a good price, move search ad budget toward termite and mosquito keywords LSA covers less well. Our LSA vs. Google Ads comparison goes deeper.

On approvals: pest control isn’t one of the categories in Google’s Advanced Verification policy (that program covers garage door and locksmith services), but LSA runs its own background and license screening, and Texas wants your TDA license issued before you advertise at all.

Conversion tracking for calls

Most pest leads arrive as phone calls, so an account that only counts form fills is flying blind. Google’s call conversion help explains that you set a minimum call length, and every call lasting at least that long counts as a conversion. Tracking calls from ads and your website requires Google forwarding numbers, so turn on call reporting in account settings.

For pest, we set the minimum call length around 60 to 90 seconds so wrong numbers and robocalls don’t count. Then go a step further: import booked jobs from your scheduling software as offline conversions, so Google learns which clicks became customers. Our post on call tracking covers the tools.

Your first 30 days in the account

A new pest account is never right on day one. Here’s the routine we follow in the first month, and it’s the same one we’d hand an owner running ads in-house.

Week 1

Check that calls are being recorded as conversions by placing a test call from an ad preview and staying on past your minimum length. Look at the search terms report every day. You’ll find DIY and product searches the starter list missed, especially brand names of retail sprays. Add them.

Week 2

Listen to a sample of recorded calls if your tracking tool allows it. Are people asking for services you sell, in towns you serve? Are calls going unanswered at lunch or after 5 p.m.? Missed calls are wasted clicks, and they’re usually a staffing fix, not an ads fix.

Weeks 3 and 4

Compare cost per lead by campaign and by ZIP. Pause keywords that spent twice your target cost per lead without a single call. Rewrite the weakest headlines, keeping your best performers. If the termite campaign is limited by budget during swarm weeks, move money from general pest rather than raising the total.

By day 30 you should know three things: your real cost per lead, which pest brings the cheapest booked jobs, and which areas are worth more budget. If you can’t answer those, the tracking is the problem, and that’s the first thing to fix. Seasonality: shifting bids and budgets by month

Smart Bidding already learns regular seasonal patterns. Google’s own help on seasonality adjustments says they’re built for short events of one to seven days and may not work as well past 14. So don’t set a “termite season” adjustment for three months. Use one for a two or three day burst, like the first big Formosan swarm nights or the week after a flood.

For the longer seasons, move budget between campaigns instead. Raise the termite budget in March, let mosquito take over in summer, lift rodent in late fall. Expect cost per click to rise when everyone else does the same, and decide your ceiling in advance with a maximum CPC limit.

If you already have an account running, start with two checks before you build anything new. Under campaign settings, open Locations and confirm the option is “Presence,” not “Presence or interest.” Then pull the last 90 days of search terms, sort by cost and read the top 50 out loud. You’ll know within ten minutes whether DIY searches are eating the budget.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a pest control company spend on Google Ads?

Start from what you can pay for a new customer. If a customer is worth $500 or more over a year on a plan, spending $100 to $150 to win one is reasonable. A small shop can test with a few thousand dollars a month, spending more from March through June for termites. LocaliQ data from 2020 and 2021 showed pest companies spending about $1,250 a month.

What is a good cost per lead for pest control Google Ads?

It varies by pest and season. LocaliQ’s 2020 and 2021 benchmark put pest and rodent control at about $39 per lead, while an agency writing in PCT Online in 2025 reported an average near $98, with mosquito leads as low as $30 in some months. Termite leads usually cost more but are worth more. Judge cost per booked job, not just cost per lead.

Is the Reddit advice on pest control Google Ads right about broad match?

Mostly, yes. The common advice on Reddit and in trade forums is to avoid broad match on small budgets, and that matches what we see. Broad match tends to pull DIY searches for sprays, traps and poisons. Use exact and phrase match, add negative keywords from day one and review the search terms report every week before trying broad match with Smart Bidding later.

What are good pest control advertising ideas besides Google Ads?

Local Services Ads are the closest cousin and often the cheapest lead. Beyond Google, try realtor and home inspector partnerships for termite inspections, door hangers and door-to-door teams in neighborhoods where you already service homes, Nextdoor for recurring plans, and email or text reminders to past one-time customers. Make sure every channel points to the same reviews and license number.

Do pest control companies need special approval to run Google Ads?

Google’s Advanced Verification program applies to garage door and locksmith advertisers, not pest control. You will go through screening if you run Local Services Ads. In Texas, the bigger requirement is your Structural Pest Control Service business license from the Department of Agriculture, which you need before you advertise, plus following the state’s rules on safety and environmental claims.
