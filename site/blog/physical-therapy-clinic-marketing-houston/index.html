Thursday, 4:30 p.m. Two cancellations in the last hour and a 5:15 that won’t show. The orthopedic group in Memorial City that used to send you fifteen referrals a month opened its own therapy department in the spring, and you’ve felt it ever since. Both numbers are “true.” Only one of them pays rent.

Physical therapy clinic marketing in Houston has two halves. One is the referral relationships with physicians, surgeons and athletic trainers that most clinics were built on. The other is reaching patients directly, which got a lot more practical in Texas in 2025. Clinics that grow do both, and they measure one thing: cost per arrived evaluation.

This guide is for outpatient clinics with one to five locations across Houston, The Woodlands, Katy, Sugar Land, Pearland or Kingwood.

Direct access in Texas: what changed in 2025

For years, Texas let a PT treat without a referral for only 10 consecutive business days, which was barely long enough to be worth marketing. HB 4099 changed that. Per the HB 4099 bill analysis, the limit became 30 consecutive calendar days, the separate 15-business-day exception was repealed, and a referral is still required to keep treating past the limit. The law took effect September 1, 2025.

The board’s implementing rule spells out who qualifies. Under the board’s adopted rule §322.1, effective November 1, 2025, the PT must have been licensed for at least one year, carry professional liability insurance of at least $100,000 per claim and $300,000 aggregate, and either hold a doctoral degree in physical therapy or have completed at least 30 CCUs in differential diagnosis. The patient also signs a board-prescribed disclosure form before treatment starts.

What it means for marketing:

  • You can advertise “no referral needed” honestly, as long as your treating PTs qualify and your intake handles the disclosure form.
  • Thirty days covers a real episode of care for many simple cases (ankle sprains, mild low back pain, early running injuries). Say what happens if they need more.
  • Insurance is a separate question. Plenty of commercial and managed plans still require a referral or prior authorization no matter what Texas law allows. Your intake team should check the plan before the first visit, and your website should say so.
  • Medicare runs on its own rules. A physician or qualified practitioner still has to certify the plan of care. For referred patients, Medicare added an exception in 2025: a signed and dated order in the record, plus evidence the plan of care went to the referring provider within 30 days of the evaluation, satisfies certification, per APTA’s explainer.

Physician referrals are still the backbone

Direct access is a growth channel. For most clinics, referrals are still the base, and they erode quietly when nobody tends them.

What keeps a referral source sending patients is mostly boring:

  1. Fast scheduling. Surgeons’ offices want their post-op patients seen within days. If your next opening for a new ACL reconstruction is two weeks out, they’ll send the next one elsewhere.
  2. Clean communication. Send the eval summary and plan of care promptly, then progress notes at set intervals. For Medicare patients the 30-day plan-of-care window gives you a natural deadline.
  3. One point of contact. Someone at your clinic owns each referring office, knows the MA and the scheduler by name, and visits in person.
  4. Tracking by source. Count referrals by physician each month. A drop from eight to three is a phone call this week, not a surprise in December.

Where you look matters in Houston. Orthopedic and sports medicine groups cluster around the Texas Medical Center, Memorial City, The Woodlands and Sugar Land, and many now own therapy services. Independent surgeons, pain management practices, OB-GYNs (for pelvic health), podiatrists and primary care groups are often the better targets. So are high school and club athletic trainers in Katy ISD, Cy-Fair ISD and Conroe ISD territory.

Keep it clean. Gifts, free services or anything of value given to referral sources raise federal anti-kickback questions, especially with Medicare patients. A lunch-and-learn about your post-op protocols is normal practice; anything more generous needs a call to your healthcare attorney first.

Insurance and cash-pay patients need different marketing

A patient with a PPO looking for help after a rotator cuff repair cares about “in network,” “near me” and “how soon.” A runner training for the Chevron Houston Marathon who wants a gait analysis and dry needling cares about expertise and results, and will often pay cash to skip the insurance hassle.

Pelvic health is the clearest cash-pay or out-of-network opportunity we see, and it’s underserved in the suburbs. Postpartum patients in Cypress or Pearland often drive into the city for it. A clinic in those areas with a trained pelvic health therapist has a real reason to advertise.

A note on free offers. A “free 15-minute injury screen” is common and can work. If you see Medicare or Medicaid patients, giveaways to those beneficiaries are limited by OIG’s nominal value policy, which caps gifts of nominal value at $15 per item and $75 per year per beneficiary, with no cash or cash equivalents. Have your compliance person sign off on the offer before it runs.

SEO for condition-specific searches

Patients search by problem, rarely by profession. “Lower back pain treatment Sugar Land.” “Physical therapy for sciatica.” “Knee replacement rehab near me.” “Vertigo treatment Houston.” A clinic with a single “Services” page listing twenty conditions in bullet points won’t rank for any of them.

Build one page per condition you want more of, and make each one useful:

  • What the problem usually is and how you evaluate it, in plain language.
  • What treatment looks like, typical number of visits, and when to expect improvement (as a range, without promises).
  • Whether they need a referral, with the direct access rules in a sentence.
  • Insurance accepted and cash-pay pricing if you offer it.
  • Which therapist leads that program, with credentials.
  • A booking button and a phone number at the top.

Good starting pages for most Houston clinics: low back pain, neck pain, sciatica, post-surgical rehab (ACL, rotator cuff, total knee and hip), sports injuries, running injuries, pelvic health, vestibular and vertigo, and work injuries if you take workers’ comp. Then add location pages for each clinic, since “physical therapy Katy” and “physical therapy Kingwood” are separate searches with separate map packs.

Keep the claims factual. The Texas PT board can discipline advertising that’s false, misleading or deceptive under rule 322.4(b)(12), and the Texas PT board’s advertising FAQ lists claims of superiority and promises that a patient will get better fast as examples of what not to say. “Our therapists have treated thousands of post-op knees” is fine if true. “The best PT in Houston, back to normal in two weeks” isn’t.

Google Ads for physical therapy clinics

Search ads work for PT because the intent is so specific. Someone typing “physical therapy for shoulder pain near me” is a patient, today.

A starting structure:

  • General: physical therapy near me, physical therapist [town], PT clinic [neighborhood].
  • Condition: one ad group per condition page above, sending traffic to that page.
  • Post-surgical: ACL rehab, rotator cuff surgery physical therapy, knee replacement physical therapy.
  • Specialty programs: pelvic floor physical therapy, vestibular therapy, dry needling, sports PT.
  • Brand: your clinic names.

Negatives matter more in PT than most niches, because so many searches come from students and job seekers:

Sample copy for a condition ad group. Headlines: “Back Pain Physical Therapy, Katy,” “No Referral Needed for Most Patients,” “Evaluations This Week.” Description: “One-on-one sessions with a doctor of physical therapy. Most major plans accepted. Book your evaluation online.”

For a sense of cost, WordStream and LocaliQ’s 2026 benchmarks reported a median $4.76 cost per click, a 12.43% conversion rate and a $40.04 cost per lead for Physicians & Surgeons search campaigns from April 2025 to March 2026. PT doesn’t have its own category in that report, so treat it as a rough proxy. A lead isn’t an eval, and the section on measurement below is where the real number lives.

Two policy limits shape targeting. Google’s health in personalized advertising policy bars advertiser-curated audiences for health content, including Customer Match lists and your data segments (the lists remarketing runs on), along with lookalikes and audience expansion. In practice that pushes PT clinics toward keyword and location targeting, which is where the intent is anyway. And Google’s Local Services Ads include a Physical therapist category, per Google’s Local Services Ads category list, though booking leads aren’t available for health care verticals, so you’ll get calls and messages. HIPAA, pixels and remarketing: what you can and can’t track

This is where PT clinics get into real trouble, usually by accident. A website builder or a well-meaning agency installs the Meta pixel and Google tags on every page, including the appointment request form and the intake paperwork. Now a third party gets the fact that a named person booked pelvic floor therapy.

The federal guidance has a history. HHS’s Office for Civil Rights issued a bulletin on tracking technologies in 2022 and revised it in March 2024. In June 2024, a federal court in Fort Worth vacated the part that treated an IP address plus a visit to an unauthenticated public page about a health condition as protected health information. The rest of the bulletin wasn’t vacated, and it still applies to logged-in patient portals and other authenticated pages, per Holland & Knight’s summary of the ruling.

So the practical lines, in our experience working with health clients and their counsel:

Meta adds its own layer. Starting in 2025, Meta began restricting which events health and wellness advertisers can send, with events like Schedule commonly affected and custom events requiring review, according to Freshpaint, which notes Meta hasn’t publicly confirmed all the details. If your Meta campaigns suddenly can’t optimize for bookings, that’s likely why.

The answer for most clinics: track conversions as “a booking happened” without the condition or the person attached, use a vendor that will sign a business associate agreement for anything touching patient data, keep pixels off forms and portals, and import arrived evaluations from your EMR as offline conversions without health details. Talk to your compliance advisor before you change any of it. Our medical practice marketing guide covers more of the healthcare-specific setup.

Reviews without a HIPAA problem

Reviews drive map rankings for PT the same way they do for anyone. PT has two extra constraints.

First, how you reply. In 2023, HHS announced a $30,000 settlement with a New Jersey provider that replied to negative online reviews with details of patients’ diagnoses and treatment. Never confirm that a reviewer is a patient, and never mention their condition or care, even if they did. A safe reply: “Thank you for the feedback. We take every concern seriously and would like to talk. Please call our clinic manager at [number].” Same reply for a glowing review, minus the concern.

Second, how you ask. Google’s policy bars incentives, gating and asking for specific content, per Google’s prohibited content policy. The best moment is the discharge visit or the session where a patient hits a milestone (first pain-free run, back to golf at Woodforest). A text that evening with the link does the rest.

Patient stories on your website and social media are different from Google reviews. Get a signed HIPAA authorization before using anyone’s name, photo, video or story, keep it on file, and don’t publish results that read as a promise. Our Google reviews guide has more templates.

Frequently asked questions

Can you see a physical therapist without a referral in Texas?

Yes, within limits. Since HB 4099 took effect in September 2025, a qualified Texas physical therapist can treat a patient without a referral for up to 30 consecutive calendar days. The PT must have been licensed at least a year, carry required liability insurance, and hold a doctoral degree or 30 CCUs in differential diagnosis. A referral is required to continue, and insurance plans may have their own rules.

How do physical therapy clinics get more patients?

Most grow through two channels at once. Physician referrals still matter, so schedule post-op patients fast and send plans of care promptly. Direct-to-patient marketing fills the rest: a strong Google Business Profile, a page for each condition you treat, search ads on condition keywords, and steady reviews. Track arrived evaluations by source, because leads alone hide where patients actually come from.

Can a physical therapy clinic use Facebook pixel and Google remarketing?

Carefully. HHS guidance on tracking technologies still applies to logged-in portals and authenticated pages, so keep pixels off intake forms, booking flows and portals. Google bars Customer Match and remarketing lists for health content, and Meta restricts some events for health advertisers. Track conversions without health details, use vendors that sign business associate agreements, and have a compliance advisor review your setup.

How much does Google Ads cost for physical therapy?

Recent benchmarks for Physicians and Surgeons search campaigns show median clicks near $4.76 and leads near $40, which is a rough proxy for PT. Many single-location Houston clinics start around $2,000 to $4,000 a month. The number that matters is cost per arrived evaluation, so track how many leads become booked and attended evals before deciding to spend more.

How should a PT clinic respond to online reviews?

Never confirm that the reviewer is a patient or mention their condition or treatment, even if they did. HHS settled with a provider for $30,000 over review replies that disclosed patient details. Thank the reviewer, say you take concerns seriously, and invite them to call the clinic manager. Ask every discharged patient for a review by text, without incentives or screening for happy patients.
