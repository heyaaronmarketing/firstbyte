Thursday is consult day. Fourteen on the books. Three no-show. Two came in with screenshots from other surgeons’ Instagram accounts and a quote from a practice near the Galleria, and mostly wanted to know if you’d beat it. One wanted a procedure you don’t do. Four said they’d “think about it.” Your patient coordinator booked one surgery and has a deposit on another.

Your marketing report says Instagram followers are up and cost per lead is $38. Nobody can tell you which of those 14 consults came from which ad, or what a booked surgery actually cost you.

Plastic surgeon marketing in Houston works when it brings in fewer, better consults: people who already trust your work, know roughly what it costs, and are ready to pick a date. That takes a portfolio people can verify, compliant before-and-after content, procedure-specific search ads, and tracking that follows a patient from first click to surgery date without leaking their health information.

This guide is for board-certified plastic surgeons and cosmetic surgery practices from the Medical Center and River Oaks out to The Woodlands, Sugar Land, Katy and Clear Lake.

Houston is a crowded aesthetic market

Houston has a deep bench of plastic surgeons, plus dermatologists, facial plastic surgeons, oculoplastic surgeons, OB-GYNs and med spas all chasing some of the same patients. Then there are the practices in Miami, Mexico and Colombia advertising to Houstonians directly on Instagram.

Nationally, demand is steady. ASPS members performed nearly 1.6 million cosmetic surgical procedures in 2024, up 1%, led by liposuction (349,728), breast augmentation (306,196), tummy tuck (171,064), breast lift (153,616) and eyelid surgery (120,755), per ASPS’s 2024 procedural statistics. Minimally invasive procedures topped 28.5 million.

In a market this crowded, the practices that win usually own two or three signature procedures in their marketing instead of listing 40 services. “The tummy tuck and mommy makeover surgeon in The Woodlands” is a position people remember, and it’s far easier to rank for.

Board certification claims and Texas Medical Board advertising rules

Credentials are the first filter for a lot of patients, and Texas regulates how you claim them.

Under TMB Rule 164.4, as explained on the Texas Medical Board’s board certification advertising page, a physician can advertise as “board certified” when the certifying board is a member board of the American Board of Medical Specialties, a member board of the American Osteopathic Association Bureau of Osteopathic Specialists, or the American Board of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery. There’s also a path for boards the TMB has found substantially equivalent, and a grandfather clause for certifications granted before September 1, 2010 by boards approved before then. The American Board of Plastic Surgery is an ABMS member board, and patients can check a surgeon’s status with ABPS’s public verification tool.

TMB Rule 164.3 adds a list of things that make an ad misleading. The ones we see trip up aesthetic practices:

  • Using “board eligible,” “board qualified” or similar wording to suggest board certification.
  • Testimonials without a disclaimer about the person’s credentials, or without disclosing that they were paid.
  • Failing to identify models or actors as models, instead of actual patients. Stock photos of perfect abs on a liposuction page are a problem.
  • Untruthful or deceptive claims about costs and fees, and claims of “free” services when a third party gets billed.
  • Claiming a unique or exclusive skill without substantiation. “Houston’s only surgeon who…” needs proof.
  • Failing to disclose that an article or infomercial is a paid presentation.

the Texas Medical Association also notes the tension between the Occupations Code and TMB rules on testimonials and calls their use a substantial legal risk. Before a campaign goes live, we’d have the surgeon or practice counsel review credential language, testimonial use and every “best” claim. If your practice includes non-plastic-surgeon cosmetic surgeons, spell out each doctor’s actual board, and don’t let an ad imply everyone holds the same one.

Before-and-after photos: consent, honesty and the rules

Before-and-after galleries sell surgery. They’re also where practices take the most risk.

Patient photos taken in your practice are protected health information, and full-face photos are an identifier on their own. Under HHS’s marketing guidance, with limited exceptions HIPAA requires a patient’s written authorization before PHI is used for marketing. A general photo consent signed at intake for the medical record isn’t the same thing. You want a separate, specific marketing authorization that says where the photos will appear (website, Instagram, paid ads), whether the face will show, and how the patient can revoke it.

Honesty rules apply too. Don’t retouch results. Keep lighting, angle and posture the same before and after. Label how long after surgery the after photo was taken. And show typical results, not your single best case, because results that don’t reflect what comparable patients get are the classic way testimonials and galleries become misleading.

A practical note: an organized gallery by procedure, body type and age range does more selling than a carousel of 200 random photos. Patients look for someone who looks like them.

Instagram: the portfolio patients actually check

For aesthetic surgery, Instagram is the portfolio, the reference check and often the first DM. Many patients find a surgeon on Google and then go straight to Instagram to see if the work is any good.

What works on organic Instagram for a Houston practice, in our experience:

  • Consented before-and-afters, posted with the procedure, timeline and a short caption from the surgeon about the goal.
  • The surgeon talking to camera about one question at a time: recovery after a tummy tuck, implant sizing, what a consult covers. Patients book with someone they feel they’ve already met.
  • Patient coordinator content. Who they’ll talk to, how financing works, what to bring.
  • Story highlights by procedure so a new visitor can find “BBL,” “Breast,” “Face” and “Pricing” in two taps.

Treat DMs as a lead channel. Someone needs to answer within the hour during business hours, move the conversation to a phone call or booking link, and never discuss a specific patient’s care in a public comment. Meta ads under the health and wellness rules

Paid Meta ads for cosmetic surgery face three layers of rules.

First, the ad content. Meta’s policies, summarized by OuterBox’s summary of Meta’s health ad rules, require cosmetic procedure ads to exclude people under 18, bar ads that generate negative self-perception to promote a procedure, and forbid copy that asserts or implies knowledge of someone’s personal attributes, including medical conditions. “Tired of your muffin top?” gets rejected. “Tummy tuck consults in The Woodlands, financing available” doesn’t.

Second, the tracking. Meta has rolled out restrictions on data from health and wellness advertisers. Search Engine Land describes tiers ranging from limits on custom parameters and URL data, to blocking optimization for mid- and lower-funnel standard events, to fully restricting events in some cases. Practices that relied on pixel-based “lead” or “purchase” optimization saw delivery change. Lead ads that collect information on Meta itself are largely unaffected.

Third, HIPAA. Even where Meta allows an event, HHS’s bulletin on online tracking technologies says pixels on pages where people book appointments or enter health information can capture PHI. Don’t put a Meta pixel on your consult request form without a compliance review.

The workable setup for most practices: Meta lead ads with two or three qualifying questions (procedure, timeline, whether they’ve had a consult elsewhere), a CRM that receives the lead directly, and offline conversion uploads that send back “consult booked” or “surgery booked” without any health detail. Search intent in aesthetics is unusually specific. Someone searching “breast augmentation cost Houston” or “mommy makeover surgeon The Woodlands” has done some homework. Consult fees, pricing pages and financing

Whether to charge for consults is a real debate. A consult fee, often credited toward surgery if the patient books, cuts no-shows and filters out pure price shoppers. A free consult books more appointments and fills the calendar with lower-intent visits. We’ve seen both work. The deciding factor is usually how much surgeon time each consult takes.

Whatever you choose, say it plainly on the site and in the ad. TMB Rule 164.3 treats deceptive claims about costs and fees as misleading, so don’t advertise a “free consultation” if it’s only free when someone else is billed or if the fine print says otherwise.

On pricing, publish “starting at” or typical ranges for your main procedures and say what’s included: surgeon fee, anesthesia, facility, garments, follow-up visits. A patient comparing your all-in range with another practice’s surgeon-only number will think you’re more expensive unless you explain the difference.

Financing deserves its own page. Patient financing companies let many patients turn a $12,000 surgery into a monthly payment, and the page should explain the options, link to the application, and say that approval doesn’t commit anyone to surgery.

Patient reviews and HIPAA: how to reply without a fine

Reviews carry enormous weight in elective surgery. They’re also the place practices most often expose patient information by accident.

A Texas example: Elite Dental Associates in Dallas paid $10,000 to settle with the HHS Office for Civil Rights after replies to Yelp reviews disclosed patients’ last names, treatment details and insurance and cost information. A plastic surgery practice replying “we’re sorry you’re unhappy with your breast revision, but as we discussed at your two-week post-op…” is making the same mistake.

The FTC also regulates how you collect reviews. the FTC’s consumer reviews rule, effective October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, buying reviews conditioned on a positive sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews and most review suppression, with civil penalties of up to $51,744 per violation. Our post on the FTC fake reviews rule explains it in plain English, and the Google reviews guide has request scripts.

A request that works: a text from the patient coordinator at the three-month visit, when the patient is happy with how things are settling. “We loved having you as a patient. If you’re comfortable sharing your experience, here’s the link. No pressure at all.” Send it to every patient, not only the happy ones.

A first 90 days for a Houston plastic surgery practice

Month one. Review every credential claim, testimonial and gallery photo against TMB rules and your consent forms. Pick two or three signature procedures. Build or rebuild those procedure pages with price ranges, financing and a booking button. Audit your tracking for pixels on booking and intake pages.

Month two. Launch procedure-specific Google search campaigns with a hard negative list. Start Meta lead ads with qualifying questions. Set a DM response standard for Instagram. Start the three-month review request.

Month three. Report cost per consult attended and cost per surgery booked by channel. Cut what doesn’t book surgeries, even if it produces cheap leads. Feed booked surgeries back to the platforms.

Frequently asked questions

How do plastic surgeons get more patients?

Pick a few signature procedures and build the marketing around them: a strong gallery with proper marketing consent, procedure pages with price ranges and financing, procedure-specific Google search ads, Instagram content featuring the surgeon, and fast replies to calls and DMs. Then track every lead through consult to surgery booked, so budget goes to the channels that produce surgeries rather than cheap leads.

Can a plastic surgeon advertise as board certified in Texas?

Under Texas Medical Board Rule 164.4, a physician can advertise board certification from an ABMS member board, an AOA Bureau of Osteopathic Specialists member board, the American Board of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, or a board the TMB finds substantially equivalent, with a grandfather clause for some older certifications. The American Board of Plastic Surgery is an ABMS member. Board eligible wording is prohibited.

Do I need patient consent to post before and after photos?

Yes. Patient photos are protected health information, and HIPAA generally requires written authorization before PHI is used for marketing. Use a separate marketing authorization that names where the photos will appear, whether the face will show and how to revoke consent. Don’t retouch results, keep lighting and angles consistent, and show typical outcomes rather than only your best case.

How should a plastic surgeon respond to a negative review?

Reply without confirming the person was a patient or mentioning any treatment details. Thank them, say you take feedback seriously, and invite them to call the practice manager directly. A Dallas dental practice paid HHS $10,000 after review replies disclosed patient names, treatment and cost details. Never offer incentives tied to positive reviews, which the FTC’s 2024 reviews rule prohibits.

Can cosmetic surgery practices run Facebook and Instagram ads?

Yes, with limits. Meta requires cosmetic procedure ads to exclude people under 18 and prohibits ads that create negative self-perception or imply knowledge of someone’s medical conditions. Meta also restricts some conversion tracking for health and wellness advertisers. Lead ads with qualifying questions, plus offline conversion uploads of booked consults that contain no health details, usually work best.
