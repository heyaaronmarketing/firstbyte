Thursday, late May, 2:15 p.m. Your tech is at stop 11 of 16. The next pool is 38 minutes away in Magnolia because a customer signed up from a Facebook ad last month, and the one after that is back in Spring. Meanwhile the office has three voicemails from new homeowners in Cypress with green pools, and a weekly customer in Sterling Ridge just canceled because they’re moving.

That afternoon is the whole business. Pool service runs on routes, and the marketing that matters most decides where your next customer lives.

Pool cleaning and service marketing in Houston and The Woodlands works when it fills the streets your trucks already drive, uses one-time jobs like green-pool cleanups to start weekly accounts, and keeps those accounts long enough to pay back what you spent to get them. Equipment repair is where the margin lives. Reviews and neighbors do more selling than ads.

Below is how we’d set that up for a service company working anywhere from Kingwood to Katy.

Route density is the marketing strategy

Every pool service owner knows drive time eats profit. Fewer of them run their marketing like they know it.

Skimmer’s guide to buying and selling routes puts density near the top of what makes a route valuable: pools close together with close service days cut travel time between stops. A customer three streets from your Tuesday cluster is worth more than the same customer 20 minutes off-route, even at the same monthly price. So stop targeting “Houston” in your ads.

What that looks like in practice:

  • Map your current customers by service day (see the example map below). Most routes have three to six clusters, often a subdivision or a few adjacent ones, like Creekside Park, a couple of Cypress neighborhoods off Fry Road, or a pocket of Kingwood.
  • Draw ad targeting as radius circles around those clusters, a mile or two each, instead of one big circle around your shop.
  • Set a “route-fill” offer only for those circles, such as a first month at [your price] for homes in neighborhoods you already service.
  • Quote off-route customers honestly, with a higher price or a waitlist. Some will still say yes, and those are profitable.

Door hangers still work for this, oddly well. When a tech finishes a pool, a few hangers on neighboring doors saying “we service a pool on your street every Tuesday” costs almost nothing and targets exactly the right houses.

The recurring revenue math behind every ad decision

A weekly customer isn’t a $160 sale. It’s a monthly payment that might last four or five years, plus repairs. Until you know that number, you can’t judge any ad.

The industry is in a confident mood. Skimmer’s 2026 State of Pool Service report, based on more than 1,600 pool pros, found more than 80% expect higher revenue in 2026, monthly billing remains the most common pricing model, and most companies plan increases under 10%. Your own numbers matter more than anyone’s survey, though.

Cancellations are the other half. The main reasons we hear are moving, price, and “the tech stopped showing up on the same day.” You can’t fix moving, but you can ask the departing customer to introduce you to the buyer. That’s a weekly account that literally comes with the house.

Green-pool cleanups, startups and other front-door offers

Nobody searches for “weekly pool service” with urgency. They search for it when something has gone wrong: a green pool, a new house, a bad experience with the last company.

Green-pool cleanups are the best front door in the business. The customer is motivated, the before and after photos sell themselves, and once the pool is clear the obvious next question is “who’s going to keep it this way?” Build a dedicated landing page for green-pool recovery with honest pricing ranges (“most cleanups run [your price] to [your price] depending on size and condition”), a short gallery, and a clear offer to credit part of the cleanup if they start weekly service within 30 days.

Other front doors worth a page and an ad group:

  • New homeowners. Houston relocations often land in houses with a pool the buyer has never maintained. A “new to pool ownership” visit that walks them through the equipment builds trust fast.
  • Foreclosures, rentals and listings. Agents and investors need pools cleaned before photos or showings, often in a hurry.
  • Spring startup. Many Houston pools run year-round, but plenty of owners let them slide over winter, and March through May brings a wave of “get it ready” calls.
  • Switchers. “Unhappy with your pool service?” is blunt and works, as long as you can actually promise the same day and the same tech each week.

Every cleanup or startup job should end with a written weekly-service offer and a text the next day. That handoff is where most companies lose the account.

Houston’s pool calendar: freeze nights, hurricane season and cleanups

Houston pools don’t close for winter the way northern pools do, so “opening season” is softer here. What we get instead are weather spikes.

Freeze nights come first. When forecasters call for a hard freeze, customers want to know whether to run the pump, cover the equipment or call you. A short freeze guide on your site, emailed and posted to your Business Profile the day a freeze watch is issued, earns trust and fills the week after with repair calls for cracked equipment. Anyone who lived through February 2021 reads that email.

Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30. When Beryl came ashore on July 8, 2024, CenterPoint was working on 2.26 million power outages, according to the Texas Tribune. Pumps sit off when the power is out, storms fill pools with leaves and limbs, and a Houston pool in July doesn’t stay clear for long without circulation. Storm cleanups are a real business line for a few weeks, and they’re a recruiting event for weekly accounts if you handle them well.

Weekly customers come first after a storm, and saying so in advance is good marketing. A line on your service page like “weekly customers get priority after storms” sells the plan to people who remember Beryl.

Licensing: what Texas requires, and what to say in your ads

Texas has no statewide license for pool cleaning or maintenance, and none for pool construction either, as PoolDial’s rundown of Texas pool licensing lays out. Anyone can start a route. That’s why so many homeowners have a story about a pool guy who disappeared.

Equipment work is a different matter. TDLR’s guidance on pool electrical devices says that if a pool system needs a new or upgraded line circuit, only a licensed electrical contractor with a designated master electrician can do that work. Holders of a residential appliance installer license can install and replace devices such as pumps, motors, heaters, lights, GFCIs and automation on the load side of the control center, and add devices like salt systems or variable-speed motors that don’t increase amperage or need the main panel. The same guidance says maintenance and repair by an authorized party uses components of the same type and ampacity as the originals. It’s dated 2019, so check with TDLR before you build a pitch around it. Gas lines and plumbing connections bring in other licenses, and commercial and HOA pools fall under public pool rules and local health departments.

For marketing, the rule is simple: say exactly what you hold. “Insured, with a TDLR-licensed residential appliance installer on staff for equipment work” is specific, checkable and better than a vague “licensed and insured.” If you don’t do electrical work, say who you bring in. Homeowners who’ve been burned before will notice the honesty.

Equipment repair upsells without being the pushy pool guy

The weekly visit pays the bills. Equipment is where a good year comes from: pumps, filters, salt cells, heaters, automation and the occasional leak hunt.

The trick is to make the upsell feel like information. Most service software sends a visit report with chemistry readings and a photo. Use it. A photo of a cracked pump lid or a salt cell scaled over, with a one-line note and a link to a quote, does the selling for you. Customers trust the tech they see every week. They don’t trust a cold call about a variable-speed pump.

Two cautions. Don’t make every report a sales pitch, or customers learn to ignore them. And price repairs clearly before the work, because a surprise equipment bill is the most common reason we see behind a one-star pool review in Houston.

Google Business Profile, Local Services Ads and Google Ads for pool service

For a route business, Google Maps visibility matters mostly in your clusters. Set your service area to the cities and ZIP codes you actually want, hide your home address if you don’t serve customers there, add photos of real trucks and real pools, and list services one by one: weekly pool cleaning, green pool cleanup, filter cleaning, salt cell cleaning, pool equipment repair, pump replacement.

Google’s Local Services Ads category list includes both “Pool cleaner” and “Pool contractor,” so service companies can run Local Services Ads in the right category. You pay per lead rather than per click, and you can dispute leads that weren’t real customers (our post on LSA disputes explains how). Set your LSA service area to the same clusters you target elsewhere, because LSA will happily send you a lead in Pearland when your route is in Tomball.

In Google Ads, split campaigns by intent:

  • Recurring service: weekly pool service The Woodlands, pool cleaning service near me, pool maintenance Spring TX.
  • Cleanups: green pool cleanup, pool cleaning after storm, algae pool cleaning Houston.
  • Repair: pool pump repair, pool heater repair Katy, salt cell replacement, pool leak detection.

Negatives on day one: pool supply, pool store, chlorine price, pool robot, robotic cleaner, Leslie’s, Home Depot, above ground (unless you service them), DIY, how to, YouTube, jobs, hiring, pool cleaner salary, CPO class, and builder terms like “pool installation cost” if you don’t build. Nextdoor, neighbors and reviews

Pool service is a neighborhood business, and Nextdoor is where neighborhoods ask for recommendations. “Who do you use for your pool?” threads in The Woodlands and Kingwood can run 60 comments deep. You can’t buy your way into those, but you can earn your way in by doing good work for people who post.

Paid Nextdoor ads are worth testing in route clusters, especially with an offer framed around the street (“two openings on our Thursday route in Alden Bridge”). Our Nextdoor advertising review covers what to expect and when to stop.

Reviews do the rest. Ask every weekly customer for a review around their third visit, once they’ve seen consistency, and ask every cleanup customer the day the pool turns blue. Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps bans offering incentives for reviews, discouraging negative reviews and selectively asking only happy customers, so send the same link to everyone. Reply to every review with specifics. Our Google reviews guide has request scripts you can copy.

A sample review text, for illustration: “Hi Dana, it’s Marcus with [company]. Your pool’s been on our Tuesday route a month now. If you have a minute, a Google review helps us a lot: [link]. Thanks for having us.”

Buying routes vs. building them

Sometimes the fastest way to add density is to buy it. Routes trade on what Skimmer’s guide to buying and selling routes describes as a sliding scale based on a multiple of monthly service revenue, with denser, cleaner routes commanding a higher multiple.

From a marketing view, a purchased route is a retention problem on day one. Customers didn’t choose you. A good transition looks like this: the seller introduces you by letter or email before the switch, your tech is on the same day of the week, the first three visit reports are extra thorough with photos, and you ask nobody for anything in the first month. After that, the normal review and repair flow kicks in.

When you compare buying with building, use the lifetime value table above. If building a weekly account through ads and cleanups costs you $400 and buying one costs more than that once you account for the customers who leave after the sale, build. If the route fills a hole in your existing clusters, the math often flips.

Pool builders, realtors and the referral network

New pools need someone to take over after startup, and many builders don’t want the service calls. A relationship with two or three pool builders in your area can be worth a steady stream of new weekly accounts each spring. Our pool builder marketing post covers how builders market themselves; from your side, the pitch is a clean handoff, a startup visit and no stealing their remodel work.

Realtors are the other referral source. A one-page “pool inspection before listing” offer for agents in The Woodlands, Spring and Cypress turns into service accounts with the buyers. Home inspectors often recommend a pool specialist too, so introduce yourself to the ones who work your area.

What to track, and a first 90 days

Track three numbers above everything else: new weekly accounts by source, cancellations by reason, and average drive minutes between stops. If marketing adds accounts and drive time goes up, you’re growing the wrong way.

  • Days 1 to 30. Map customers by service day. Rebuild Google Ads and LSA targeting around those clusters. Build pages for weekly service, green-pool cleanup and equipment repair.
  • Days 31 to 60. Start the review request flow. Launch a door hanger routine for techs. Test a Nextdoor route-fill offer in your densest cluster.
  • Days 61 to 90. Write a storm and freeze page and keep it ready. Visit two pool builders and five realtors. Review cancellations and fix the top reason.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a license to clean pools in Texas?

Texas has no statewide license for pool cleaning or maintenance, so anyone can start a route. Equipment work is different. TDLR guidance says new or upgraded electrical circuits need a licensed electrical contractor, and residential appliance installer license holders can replace pool devices like pumps and heaters within limits. Commercial and HOA pools also fall under public pool rules and local health departments.

How do pool service companies get more customers in Houston and The Woodlands?

Fill the neighborhoods your routes already cover. Target ads around existing service clusters, use green-pool cleanups and startups as front-door offers that convert into weekly service, ask every customer for a Google review, and build referrals with pool builders and realtors. Nextdoor and door hangers near current stops are cheap ways to add density without adding drive time.

Is there a Google Local Services Ads category for pool cleaning?

Yes. Google's Local Services Ads category list includes Pool cleaner and Pool contractor. You pay per lead instead of per click and can dispute leads that weren't real customers. Set the service area to match your route clusters in Houston, The Woodlands and nearby suburbs, because LSA can send leads anywhere in a broad area if you let it.

How much should I spend to get a new weekly pool customer?

Work it out from lifetime value. Multiply your monthly price by your service margin and average months a customer stays, then add repair margin. If that comes to a few thousand dollars, spending a few hundred to land a weekly account is reasonable. Track cost per new weekly account by source rather than cost per lead, since many cleanup leads never convert.

Should pool companies market storm cleanups after hurricanes?

Yes, carefully. After a storm like Beryl, many Houston pools sat without power for days and filled with debris. Have a storm cleanup page ready before hurricane season, serve weekly customers first, and post updates on your Business Profile and social pages. Price cleanups clearly and offer a credit toward weekly service, since storm customers can become long-term accounts.
