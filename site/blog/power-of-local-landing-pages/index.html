The Power of Local Landing Pages: How to Build Service-Area Pages That Rank in Houston and The Woodlands

How to build local landing pages for every Houston and The Woodlands service area: which cities to pick, what each page needs, and how to avoid doorway pages.

One page can’t rank in Katy, Conroe and Pearland at the same time. Those towns are 60 miles apart, and Google treats “water heater repair Katy” and “water heater repair Conroe” as two different questions from two different people.

Most service businesses in Greater Houston still try anyway. The homepage says “proudly serving Houston and surrounding areas,” the footer lists 30 towns in gray 11-point type, and the business wonders why it only shows up near its own shop.

Short answer: a local landing page is a page built for one service area, with proof you actually work there: jobs, reviews, photos, prices and local details. Build them only for towns you serve and want more of, make each one useful on its own, and link them from a service-area hub. Thin copies with the city name swapped are what Google calls doorway pages.

This guide walks through it in order: whether city pages still work, how to pick the towns, what goes on each page, how they connect to your Google Business Profile and your ads, and a 90-day rollout.

Do local landing pages still work in 2026?

Yes, when they’re good. Google never banned location pages. It bans a specific bad version of them.

Google’s spam policies define doorway abuse as sites or pages “created to rank for specific, similar search queries” that lead users to intermediate pages “that aren’t as useful as the final destination.” One of the listed examples is having “multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page.” Another is “creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy.”

Read that twice and you have the whole rulebook. A Katy page that’s the Cypress page with “Katy” pasted in, and that exists only to push people to a contact form, fits the definition. A Katy page that shows six jobs you did in Cinco Ranch and Elyson, explains how permits work out there and quotes a Katy customer does not.

The penalty risk got sharper in 2024. In Google’s March 2024 announcement on March 5, 2024, Google said its update and the expanded scaled content abuse policy would cut low-quality, unoriginal content in results by 40%; by April 26 it said the rollout had delivered a 45% reduction. The policy covers content produced at scale to manipulate rankings “whether automation, humans or a combination are involved.” Generating 80 city pages with an AI tool in an afternoon is the textbook case.

So the question isn’t really “do city pages work.” It’s whether you have enough real material for each town to justify a page. For most Houston service businesses that’s somewhere between three and fifteen towns, never 80.

Which Houston-area cities deserve their own page?

Start from where the money is, then check Google agrees there’s demand. We score candidate towns on five things before we write a word.

Growth is uneven around Houston, and it shows up in the data. The Census Bureau’s Vintage 2025 city estimates put Fulshear at 64,630 people in 2025, up 21.0% in a single year, while the City of Houston added 11,515 to reach 2,397,315. A 21% town on the west side and a mature inner-loop neighborhood are different bets.

Houston geography has a few quirks that change which page names make sense.

  • The Woodlands isn’t a city. It’s a township with eight villages per The Woodlands Township’s village list, and Creekside Park sits across Spring Creek in Harris County. People search “The Woodlands” far more than “Alden Bridge,” so one strong Woodlands page usually beats eight village pages.
  • “Spring” and “Cypress” are mostly mailing addresses. Huge unincorporated areas with no city hall. People search them by name constantly, so they deserve pages even though there’s no municipal anything to write about.
  • Some neighborhoods are searched like towns. Kingwood is part of the City of Houston, but people search “Kingwood” on its own, and “The Heights” and “Memorial” behave the same way. Check the keyword data before you assume a city name is what people type.
  • Permits split along city lines. A fence or patio job inside the City of Houston goes through the city’s permitting center, unincorporated Harris County through the county engineer, and Conroe or Montgomery through their own offices. That’s real, page-worthy local detail for contractors.

What goes on a local landing page that actually ranks

Here’s the anatomy we build to. Not every page needs every block, but a page with fewer than half of them usually isn’t ready.

A few of these deserve more explanation, because they’re where most city pages fall flat.

Local jobs with photos. “Recent work in Katy” with three to six projects, each with a neighborhood or cross street, what the problem was, and a real photo. Not stock, and not the same three photos on every page. This is the single block that makes a page impossible to copy.

Reviews from that town. If your review platform lets you filter by location, pull two or three from customers there. A Pearland homeowner trusts a Pearland review more than one from Tomball.

Local conditions. This is where you show you’ve been there. Expansive clay soil and foundation movement in parts of Katy and Cypress. Pine pollen and tree canopy in The Woodlands. Older cast-iron plumbing in pre-1980 homes in Spring Branch. Flood-prone streets near Cypress Creek. HOA approval for exterior work in master-planned communities like Bridgeland or Woodforest. Only write what’s true for your trade.

Drive time and scheduling. “We’re based off Research Forest, so we’re usually 20 to 30 minutes from Magnolia. Same-day slots open most weekdays.” Customers want this, and it’s a detail nobody fakes well.

Pricing context. If trip charges or prices differ by area, say so. If they don’t, say that too. A page that answers the price question gets more calls, as we argue in website copy that sells.

Google publishes no word count, and we’d ignore anyone who quotes you one. A tight 500-word page with eight real job photos beats a 1,500-word page of filler about Katy’s history.

That’s honest, it’s useful to the visitor, and it keeps you clear of the “substantially similar pages” language in the same spam policy page.

Reusing some text is fine. Your warranty terms, your licensing, your process: those can be the same everywhere, and rewriting them five ways to look unique is silly. The test is whether the parts that should differ actually do.

How city pages work with your Google Business Profile

Your website’s city pages and your Google Business Profile do different jobs. The profile decides whether you appear in the map pack. The pages help you rank in the regular results below it, give the map-pack click somewhere better than the homepage to land, and back up the profile’s claim that you serve those towns.

Three rules from Google that trip up Houston businesses:

  • Service areas. You can list up to 20 on your profile, there’s no radius option, and the area “shouldn’t be more than about 2 hours of driving time” from your base, per Google’s service area help page. Match your city pages to those areas.
  • No fake offices. Per Google’s guidelines for representing your business, a rented address you don’t operate from isn’t eligible, and a coworking office needs clear signage and staff during business hours. A virtual office in Sugar Land does not get you a Sugar Land map listing. It gets you suspended.
  • Hide the address if customers don’t come to you. Service-area businesses should hide it. Hybrid businesses with a staffed storefront can show it and set service areas too.

If you do have real staffed locations, each one gets its own profile, and each profile’s website link should go to that location’s page, not the homepage. Our multi-location local SEO guide covers that setup, and the Google Business Profile guide covers the profile itself.

Using local landing pages for Google Ads

City pages pay for themselves twice if you run search ads. Google’s Quality Score has three components, per Google’s Quality Score help page: expected clickthrough rate, ad relevance and landing page experience, described as “how relevant and useful your landing page is to people who click your ad.”

So an ad group for “plumber Katy” and “Katy plumbing repair” should land on the Katy page, with an ad headline that says Katy. A headline promising “Katy plumbers” that lands on a homepage talking about The Woodlands is a small trust break, and those add up in conversion rate.

Two cautions. Don’t build city pages only for ads and block them from search with noindex unless they’re truly duplicates; a good page should do both jobs. And watch your location settings: “Presence: people in or regularly in your included locations” keeps a Katy campaign from paying for clicks from people in Ohio researching a move. URLs, internal links and schema for service-area pages

The structure matters more than people expect, because it’s how Google tells a browseable hierarchy from a pile of doorways.

ElementWhat we doWhat we avoid
URL/service-areas/katy-tx/ or /drain-cleaning/katy-tx/ for service-plus-city pages/best-cheap-plumber-katy-tx-77494/
Hub pageOne “Areas we serve” page linking to every city page, with a sentence or two on each townCity links only in a stuffed footer
Internal linksService pages link to the city pages where that service is common; city pages link back to service pagesEvery city page linking to every other city page
Title tag“Drain Cleaning in Katy, TX | Company Name”Five town names crammed into one title
SchemaLocalBusiness markup on real locations; areaServed listing the towns you coverA fake street address for each city in the markup

Google’s LocalBusiness structured data docs cover the LocalBusiness properties Google reads. Markup should describe what’s true. If there’s no office in Pearland, the Pearland page shouldn’t carry markup that implies one.

On service-plus-city combinations (drain cleaning Katy, water heaters Katy, slab leaks Katy), start with one city page per town. Add combination pages only where a service is big enough in that town to have its own jobs and reviews. Otherwise you multiply thin pages, which is how a 12-page site becomes a 300-page site Google doesn’t trust.

How long until a new city page ranks?

In the sites we’ve worked on, a new city page usually shows up in Search Console impressions within a few weeks and takes three to six months to settle into steady rankings. Towns where you already have reviews, jobs and some brand searches move faster. Towns where you’re a stranger take longer, and some never crack page one against companies based there.

Treat that curve as illustrative. What we’d actually watch in Search Console, per page: impressions for queries containing the town name, average position for your top two service terms plus the town, and clicks. Then in your call tracking or CRM, leads with that town’s ZIPs. A page with rising impressions and no leads has a conversion problem, not an SEO problem. Mistakes we see on Houston service-area sites

Most of these come from a template someone bought, or from an SEO package that charged per page.

  • Forty city pages and two of them have ever produced a call.
  • Pages for towns the business won’t actually drive to. A customer in League City books, the tech declines the job, and now you have a one-star review from League City.
  • Stock photos of the Houston skyline on the Magnolia page.
  • Copy that mentions the town name 14 times and says nothing about the town.
  • No way to reach the pages except the sitemap. If your own site doesn’t link to a page, don’t expect Google to care about it.
  • City pages built once in 2021 and never touched. Add new jobs and reviews every quarter.

The fix for almost all of them is fewer pages, each one better, linked from a real hub.

Frequently asked questions

How many local landing pages should a Houston service business have?

Only as many as you can fill with real local material. For most single-location service businesses around Houston and The Woodlands that means three to fifteen towns where you’ve done paid jobs, see search demand and can drive profitably. Start with your best three to five, add one or two a quarter, and list the rest on an areas-we-serve hub page instead of giving each a thin page.

Are city pages considered doorway pages by Google?

Not automatically. Google’s spam policy targets pages created to rank for similar queries that funnel visitors elsewhere, including substantially similar pages aimed at different cities. A city page with local jobs, photos, reviews, drive times and conditions specific to that town is a useful page. A copy of another city page with the name swapped is the kind Google describes as doorway abuse.

Can I get a Google Business Profile in every city I serve?

No. A Business Profile needs a real location where you operate, and Google says virtual offices are not eligible. Coworking spaces only qualify with clear signage and staff during business hours. Service-area businesses should list one profile, hide the address if customers don’t visit, and add up to 20 service areas. City landing pages on your website support those areas.

Should I use AI to write local landing pages?

AI can help with outlines and editing, but it can’t supply the parts that make a city page rank: your jobs, photos, reviews and local knowledge. Google’s scaled content abuse policy applies whether content is made by automation, people or both, and it targets mass-produced pages with little value. Use your own job records as the raw material and keep each page specific.

Do local landing pages help with Google Ads in Houston?

Yes. Landing page experience is one of the three parts of Google Ads Quality Score, so sending a Katy ad group to a page about Katy usually beats sending it to your homepage. The page should repeat the town and service from the ad, show local proof, and make calling easy. Use presence-based location targeting so you only pay for people actually in that area.
