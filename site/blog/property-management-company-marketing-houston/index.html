Property Management Company Marketing in Houston: How to Win More Doors

Property management marketing for Houston: winning owner clients with TREC-compliant sites, suburb SEO, Google Ads, reviews, referrals and cost per new door.

Tuesday afternoon. An owner calls from Denver. Her company moved her there last spring, she couldn’t get her price for the house in Cypress, so she rented it out herself. Now the tenant has left, the house has sat empty for three weeks, the AC quit in the heat, and she’s Googling “property management Cypress TX” from her desk.

She finds three companies. The first has a 3.4-star rating, mostly from tenants angry about deposits. The second has no prices anywhere on the site. The third shows its fees, has a page about Cypress rents and HOA leasing rules, and a 4.7 rating with reviews from owners. She calls the third one.

That’s property management marketing in one search. Tenants are not your customer here. Owners are, and every new owner adds doors under management that pay you every month for years.

To win more property management clients in Houston, build a site and Google profile that speak to owners (with TREC’s required links and broker name), rank for “property management [suburb]” with real local pages, run search ads on owner-intent keywords only, manage the tenant-review problem honestly, and grow referral partners who meet owners before you do. Measure all of it in cost per new door.

Who your owners are, and what they’re searching

Houston keeps producing landlords. HAR counted 47,292 leases in 2025, up 6.2% from 2024, per HAR’s full-year 2025 rental report. Community Impact reported the average number of single-family homes leased per month rose from 3,592 in 2023 to 3,945 in 2025, while average rent went from $2,236 to $2,319. In July 2026, single-family leases were up 12.1% from a year earlier at 5,185, with 9,832 rentals available and a typical 37 days on market, per HAR’s July 2026 numbers.

More inventory and longer days on market are good news for your marketing. Owners who are getting nervous about a vacant house start looking for help.

Most of the small companies we see in Houston make the same mistake: the homepage is written for tenants (“Find your next home!”) because that’s where the listings feed lives. Put the listings on their own tab. Make the homepage about owners.

TREC rules come first: licensing and what your marketing must show

In Texas, most residential property management is a licensed activity. TREC’s licensing FAQ says a license is required if the manager’s duties include showing or leasing the property for pay, and also for anyone who controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family home. The on-site manager exemption covers apartment complexes only; condos and townhomes don’t qualify. In practice, a property management company that leases houses runs under a broker license, and that broker’s name has to appear in its advertising.

The advertising rules then shape your website and social accounts. Per TREC’s social media and advertising guide, Rule 535.155 requires the broker’s name on ads, at least half the size of the largest contact information. Rules 531.18 and 531.20 require links to the Consumer Protection Notice and the Information About Brokerage Services (IABS) form, which on social media can live on the profile or one direct click away. TREC’s forms page adds that the IABS link must be in a readily noticeable place on the homepage, labeled “Texas Real Estate Commission Information About Brokerage Services” in at least 10-point font.

The rules don’t make a site any less effective. A licensed broker name and the TREC links are trust signals for an owner who’s about to hand you a house worth $350,000. Put your license number on the About page and link it to TREC’s license lookup. Many of the cheap “we’ll manage your rental” operations on Facebook can’t do that.

SEO for “property management [suburb]” searches

Owners search with a place name. “Property management Katy.” “Property management company Sugar Land.” “Rental management The Woodlands.” “Property manager Pearland.” Each of those deserves a real page, and “real” is the whole trick.

A thin page that swaps the town name into the same 300 words won’t rank and won’t convince anyone. A strong suburb page includes:

  • How many doors you manage in that area, and which neighborhoods (Cinco Ranch, Bridgeland, Towne Lake, Shadow Creek Ranch, Sienna, Kingwood, Creekside Park).
  • Typical rent ranges for three- and four-bedroom homes there, from your own leases, with the date.
  • Local wrinkles: HOA leasing restrictions and registration rules, MUD and tax notes for owners, school district pull, distance to the Energy Corridor, the Medical Center or The Woodlands business district.
  • Your typical days to lease in that suburb last quarter.
  • Reviews from owners in that area, and a photo of a home you manage there (with the owner’s okay).

Then build the owner-question pages that pull in early-stage searches: “how much do property managers charge in Texas,” “rent or sell my house in Houston,” “what does a property manager do,” “how to evict a tenant in Texas.” That last one got more timely when Senate Bill 38 took effect January 1, 2026, changing eviction procedures and adding a faster route against squatters. An owner-facing explainer (with your attorney’s review) is exactly the content accidental landlords look for.

On Google Business Profile, choose Property management company as the primary category and add Real estate agency only if you also list homes for sale. List services in owner language: tenant placement, full-service management, leasing only, maintenance coordination, HOA compliance. Content that attracts owners: rent estimates, fee tables and real numbers

The single best lead magnet in property management is a rent estimate. An owner who types in an address and asks “what would this rent for?” is telling you they’re thinking about it.

Done well, it’s a short form (address, beds, baths, square footage, condition) and a human-reviewed estimate within one business day, sent with three comparable leases and a note on how long similar homes took to lease. Done badly, it’s an instant automated number that’s $300 too high, which sets up a vacancy and an unhappy owner. We’d skip the instant number. The one-day turnaround is a reason to talk.

Other content that earns its keep:

  • A fee table. Monthly management percentage, leasing fee, renewal fee, any markup on maintenance, what happens when a house sits vacant. Owners compare companies on this. Hiding it sends them to the competitor who doesn’t.
  • A sample owner statement and a sample inspection report, with personal details removed.
  • A quarterly Houston rent report built from your own leases plus HAR’s public numbers. HAR put the average single-family rent at $2,245 in December 2025 and $2,419 in July 2026. Local agents and reporters link to this kind of page.
  • A rent-or-sell calculator for accidental landlords, with honest assumptions about vacancy, repairs, management fees and taxes.
  • Storm and freeze pages. What you do for owners before and after a hurricane or a hard freeze. In Houston, owners remember who called them after the last storm.

Google Ads for owner leads, not tenant clicks

The biggest waste we see in property management Google Ads is paying for tenants. Searches like “houses for rent Katy” have huge volume and cheap-looking clicks, and every one of them is a person looking for a home, not an owner looking for a manager.

Keep the account to owner intent:

  • Suburb campaigns: “property management [town],” “property management company [town],” “rental property manager [town].”
  • Service campaigns: “tenant placement service Houston,” “leasing only property management,” “single family property management Houston.”
  • Situation campaigns: “rent my house,” “I need someone to manage my rental,” “property management for out of state owners.”
  • Brand, so a review site or competitor can’t sit on your name.

Negatives on day one: for rent, rentals near me, apartments, houses for rent, rent to own, section 8 housing (unless you’re targeting owners who accept vouchers, with separate copy), low income, jobs, careers, salary, certification, course, license class, software, app, Buildium, AppFolio, HOA management (unless you offer it), commercial (unless you offer it), and “near me” tenant phrases like “cheap houses.”

On policy: Google’s housing rules restrict targeting by age, gender, parental status, marital status and ZIP code for housing ads, but Google’s housing ads policy FAQ lists property management services among ads that may not have targeting restricted. Single-home listings and real estate brokers are on the restricted list. So an owner-acquisition campaign for your management services generally isn’t limited, while an ad for a vacant rental is. Keep them in separate campaigns.

Sample ad copy:

  • Headline: “Katy Property Management”
  • Headline: “Leased in 30 Days or Less, Avg.” (only if your data proves it)
  • Headline: “See Our Fees, No Surprises”
  • Description: “Licensed Texas broker managing [number] homes in Katy and Fulshear. Free rent estimate in one business day.”

Owner-intent clicks cost more than tenant clicks, and there are fewer of them. That’s fine. One owner can be worth years of fees. Our Houston CPC benchmarks give a sense of click costs, and our search team builds these accounts.

Facebook and Instagram: housing rules apply to your listings, not your owner ads

Meta’s Housing special ad category covers ads that promote a housing opportunity, including listings for the sale or rental of a home or apartment, per Jon Loomer’s guide to Meta’s special ad categories. When you advertise a vacant rental, the category is required, and it brings the restrictions: age locked to 18 to 65+, all genders, at least a 15-mile radius, no ZIP code targeting, no lookalikes and limited detailed targeting.

Ads aimed at owners (“Own a rental in Cypress? See what we’d charge to manage it”) don’t promote a housing opportunity to the viewer, so they generally sit outside the category. Meta’s list isn’t exhaustive, though, and reviewers sometimes flag anything with a house photo. If an owner ad gets rejected, run it under Housing rather than fighting it. Owners are spread across every age and gender anyway, so you lose little.

What tends to work on Meta for owner acquisition:

  • Retargeting people who visited your fee page or rent estimate page.
  • Short videos of a real make-ready or inspection with the manager talking to camera.
  • Lead forms with qualifying questions: how many properties, where, currently rented or vacant, currently managed or self-managed.

Those questions cut volume and raise quality. Our guide to fixing bad Facebook leads explains why. Whatever you run for tenants, keep Fair Housing in the copy: describe the home, never the kind of renter you want.

Reviews: when tenants drag your rating down

Here’s the uncomfortable math of property management reviews. You have two customer groups. Owners pay you and rarely review. Tenants don’t pay you, interact with you mostly when something’s broken or a deposit is withheld, and review often. A company doing its job well (enforcing leases, charging for real damage) can still end up at 3.5 stars.

What you can’t do, per Google’s prohibited content policy for Maps: offer incentives for reviews, discourage negative reviews, selectively ask only happy people, or post reviews from staff, family or anyone with a conflict of interest. Google also tightened its language in April 2026 against review quotas and asking for specific content, per PPC Land. And you can’t get a review removed just because it’s from a tenant. Tenants are legitimate reviewers. Only policy violations, like harassment or off-topic content, are removable.

What you can do:

On replies, two Houston-specific cautions. Never disclose a tenant’s balance, eviction status or personal details in a public reply. And keep replies free of anything that could read as a judgment about a protected class. A good reply sounds like: “We’re sorry the move-out felt unfair. Deposit deductions are itemized with photos, and we’re glad to walk through them. Please call our office at [number].”

Owners reading your profile know tenants complain. What they’re watching is how you respond and whether owner reviews say you protect their asset. Our guide to Google reviews has request templates.

Referral partners who meet owners before you do

Many of the best owner leads never search at all. Someone they trust hands them your name.

  • Real estate agents. Agents meet accidental landlords constantly: sellers who can’t get their price, buyers keeping their old house. Many agents don’t want to manage rentals. Offer to send the owner back to them when it’s time to sell, and keep that promise. Our post on how local agents market themselves shows their side.
  • Relocation and HR contacts. Transferees leaving Houston often keep the house. Corporate relocation teams appreciate a manager they can name.
  • Estate and probate attorneys, CPAs and financial planners. Inherited houses and tax questions both lead to rental decisions.
  • Investor groups and lenders who finance rental purchases.
  • Contractors and make-ready vendors who hear owners complain about their current manager.

Check with your broker of record before paying anyone for a referral; TREC rules on compensation apply. A thank-you lunch and sending business back usually work better than a check anyway.

Measuring cost per new door

A door is worth far more than a lead. Say a home rents for $2,300 and your management fee is 8% (illustrative; use your own fee). That’s about $184 a month, roughly $2,200 a year before leasing and renewal fees, and an owner who stays five years is worth well over $10,000 in revenue. Spending $800 or $1,000 to win that door can be a great deal. Most companies never calculate it, so they cut the channels that work because the cost per lead looks high.

To measure it:

  1. Track owner leads separately from tenant inquiries, with tracked numbers per channel and forms tagged by source. Our call and form tracking guide covers setup.
  2. Count signed management agreements, and the number of doors each brings (an investor with four houses is four doors).
  3. Divide spend by new doors, per channel, by quarter.
  4. Track door churn. If you win 30 doors a quarter and lose 20, the problem isn’t marketing.

Frequently asked questions

How do property management companies get new clients?

Most new owner clients come from Google searches for property management plus a suburb, referrals from real estate agents and other professionals, and the company’s reviews and website. The companies that grow fastest publish their fees, offer a human-reviewed rent estimate, build a real page for each suburb they serve, and run search ads only on owner-intent keywords instead of tenant searches.

Do you need a license to be a property manager in Texas?

Usually. TREC says a license is required when a property manager shows or leases property for an owner for pay, and for anyone who controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family home. On-site managers of apartment complexes are exempt, but that exemption doesn’t cover condos or townhomes. Property management companies typically operate under a licensed broker.

Do Meta housing ad rules apply to property management ads?

They apply to ads for rental listings, which must run in Meta’s Housing special ad category with age, gender, ZIP code and radius restrictions. Ads aimed at owners about your management services generally don’t promote a housing opportunity, though Meta reviewers sometimes flag them. On Google, property management services are on the list of ads that may not have targeting restricted, while single-home listings are.

How can a property management company deal with bad tenant reviews?

You can’t remove reviews just because they’re from tenants, and you can’t offer incentives or ask only happy people. Instead, ask every owner for a review, ask tenants at good moments like move-in or a fast repair, reply professionally without disclosing private details, and fix the causes, such as unclear deposit deductions or slow maintenance. Report only reviews that actually break Google’s policies.

How much should a property management company spend to get a new door?

It depends on your fees and how long owners stay. A single-family door renting near the Houston average can bring in a couple thousand dollars a year in management fees, so spending several hundred dollars to win it can pay back within months. Track spend, signed agreements and doors by channel each quarter, and watch churn, since losing doors erases acquisition gains.
