Signs Your Marketing Agency Isn’t Working (and What to Do About It)

Signs your marketing agency isn’t working: vanity metrics, no account access, stale reports, vague spend. A 30-minute self-check and how to switch cleanly.

Monday morning at an HVAC company in Katy. The agency report landed over the weekend: impressions up 18%, click-through rate “strong,” a green arrow next to “conversions.” The owner scrolls past it and looks at the dispatch board. Same number of booked calls as last June, in a hotter June, with more spent on ads than ever.

He’s not sure if the agency is bad, or if the market is just tough, or if he’s reading the report wrong. That uncertainty is the real problem. He can’t judge the work, so he either keeps paying or fires them on a hunch.

The signs your marketing agency isn’t working are usually these: reports built on impressions and clicks instead of leads and revenue, no access to your own accounts, reports that look the same every month, a new junior account manager every quarter, vague answers about where the money went, and no evidence of testing. Most of those you can confirm yourself in about 30 minutes, inside Google Ads and GA4, without anyone’s permission.

We’re an agency, so take this with whatever salt you like. We’ve also inherited a lot of accounts from other agencies, and we’d rather owners knew how to check our work than trusted us blindly. Plenty of agencies do good work. Some relationships just drift, and nobody notices until the renewal.

First, give it a fair window

Not every slow month is the agency’s fault. Before you judge, check a few things on your own side.

  • How long has it been? A new Google Ads account usually needs a few weeks of data before the bidding settles. SEO takes months. Three months is a fair first look for ads; six for SEO.
  • Did anything change at your end? A new receptionist, a phone tree, slower callbacks, a price increase, a website edit that broke the form.
  • Did the season move? A pool company in Magnolia and a roofer after a hailstorm in Cypress have very different Januaries.
  • Are leads being answered? If calls go to voicemail at lunch, the ads can be perfect and still look broken. Our speed-to-lead guide covers this side.

If those check out and results are still flat after a fair window, it’s time to look at the agency’s side.

Sign 1: the report is all vanity metrics

Impressions, reach, clicks, click-through rate, “engagement,” follower growth, average position. These describe activity. Some are useful for diagnosing a campaign. None of them pays your payroll.

A report that’s working for you leads with the numbers you’d recognize from the business: calls over a minute, form fills, booked appointments, cost per lead, and ideally cost per job or sale once you share that data back. If the report has those and the vanity numbers underneath, great. If the vanity numbers are all there is, ask why.

Watch the word “conversions” too. In Google Ads, a conversion is whatever someone set it to be. Per Google’s phone call conversion guide, one type of call tracking, “clicks on a number on your mobile website,” records only the click on the phone number, not whether a call happened or how long it lasted. Page views, scroll depth or a click on a “Contact” button can also be set as conversions. Ask the agency to list every action counted as a conversion and which ones are primary. Our post on measuring marketing success has a fuller list of metrics worth tracking.

Sign 2: you don’t have access to your own accounts

If you can’t log into Google Ads, GA4, your Google Business Profile and your Meta ad account with your own email, you can’t check anything in this post. That alone is a sign, even if the work is good, because it means the relationship runs on trust only.

You want at least read-only access to everything, and Admin on the accounts you own. Per Google’s access level page, Read-only users can see campaigns, billing details and performance reports without being able to change anything, which is exactly what an owner needs for spot checks. We won’t repeat the whole ownership walkthrough here; our post on who owns your Google Ads account when you switch agencies goes account by account. If an agency resists giving you read-only access to your own ads, that’s about as clear a sign as you’ll get.

Sign 3: reports that never change

Open three monthly reports side by side. Same commentary with the dates swapped? Same “next steps” every month (“continue optimizing,” “monitor performance”)? Same campaigns, same ads, same landing pages for a year?

Markets move. Houston has hurricane season, a long summer, school calendars in Conroe and Cy-Fair, and competitors who change their bids every week. An account that’s actively managed changes too. Reports that don’t change usually mean the account doesn’t either.

You can confirm it in change history, which we cover in the 30-minute check below.

Sign 4: a new junior account manager every few months

Agencies have turnover. Ours included, occasionally. A new contact once a year is normal. A new contact every quarter, each one younger than the last and each one asking you to re-explain your business, is a pattern.

The cost isn’t just your time. Each handoff loses context: why a keyword was excluded, which service area doesn’t pay, that Tuesday calls are slow because your techs are on install jobs. The new person often “refreshes” the account to show activity, which resets what was working.

Ask who actually makes changes in the account. Change history shows the email address of every user who edits it, so you can see whether the senior person from the sales pitch has touched it at all. Ask what happens to your account when your contact leaves, and whether notes are kept. A good agency has an answer.

Sign 5: questions about spend get vague answers

“Where did the $7,000 go last month?” should have a straight answer within a day: which campaigns, which searches, what came back. Google’s advertiser guide to working with third parties says you have the right to know, at a minimum, the clicks, impressions and total cost of your Google ads, and it lists “not sharing the cost and performance of advertising campaigns” as a red flag.

The vague answers sound like this: “The algorithm is learning.” “Performance Max doesn’t really show that.” “Brand awareness is hard to measure.” Each can be partly true. None should be the whole answer every time. If the agency bills your ad spend, match its invoice to the billing summary inside Google Ads. They should match, or the difference should be a fee you agreed to in writing.

Sign 6: nothing is ever tested

Good accounts are always testing something: a new ad message, a different landing page, a bid strategy, a new offer. Google gives agencies a built-in way to do this. The Experiments page sits in the left menu of Google Ads, and per Google’s Experiments page documentation, it supports ad variations, custom experiments for bidding, match types and landing pages, Performance Max experiments and more.

An empty Experiments page isn’t proof of neglect. Plenty of good managers test by rotating ads or splitting campaigns. But ask: “What did we test in the last 90 days, and what did we learn?” If there’s no answer, there was probably no test.

Related tell: lots of changes made by “Google Ads system” in change history. Google can apply recommendations automatically when someone opts in, and per Google’s page on auto-applied recommendations, applied recommendations show on the History tab and in change history. Some auto-applied items are fine. An account run mostly on auto-apply is an account nobody is steering, and you’re paying a management fee for it.

Run your own 30-minute check

You don’t need to be an expert to do this. You need your own login and half an hour. Do it on a quiet afternoon with a notepad.

Minutes 1-8: Google Ads change history

In Google Ads, open Change history from the Campaigns menu. Set the date range to the last 90 days. Per Google’s change history page, it covers up to two years, shows the email address of whoever made each change in the User column, and lets you filter by change type and campaign.

What to look for: are there changes every week or two? Who’s making them, a person or “Google Ads system”? Are they meaningful (new negative keywords, ad tests, budget shifts toward what works) or cosmetic? Twelve changes in 90 days on a $10,000-a-month account would worry us.

Minutes 9-16: the search terms report

Go to Campaigns, then Insights and reports, then Search terms. This is the list of what people actually typed before they clicked your ad. Google’s search terms report documentation notes some low-volume terms are left out for privacy, so you won’t see everything.

Sort by cost. Read the top 50. You’re looking for money spent on searches you’d never want: job seekers (“HVAC technician jobs Houston”), DIY (“how to recharge AC”), competitors you can’t serve, towns outside your area, wrong services. A few are normal. Pages of them, months in, mean nobody is adding negative keywords. This is the single fastest test of whether a search account is managed. Our post on Google Ads clicks with no calls covers what to do with what you find.

Minutes 17-24: GA4 key events

In GA4, go to Admin, then Data display, then Events, and look at the Key events tab. Key events are what GA4 used to call conversions; Search Engine Roundtable reported the rename on March 22, 2024, and Google’s events and key events page explains that you then create Google Ads conversions from the key events that matter. Standard properties allow up to 30 key events, per Google’s key events help page.

Check that each one is a real business action (form submitted, call, booking confirmed), not page_view or scroll. Then look at the counts over the last month. Do they look like reality? If GA4 says 140 form leads and your inbox got 31, something is double-counting.

Minutes 25-30: call tracking

Call the number in your ads and on your website from your cell. Does it ring through? Does it show up in the call tracking or Google Ads call report within the hour? Then pull last month’s call log and listen to five recordings. Are they customers, or spam and vendors? Check the minimum call length your conversion is set to count; per Google’s phone call conversion guide, that’s a number the advertiser sets, and a very short one counts hang-ups.

When you’re done, write down what you found in four lines. That page is the start of the conversation.

How to have the conversation

Go in with specifics and a fair tone. The goal is to find out whether the agency can fix it, not to win an argument. Some of the best client relationships we have started with a hard call like this.

  • Lead with numbers. “We spent $21,000 over three months and booked 38 jobs from ads. Last year we booked about the same with less. I want to understand why.”
  • Bring the evidence. “There are $1,900 of clicks on job-seeker searches in the search terms report.” “Change history shows six changes since August.”
  • Ask for a plan, in writing, with dates. What will change in the next 30 days, and what result should you expect to see by day 60?
  • Ask who will do it. Name the person.
  • Agree on how you’ll judge it. Booked jobs or qualified leads, by source, at a cost you can live with.

Then give it the 30 to 60 days. A good agency will come back with changes you can see in change history within a week. If the reply is defensive, or the plan is “keep optimizing,” you have your answer.

How to switch cleanly

If you decide to move, the order matters more than the speed. Most messy exits come from cancelling first and asking for access second.

What “working” looks like

To be fair to agencies, here’s what a healthy relationship looks like from our side of the table. You can see every account. The report leads with leads and cost per lead, and it changes because the account changes. You know the name of the person running your ads, and they’ve been there a while. Spend questions get a straight answer in a day. Something is being tested most months, and you hear what was learned, including the tests that lost.

Results still go up and down. Houston’s summers, storms and school calendars see to that. But you understand why, and you trust the explanation because you can check it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my marketing agency is doing a good job?

Look at leads and revenue rather than impressions and clicks. A good agency gives you access to your own accounts, reports calls, forms and bookings with cost per lead, and makes regular changes you can see in Google Ads change history. Read your search terms report: if it is full of irrelevant searches months in, the account probably is not being managed closely.

How long should I give a new marketing agency before judging results?

For Google Ads, about three months is a fair first look, since accounts need a few weeks of data before bidding settles. SEO usually needs six months or more. In the meantime you can still judge the process: tracking set up and verified, regular changes in change history, clear answers about spend, and a written plan with dates.

What is Google Ads change history and how do I find it?

Change history is a log of every edit made to your Google Ads account over the past two years. Open it from the Campaigns menu in Google Ads. It shows what changed, when, and the email address of the user who made the change, and you can filter by date, change type and campaign. It is the quickest way to see whether an account is actively managed.

Should I tell my agency I am unhappy or just switch?

Usually tell them first, with specifics. Bring numbers, such as spend versus booked jobs, and evidence from the search terms report or change history, then ask for a written plan with dates. Give it 30 to 60 days. If changes show up quickly and results move, the relationship may be worth keeping. If the response is defensive or vague, start planning a clean switch.

How do I switch marketing agencies without losing my Google Ads data?

Keep the existing Google Ads account rather than starting a new one, because campaign history stays with the account. Before giving notice, confirm you hold Admin access, move billing to your own payments setup if the agency pays Google, and export anything held in the agency's tools. Ask that campaigns stay live during the notice period, then remove old users after the handover.
