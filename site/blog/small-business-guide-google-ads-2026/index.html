Google Ads for Small Businesses in Houston and The Woodlands: The 2026 Guide

A step-by-step 2026 Google Ads guide for Houston and The Woodlands small businesses: budgets, tracking, campaign types, keywords, negatives and targeting.

When we open a small business Google Ads account for the first time, the money is usually leaking in the same four places: no conversion tracking, ads showing to people outside the service area, broad keywords with no negatives, and every click landing on the homepage. Fix those four and most accounts improve before anyone touches the ad copy.

Short answer: Google Ads is worth it for most Houston small businesses that sell a service people search for. Start with a Search campaign on high-intent keywords, track calls and forms before spending, target only the area you serve, add negative keywords on day one, and plan on at least $1,500 to $3,000 a month for a fair test.

This guide walks through the setup in order, with the actual settings, updated for what changed in 2026: AI Max, the end of call-only ads and a quiet budget pacing change.

How much should a small business spend on Google Ads in Houston?

Start from leads, not from a round number. The median cost per lead across U.S. search campaigns was $66.69 in WordStream’s 2026 Google Ads benchmarks, which covered 13,474 campaigns from April 2025 to March 2026, with a median cost per click of $5.42 and a conversion rate of 8.18%. Service categories run higher: Home and Home Improvement at $8.33 a click and $90.92 a lead, Dentists at $8.00 and $72.97, and Attorneys at $9.87 and $131.63.

Simple math: if you want 25 leads a month and your category costs about $90 a lead, budget roughly $2,250 a month. Houston is a big, competitive market, so we wouldn’t assume you’ll beat the national median in month one. Our Houston cost-per-click benchmarks go category by category.

Two budget mechanics trip up new advertisers. Google can spend up to twice your average daily budget on a busy day, but over a month it won’t spend more than 30.4 times the daily budget, per Google’s budget help page. And after a pacing change Google made on June 1, 2026, campaigns that switch off whole days of the week now pace toward that full 30.4x monthly figure, even on the days they run. If you only advertise weekdays and set $100 a day, expect to spend around $3,040 a month, not $2,200. Lower the daily number to match the monthly total you want.

A budget that’s too small is its own problem. At $10 a day in a $9-a-click category, you get one click a day and learn nothing for months. Narrow the keywords and the area until the budget buys at least 10 to 15 clicks a day.

Step 1: Set up the account so you own it

New Google Ads accounts are steered toward a simplified “Smart campaign” setup. It’s easy and it gives you very little control over where your money goes. When Google offers to create a campaign during sign-up, look for the option to switch to Expert mode, and use it.

Make sure the account is under your business’s Google login, with your card on file, and add any agency or freelancer as a user. Never let someone else create the account for you on their login. We wrote a whole post on who owns your Google Ads account because untangling this later is miserable.

Complete advertiser verification when Google asks for it. Link Google Analytics 4 and your Google Business Profile under Tools, then Data manager. The Business Profile link is what lets location assets show your address and reviews in ads.

Step 2: Track calls and forms before you spend a dollar

Without tracking, Google’s bidding has no idea which clicks became customers, and neither do you. Set these up before launch:

  • Form submissions counted on the thank-you page or a confirmed form event, not on button clicks.
  • Calls from ads, using Google’s call assets with a minimum call length (we usually use 60 seconds for service businesses).
  • Calls from the website, via a call tracking number that swaps in for ad visitors.
  • Enhanced conversions for leads, which matches hashed form data back to the click and fills gaps from cookie loss.

Mark only real leads as primary conversions. Page views and “clicked directions” as primary goals teach the system to chase cheap, useless actions. The GA4 and Google Ads tracking guide has the click-by-click setup, and call tracking explains numbers.

Step 3: Which campaign type should a small business use?

For most local service businesses, start with Search. It shows text ads to people typing what you sell, and you can see and control the searches. Performance Max spreads one budget across Search, Maps, YouTube, Display and Gmail, and it works best for businesses with plenty of conversion data or a product feed.

Three 2026 changes to know:

AI Max for Search. Starting September 1, 2026, Google began auto-upgrading Search campaigns that used automatically created assets or the campaign-level broad match setting into AI Max, with search term matching (and for ACA campaigns, text customization) on by default, per Search Engine Roundtable. AI Max can also change which landing page a click goes to through final URL expansion, per Google’s AI Max help page. On a small account, check whether yours was upgraded, review the search terms it adds weekly, and set URL exclusions for pages you never want ads sending people to, such as careers or blog posts.

Call-only ads are going away. Google removed the option to create new call-only ads in February 2026, and existing ones stop serving in February 2027, per Google’s call ads transition notice. Move to responsive search ads with call assets now, on your own timeline.

Performance Max got more controllable. It now accepts up to 10,000 negative keywords per campaign. If you run PMax, add your negative list and brand exclusions on day one. Our Performance Max vs. Search comparison goes deeper.

And if you’re a home service business, look at Local Services Ads alongside Search. They’re pay-per-lead and sit above regular ads on many searches; see Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads.

Step 4: Keywords, match types and the negatives that save your budget

Group keywords by the service and the page they belong to. One ad group per service keeps ads and landing pages matched. Here’s an illustrative structure for a plumbing company based in The Woodlands.

Emergency plumbingemergency plumber near me, 24 hour plumber The WoodlandsPhrase and exactEmergency service page with click-to-call
Water heaterswater heater replacement Spring TX, tankless water heater installPhrase and exactWater heater page with price ranges
Slab leaksslab leak repair, slab leak detection ConroePhrase and exactSlab leak page explaining the process
Brand[your business name], [your name] plumbing reviewsExactHomepage

We start most small accounts on phrase and exact match. Broad match can work with smart bidding and a lot of conversion data, but on a $2,000-a-month account it tends to wander into searches you never meant to buy. Once tracking is solid and you’ve got a month or two of data, testing broad in one ad group is reasonable.

Negative keywords are where small budgets get saved. Load a starter list before launch, then read the search terms report (Insights and reports, then Search terms) every week for the first two months and keep adding.

Step 5: Location targeting for Houston and The Woodlands

Houston is huge and the traffic is real. Few customers in Kingwood will hire a company that’s an hour away in Sugar Land when there are 30 closer options. Target where you actually work, and draw it tighter than you think.

The setting that matters most is hidden under Locations, then Location options. Google’s default is “Presence or interest,” which includes people who’ve merely shown interest in your area, per Google’s advanced location options page. Switch it to Presence: people in or regularly in your included locations. Otherwise someone in Ohio researching a move to The Woodlands can trigger your “emergency plumber” ad.

Use ZIP codes or towns rather than one big radius when your area has edges. A radius around Market Street reaches into neighborhoods across Lake Woodlands you might not serve. If you’re in Cypress, think about whether you really work east of I-45. And exclude areas you won’t drive to, even if they’re inside the radius. You can add location bid adjustments later once you see which towns produce jobs.

Step 6: Write ads and landing pages that match the search

Responsive search ads take up to 15 headlines and 4 descriptions, and Google mixes them. Write headlines that each stand on their own. An illustrative set for the water heater ad group:

  • Water Heater Replacement Spring
  • Same-Day Installs, Most Models
  • Upfront Prices Before We Start
  • Licensed Texas Master Plumber
  • 4.9 Stars From 600+ Neighbors (only if true)
  • Tankless and Tank Water Heaters

Add sitelinks, callouts and a call asset scheduled for hours when someone answers. Pin only if you have a legal or brand reason to.

Then send the click to the matching page. Someone who searched “slab leak repair Conroe” should land on a page about slab leaks that mentions Conroe, shows a phone number at the top and explains what happens next. The homepage is almost never the right answer. Landing pages 101 covers the layout, and if clicks come in but calls don’t, the not-converting checklist is the place to start.

Step 7: Bidding, and when to let Google’s automation drive

Automated bidding is only as smart as the conversions you feed it. Our rule of thumb for small accounts:

  1. Weeks 1 to 4: Maximize conversions if tracking is solid from day one. If it isn’t, Maximize clicks with a maximum CPC cap so a few expensive clicks don’t eat the budget.
  2. After about 30 conversions in 30 days: test a target CPA set near your actual cost per lead. Setting it far lower than reality just throttles volume.
  3. Ongoing: change bid targets by 10% to 20% at a time and wait a week or two between changes. The system needs time to recalibrate.

Those thresholds are our experience, not a Google rule. Two settings to check under the campaign’s Networks: uncheck Display Network on Search campaigns (it’s a different product with different intent), and watch Search partners in the segment report before deciding to keep it.

How long does it take for Google Ads to work?

Calls can start the first week. Knowing whether the account is profitable takes longer: two to three months for most small businesses, because you need enough leads to judge quality, and enough closed jobs to know what a lead is worth.

Judge the account on cost per customer, not cost per click. A $12 click that books a $4,000 repair beats a $3 click that never calls. That means someone has to tell the ad account, or at least a spreadsheet, which leads turned into jobs. Our post on understanding cost per lead shows the math.

A weekly 30-minute routine covers most of what a small account needs. Read the search terms from the last seven days and add negatives. Listen to two or three recorded calls from ads, because the recordings tell you more about lead quality than any dashboard. Check that spend is pacing where you expect for the month. Look at which ad groups produced leads and which only produced clicks. Then leave everything else alone. Ignore impression share swings and the auto-generated recommendations tab for now; accepting every recommendation is a reliable way to raise spend without raising leads.

Your first 90 days, and the mistakes we see most

Here’s the order we’d run a new small business account in.

The mistakes we see most in Houston small business accounts, roughly in order of how much money they waste:

  • Location set to “Presence or interest,” so a share of clicks come from outside the service area.
  • Primary conversions that aren’t leads, such as page views or direction clicks.
  • No negatives, so the budget pays for job seekers, DIYers and people looking for free stuff.
  • Ads running 24/7 with a call asset, when nobody answers after 6 p.m.
  • Seasonal blindness. AC and plumbing demand jumps with the first heat wave and the first hard freeze; roofers see spikes after hail. Raise budgets before the rush. Our Greater Houston seasonal marketing guide has the calendar.
  • Changing everything at once every week, so nothing ever has time to learn.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum budget for Google Ads for a small business?

Google has no minimum spend, but a useful test usually needs enough budget for 10 to 15 clicks a day. With service-category clicks often costing $5 to $10, that means roughly $1,500 to $3,000 a month for most Houston service businesses. If that's too much, narrow the keywords and the service area rather than spreading a small budget thin across everything.

Is Google Ads worth it for a small local business in The Woodlands?

Usually yes for businesses that sell something people actively search for, like home services, legal, medical, auto repair or local retail. It works when calls and forms are tracked, ads target only the area you serve, negatives block junk searches and clicks land on matching pages. It is a harder fit for businesses customers rarely search for, where social ads or referrals tend to work better.

Should I use Performance Max or Search campaigns for a service business?

Start with Search for most service businesses, because you can see and control which searches trigger your ads. Performance Max spreads budget across Search, Maps, YouTube, Display and Gmail and tends to perform better with lots of conversion data. If you test it, add negative keywords and brand exclusions, use real lead conversions only, and compare its cost per customer against Search.

Why am I getting Google Ads clicks from outside Houston?

Most often because the location option is set to Presence or interest, the default, which includes people who have only shown interest in your area. Change it to Presence, meaning people in or regularly in your locations, under Locations and Location options in campaign settings. Also check that your radius or ZIP list doesn't reach areas you don't serve, and review the location report monthly.

Can I run Google Ads myself or should I hire someone in Houston?

You can run it yourself if you'll learn the settings and spend a couple of hours a week reading search terms, adjusting negatives and checking lead quality. Hire help when the budget is large enough that wasted spend costs more than management, when you can't keep up with weekly reviews, or when results have stalled. Either way, keep the account under your own login.
