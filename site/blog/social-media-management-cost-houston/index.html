Picture a dentist in Spring holding three proposals and asking which one is “the real price.” One is $650 a month. One is $2,800. One is $6,500 plus ad spend. All three say “social media management” at the top.

They were all real prices. They were for three different jobs.

The short answer: in Houston and The Woodlands, social media management usually runs about $500 to $2,000 a month for a freelancer, $1,500 to $6,000 a month for an agency, and roughly $7,000 to $10,500 a month for a full-time in-house hire once salary and benefits are counted. Ad spend is a separate line on top of any of those.

The rest of this guide shows what you get at each price, where the salary numbers come from, how ad budgets and management fees fit together, and how to line up proposals so you’re comparing the same thing.

If a proposal’s price is in the third row but its scope reads like the first, ask what you’re paying for.

What a social media manager costs to hire in-house in Houston

Owners often compare an agency fee against a salary number they saw on a job board. That’s the wrong comparison, so let’s build the right one.

Three public sources give a spread for Houston. Robert Half’s 2027 Salary Guide puts a Houston social media manager at $69,375 (low), $88,800 (mid) and $106,005 (high). Built In’s Houston salary data shows an average base of $72,538 with a median of $65,000 and a range of $53,000 to $96,000. PayScale reports an average of $56,162 across 47 salary profiles, with a 10th-to-90th percentile range of $33,000 to $111,000. Those differences make sense once you notice who each one samples: Robert Half leans toward placed corporate roles, PayScale includes a lot of junior and small-business jobs.

Salary isn’t the cost. Employer benefits add a lot. In the June 2026 Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, wages and salaries were 70.0% of total compensation for private industry workers, with benefits making up the other 30.0%. That’s a national average across all jobs, so use it as a rough multiplier, not a quote from your payroll company.

Do the division and the picture changes. An $88,800 salary at a 70% wage share comes to about $127,000 a year in total compensation, or roughly $10,600 a month. A $56,000 hire lands near $6,700 a month. Then add the things a new hire needs on day one: a scheduling tool (Sprout Social’s pricing page lists its Standard plan at $199 per seat a month), a design subscription, a decent phone and a light, and the hours you or a manager spend directing them.

An in-house person can be worth every dollar. They’re there for the ribbon cutting on Market Street, they know the staff by name, and they can film a 20-second clip the minute the new patio opens. The catch is breadth. One person rarely writes, designs, edits video, runs ads and reads attribution data equally well, and you’re back to hiring when they leave. What you get from a Houston freelancer at $500 to $2,000 a month

Freelancers are the right call for plenty of small businesses, and some of the best social people in Houston work for themselves. Price varies widely. Upwork’s national marketplace lists social media managers at $14 to $35 an hour, per Upwork’s hiring cost page. Local freelancers who can come to your location in Tomball or Pearland and film usually charge more than that, which is fair, because they’re selling time in the car too.

At $35 an hour, $700 a month buys 20 hours. That’s about what it takes to plan, write, design and schedule 12 decent posts and answer a modest number of comments. It doesn’t leave room for video editing, which is where the time goes now.

Where freelancers shine:

  • A single-location business (a salon in Kingwood, a bakery in Old Town Spring) that needs consistent posting and a responsive inbox.
  • Owners who will send photos and stories every week. A freelancer working from your camera roll beats one working from stock photos every time.
  • A test period before you commit to an agency retainer.

Where they struggle: vacations, illness and sudden growth. Ask who covers the account when they’re out, and get that answer in writing.

What you get from a Houston or The Woodlands agency

An agency costs more because you’re paying for a team: a strategist, a designer, a video editor, a community manager, and usually someone who runs paid campaigns. You also pay for overhead and for not having to manage any of those people.

For a business in The Woodlands, Katy or the Galleria area, a good agency retainer in the $2,000 to $4,000 range should get you a monthly content plan you approve before anything posts, original photo and video from a shoot day (monthly or quarterly), replies to comments and DMs within a set time, and a report that ties activity to calls, bookings or form fills. Likes alone don’t count.

That chart is an illustrative split from a mid-size organic retainer. The part owners underestimate is production. A one-minute Reel that looks effortless can take a couple of hours between shooting, editing, captions and cover frames.

Shoot days deserve their own line in any proposal. Drive time matters in a metro this spread out: a crew based near Research Forest can be at a client in Shenandoah in ten minutes and in Sugar Land in over an hour on a bad I-45 day.

Ask whether shoot days are included, how many, and what a location outside the agency’s usual area adds. Nobody wants a surprise mileage invoice after a Pearland shoot.

Ad spend vs. management fee: two different bills

This trips up more owners than anything else in social pricing. There are two separate costs:

  • Ad spend is what Meta or TikTok charges your card to show your ads. It should be billed to your own ad account, on your own card.
  • The management fee is what you pay the person or agency to plan, build, test and optimize those ads.

Management fees come in three common shapes: a flat monthly fee, a percentage of ad spend (10% to 20% is common in proposals we see), or a flat fee with a minimum. Each has trade-offs, which we lay out in retainer vs. percentage pricing.

How much ad spend is enough? For a local business running lead or booking campaigns in Greater Houston, we’d rarely start below $1,000 to $1,500 a month in spend. Below that, Meta’s system doesn’t get enough results to learn from, and you spend most of the month in the learning phase. That’s our experience across accounts, not a Meta rule.

One honest note on value. If you’re only going to have one paid channel, organic posting alone rarely produces a steady stream of new customers for a local service business; paid campaigns usually do. Which platforms should a Houston business pay to manage?

Every platform you add costs money, mostly in content and community time. Pick based on where your customers are and what you can feed with content.

Nationally, YouTube reaches 84% of U.S. adults, Facebook 70%, Instagram 51%, TikTok 37%, LinkedIn 31% and Nextdoor 18%, according to Pew Research Center’s 2026 fact sheet. Those numbers skew by age, and Houston’s customer mix differs from block to block: a pediatric practice in Cypress, a B2B supplier off the Hardy Toll Road and a cocktail bar in the Heights should not pay for the same platform list.

Our general rule: two platforms done well beat four done badly. Most local businesses we see do best starting with Facebook and Instagram (one Meta workflow, one ad account) and adding TikTok or LinkedIn only when there’s a clear audience and someone to make the content. One-time costs and what a monthly report should show

The monthly fee isn’t the whole first-year bill. Expect some setup costs in month one, and ask about them before you sign.

  • Onboarding or setup fee. Common with agencies, often equal to somewhere between half a month and a full month of the retainer in proposals we see. It covers account audits, brand templates, the first content calendar and connecting tracking. Ask what you keep if you leave.
  • Profile cleanup. Old Pages from a previous location, a duplicate Instagram account your nephew made in 2019, and a Page nobody has admin access to anymore. Sorting out Meta access can take hours, sometimes days.
  • Tracking. If ads are in scope, the Meta pixel and conversion events need to be set up on your website and tested. Without them, nobody can tell you which ads produced calls or bookings.

Once things are running, a useful monthly report fits on one or two pages. It should show what was posted and what performed, response times in the inbox, and the business numbers you agreed on at the start: calls, form fills, bookings, store visits or online orders. For ads, add spend, cost per lead and what changed in the account that month. A report that’s all follower counts and “impressions up 12%” is telling you the provider doesn’t know what you sell. Most bad social relationships could have been spotted in the proposal. These are the ones we see most often around Houston:

The ownership point matters more than the rest. Your Facebook Page, Instagram account and ad account should live in your own Meta business portfolio, with the agency added as a partner. If an agency creates the ad account inside their portfolio “to save time,” you can lose your ad history and audiences when you leave. How to compare social media proposals side by side

Proposals are written to be hard to compare. Each one uses different units: posts, “assets,” hours, “touchpoints.” Translate every one into the same five numbers before you look at price.

Once they’re on one sheet, divide the fee by the number of original pieces (posts plus videos made for you, not reposts or text-only stories). That gives you a cost per piece. Then check response time and reporting. In that example, the $650 proposal works out to about $54 per stock-photo post with no inbox coverage. The $2,800 one includes two shoot days a quarter and same-day replies. The $6,500 one includes ads management, which a single-location practice may not need yet.

They took the middle one, and planned to add ads after three months of content. That was the right order for them.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a small business in The Woodlands pay for social media management?

Most small businesses in The Woodlands spend somewhere between $1,000 and $4,000 a month on social media management, depending on how many platforms, how much original video and whether comment and message replies are included. A freelancer can handle simple posting for less. Paid ads are a separate cost, and ad spend should be billed directly to your own Meta account on your own card.

Is it cheaper to hire a social media manager or an agency in Houston?

An agency is usually cheaper than a full-time Houston hire once benefits are counted. Public salary data puts Houston social media managers roughly between $56,000 and $89,000 a year, and benefits add about 30% to employer costs on average. That works out to roughly $6,700 to $10,600 a month. An agency retainer often runs $1,500 to $6,000, though you share their attention with other clients.

Does social media management include paid ads?

Not always. Many proposals cover organic posting and community management only, and quote Facebook and Instagram ad management as a separate fee. Read the scope line by line. Even when ad management is included, the ad spend itself is a separate cost paid to Meta or TikTok. Ask for ad spend to run through your own ad account so you keep the data if you switch providers.

How many posts per week does a local business need on Instagram and Facebook?

For most local businesses, three to four good posts a week on Facebook and Instagram is plenty, with short videos making up a good share of them. Consistency and quality matter more than volume. Posting daily with stock photos rarely beats posting three times with real photos of your staff, your work and your customers. Stories can fill the gaps between posts with little extra effort.

What should be in a social media management contract?

A good contract lists the platforms, the number of original posts and videos per month, response time for comments and messages, who supplies photos, how many shoot days are included, what the report contains, and the notice period to cancel. It should also state that you own your pages, ad accounts and content, with the agency added as a partner in your Meta business portfolio.
