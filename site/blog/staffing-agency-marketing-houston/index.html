Monday, 6:40 a.m. A plant manager off the Ship Channel calls your branch: he needs twelve forklift operators by Wednesday, second shift, and his regular agency just told him “maybe six.” Your recruiter pulls up the Indeed post from last week. Four applicants, two of them in Dallas.

Across town, your account manager is in the Energy Corridor pitching an AP clerk and a payroll specialist to a controller who has already talked to three other firms.

That’s staffing agency marketing in Houston. You’re running two businesses at once. One sells to employers who want people fast, and the other sells jobs to people who have plenty of other options. Most agencies market only one side well, usually by accident.

The agencies that grow here build a steady pipeline of qualified candidates through job ads and Google’s job listings, win clients with industry-specific pages and direct outreach, collect reviews from both sides, and measure everything against cost per placement. This guide covers both sides for light industrial, healthcare, administrative and professional search firms in Houston, The Woodlands and the suburbs around them.

Two-sided marketing: clients and candidates need different funnels

Clients and candidates don’t search the same way, don’t respond to the same channels, and don’t care about the same things. Treating them as one audience is the most common mistake we see in staffing marketing.

Keep separate campaigns, separate landing pages, separate budgets and separate reporting. A dashboard that mixes “leads” from both sides is useless. Ten candidate applications and one client inquiry are not eleven leads.

The balance shifts with the market. When clients are hiring and candidates are scarce, put money into candidate acquisition. When orders slow down, shift toward client development. Your recruiters and account managers will tell you which side is starving if you ask them every week.

What Houston employers are hiring for now

Your client marketing should follow the hiring. the Greater Houston Partnership’s 2026 forecast projects 30,900 new jobs in metro Houston for 2026, below the recent average of about 50,000 a year. Health care and social assistance are expected to drive nearly half of that growth, with 14,000 new positions. Construction, public education, government and professional services also add jobs. Upstream oil and gas, along with administrative support services, is expected to shrink as oil prices fall.

Nationally, staffing firms employed just under two million temporary and contract workers a week in the third quarter of 2025, down 7.5% from a year earlier, with sales of $28.1 billion, according to the American Staffing Association. The ASA’s weekly index had turned positive year over year starting in September.

The takeaway for a Houston firm: if your client book is heavy on upstream energy and back-office admin, diversify your marketing toward healthcare, construction and petrochemical maintenance now, while you still have the margin to do it. Candidate ads on Meta and Google: the employment rules

Facebook and Instagram are still the cheapest way to reach hourly workers in Pasadena, Baytown, Humble or Conroe. But job ads on Meta must run under the Employment special ad category, and that changes how you target.

Per Jon Loomer’s guide to Meta’s special ad categories, employment ads are locked to ages 18 to 65+, all genders, and a minimum 15-mile radius around any location you pick. ZIP code targeting is off, some detailed targeting disappears, you can’t exclude anything, and lookalike and saved audiences aren’t available. Google applies similar restrictions to employment ads: no targeting or excluding by age, gender, parental status or marital status, and no ZIP code targeting, per Google’s housing, employment and credit policy FAQ.

So the ad itself does the targeting. Specific beats clever:

  • “Forklift operators, 2nd shift, $19/hr, La Porte, start Wednesday” finds forklift operators. “Join our team!” finds nobody.
  • Put the pay, shift, location and start date in the first line and on the image.
  • Run separate ads in Spanish for roles where that’s your candidate pool. Our Spanish-language marketing guide covers how to do it well.
  • Use Meta instant forms with two or three screening questions (certification, shift availability, transportation). Short forms bring volume; a question or two of friction brings people who show up.

Client-facing ads (selling your services to employers) are a different campaign. In accounts we’ve run, we keep job-offer language out of them entirely so they’re not confused with employment ads. Our guide to fixing bad Facebook leads applies to candidate forms too.

Google Jobs and JobPosting structured data

When someone searches “warehouse jobs Katy” or “medical assistant jobs The Woodlands,” Google often shows a job listings box above the regular results. Your own job pages can appear there, at no cost, if they carry JobPosting structured data that follows Google’s rules.

A few points from Google’s JobPosting documentation that trip up staffing firms. Google doesn’t allow expired job postings, so you need to set validThrough to a past date, return a 404 or 410, or remove the markup when a job fills. Google recommends its Indexing API over sitemaps for job pages, because it gets new and removed jobs crawled faster. And for a staffing agency hiring on behalf of a client it can’t name, Google says to use “confidential” as the hiring organization name.

Most applicant tracking systems can output this markup. Check that yours does it correctly with Google’s Rich Results Test, because a lot of them get pay or location wrong.

Indeed sponsorship and LinkedIn

Indeed. Since January 2019, Indeed has required recruitment-based accounts in the U.S., including staffing agencies, to pay to post their listings, per HR Dive. Today, Indeed’s pricing page describes results-based pricing. Depending on your setup, you pay per click on the job or per started application, and Indeed suggests a budget based on the title, location and competition. The same page says jobs can require payment when you aggregate them from a feed or ATS while also posting directly, or when a confidential post doesn’t name your organization.

How we’d manage it:

  • Sponsor the hard-to-fill and high-margin roles, not every req.
  • Rewrite titles to match what candidates search (“Forklift Operator,” not “Material Handler II – Client Site 4”).
  • Close or pause filled jobs the same day. Paying for applications to filled jobs is the easiest money to save.
  • Track cost per hire by job type, not cost per application. A $4 application that never shows up is expensive.

LinkedIn. For professional search (engineers, accountants, IT, project controls), LinkedIn is the main candidate channel, and recruiter seats usually beat ads for passive candidates. On the client side, LinkedIn is where you reach HR directors and plant managers with useful content, like a quarterly Houston pay rate report for the roles you fill. For light industrial, LinkedIn rarely earns its cost.

SEO for “staffing agency [industry] Houston”

Clients search for staffing by specialty and place: “industrial staffing agency Houston,” “healthcare staffing Houston,” “administrative staffing The Woodlands,” “temp agency Pasadena TX.” A generic homepage won’t rank for any of them. A page built for each will.

What each industry page needs:

  • The roles you fill, in the client’s words (millwrights, CNAs, AP clerks, process operators).
  • Typical time to fill, safety and screening process, and any industry requirements (TWIC cards, OSHA 10, background and drug screening).
  • Your service area by suburb and corridor: Ship Channel, Energy Corridor, Medical Center, I-45 north to Conroe.
  • A short case story with numbers you can actually back up.
  • A form for “request staff,” not “contact us.”

Separate those client pages from candidate pages (“forklift jobs Houston,” “CNA jobs Spring TX”). Candidate traffic is easier to win and it can flood your analytics, so report on the two separately.

For Google Maps, each branch needs its own Business Profile with accurate hours and categories. Don’t expect help from Local Services Ads; Google’s Local Services Ads category list has no staffing or employment category. Texas rules that touch staffing marketing

Texas doesn’t license general staffing agencies the way some states do. The state abolished its Personnel Employment Services program in 2011 (House Bill 3167) and deregulated Temporary Common Worker Employers in 2017 (Senate Bill 2065), according to TDLR’s own history page. Professional employer organizations are a separate category with their own rules, so check if you offer PEO services.

For unemployment tax, Texas law treats the staffing firm as the employer of its temporary employees. TWC’s unemployment coverage guide quotes Labor Code 201.029, which says “a temporary help firm is the employer of an individual employed by the firm.” That matters for your marketing claims. You can honestly tell clients that you handle payroll, unemployment tax and claims for your temps, and that’s a real selling point for a small manufacturer in Tomball who doesn’t want the headache.

Texting candidates needs care too. Texas brought marketing texts under its telephone solicitation law through SB 140, effective September 1, 2025, with exemptions for some situations, per Morgan Lewis’s summary of SB 140. Job alerts to candidates who opted in are different from promotional blasts, but get your attorney’s read on your setup. Our Texas SMS marketing rules post covers consent in more detail.

None of this is legal advice.

Reviews on Google and Glassdoor

Both sides read reviews. Clients check Google before calling, and candidates check Google, Indeed and Glassdoor before applying.

Google is the tricky one for staffing. Google’s conflict-of-interest rules have covered reviews about a current or former employment experience since late 2017, per Search Engine Roundtable. Your placed temps are your employees, so their reviews of working for you sit in a gray area. We’d focus Google review requests on client hiring managers and on candidates as job seekers (how fast you responded, how the interview went), and steer feedback about the employment itself to Glassdoor and Indeed.

Glassdoor has its own rules. Per Glassdoor’s review integrity rules, employers can’t offer money, bonuses or prizes for reviews, can’t ask employees for proof they posted, and can’t require reviews written in the office or on company equipment. Glassdoor says reviews tied to incentives are removed.

What works on both platforms:

  • Ask every client contact for a Google review after the first successful fill, by email with a direct link.
  • Ask candidates for feedback at the end of their first week, when you know how the placement is going.
  • Reply to every review, especially the angry ones from candidates who didn’t get a callback. That reply is for the next 100 candidates reading it.

Speed to fill is a marketing metric

In staffing, the agency that fills first usually wins the order, and the agency that calls a candidate back first usually wins the candidate. Speed is a marketing problem as much as an operations one, because everything you spend on ads is wasted if nobody responds.

What to set up:

  • Text new applicants within five minutes with a link to schedule a screening call. Automate it.
  • Respond to client inquiries the same hour. Route them to the account manager’s phone, not a shared inbox.
  • Keep a “ready bench” of screened candidates for your most common roles, and market to fill it before orders arrive.
  • Track time from order to first submittal, and from first submittal to start. Those are the numbers clients care about and the ones you should advertise once they’re good.

If you do both, run them as separate brands or at least separate sections of your site and separate ad accounts. Light industrial candidates don’t care about your executive search page, and a CFO doesn’t want to land on a forklift job.

Measuring cost per placement

Cost per lead and cost per application are the wrong numbers for staffing. Cost per placement (or cost per start for temp roles) is the one that matters, and you can calculate it by channel.

The formula is simple: total marketing spend on a channel divided by placements sourced from that channel. Getting the source right is the hard part. Every candidate needs a source field in your ATS, set automatically from the job board or ad, and every client needs a lead source in your CRM.

Once you have three months of clean data, compare channels by cost per start rather than cost per application. In accounts we’ve seen, the cheapest application source is rarely the cheapest placement source.

On the client side, measure cost per new account and the gross margin each account brings in its first year. A client that costs $3,000 to win and orders 40 temps is a much better deal than one that cost $500 and ordered two.

Check one more number monthly: the show rate for first-day starts. If candidates aren’t showing up, the problem may be your ad copy promising something the job doesn’t deliver.

A first 90 days for a Houston staffing agency

A realistic starting plan:

  1. Month one: split client and candidate tracking, set source fields in your ATS and CRM, fix your Google Business Profile for each branch, and check your job pages’ structured data.
  2. Month two: launch Meta candidate ads under the Employment category for your two highest-volume roles, sponsor hard-to-fill jobs on Indeed, and build two industry pages for clients.
  3. Month three: start client review requests, add automatic applicant texts, and review cost per placement by channel. Shift budget toward what produces starts, not applications.

Frequently asked questions

How do staffing agencies get more clients in Houston?

Build a page for each industry and role you fill, like industrial staffing Houston or healthcare staffing The Woodlands, with time to fill, screening process and service area. Pair that with direct outreach to plant and HR managers, Google reviews from client hiring managers, and LinkedIn content for professional roles. Track cost per new account and first-year margin, not just inquiries.

Can staffing agencies target job ads by age or ZIP code on Facebook?

No. Job ads on Meta must run under the Employment special ad category, which locks age to 18 to 65+, includes all genders, removes ZIP code targeting and requires at least a 15-mile radius. Lookalike and saved audiences aren’t available and you can’t exclude targeting. Google has similar rules for employment ads. Specific ad copy with pay, shift and location does the targeting instead.

How do I get my staffing agency’s jobs on Google Jobs?

Add JobPosting structured data to each job page with the required properties: title, description, datePosted, hiringOrganization and jobLocation. Include pay, employment type and validThrough where possible. Google doesn’t allow expired postings, so remove or expire filled jobs promptly, and use the Indexing API for faster updates. For confidential clients, Google says to use confidential as the hiring organization name.

Do staffing agencies need a license in Texas?

Texas doesn’t currently license general staffing agencies. The state abolished its Personnel Employment Services program in 2011 and deregulated Temporary Common Worker Employers in 2017, according to TDLR. For unemployment tax, Texas law treats the staffing firm as the employer of its temporary workers. Professional employer organizations have separate rules, so check with an attorney if you offer PEO services.

How should a staffing agency measure marketing ROI?

Measure cost per placement, or cost per start for temp roles, by channel. Divide what you spent on each channel by the placements it produced, which requires a source field on every candidate in your ATS. On the client side, track cost per new account and first-year gross margin. Cost per application is misleading, because cheap applicants often don’t qualify or show up.
