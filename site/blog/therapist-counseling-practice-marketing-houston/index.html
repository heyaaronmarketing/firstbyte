Rank for the specialty searches clients actually type

Therapy clients search for a problem and a place, so the practice that has one strong page per specialty and per area wins most of the organic traffic.

  • One page per specialty: anxiety, couples counseling, teen therapy, EMDR, trauma, postpartum. Describe who you see, what a first session looks like, the approach in plain words, fees and which clinicians do this work.
  • Area pages that are real: “couples counseling Katy,” “teen therapist Cypress,” “EMDR therapist The Woodlands.” Each names the office, parking and whether telehealth is offered.
  • Clinician bio pages with license type, Texas license status, training, specialties and the age groups each person sees.
  • Answers before the call: “What is a superbill?” “How is EMDR different from talk therapy?” AI search tools quote pages like these.
  • Structured data for the practice and each clinician, matching the name, address and phone on your Google Business Profile.

A single “Services” page listing fifteen specialties won’t rank for any of them. Our local landing pages guide covers area pages.

Set up Google Business Profile for a therapy practice

Your Business Profile decides whether you appear in the map results for “therapist near me,” and Google’s rules for practitioners are specific.

  • Solo practitioner: if you’re the only clinician at a branded practice, Google’s Business Profile guidelines say to use one shared profile named like “[Practice name]: [Your name].”
  • Group practice: the practice gets a profile for the location, and each public-facing clinician who can be reached there during stated hours can have one in their own name. No separate profiles per specialty.
  • Telehealth-only practices: Google requires a location customers can visit or a business that travels to customers. A virtual office doesn’t qualify, so a home-based telehealth practice shouldn’t create a fake suite address.
  • Primary category and services: pick the closest category for your practice type, then list each specialty as a service and link it to the matching page.

Use Psychology Today and directories, but don’t depend on them

Psychology Today is still worth $29.95 a month per clinician for most practices, but it shouldn’t be your only source of clients.

That price has been unchanged since 2004, according to ClearHealthCosts, which in December 2025 reported therapists describing referral drops of 50% or more since around mid-2024. Psychology Today told the outlet overall directory visits and contacts were steady. Judge it by your own numbers.

  • Track every directory separately. Give each profile its own tracked phone number and a URL with UTM tags, so you know which listing produced which first session.
  • Write the profile like a landing page. First line names who you help and where (“Teens and parents in Cypress and Tomball”), then fees, insurance and openings.
  • Check your own listing details. Some therapists told ClearHealthCosts they found platform phone numbers in place of their own.

Keep your payers’ “find a provider” listings current too. They’re free.

Run Google Ads with tight controls

Google Ads works for therapy practices when it’s Search-only, built around specialty and area keywords, and set up for the fact that health is a restricted category.

Two policy facts shape the whole account. First, Health in personalized advertising lists counseling for depression, anxiety and addiction as a sensitive interest. Advertisers in that category can’t use their own remarketing lists or Customer Match, per Google and Search Engine Land’s rundown. Second, if you treat drug or alcohol addiction, Google’s healthcare and medicines policy requires US advertisers to be certified through LegitScript before those ads can run, and not every provider is eligible. There’s also no Local Services Ads category for therapists or counselors in the US, per Google’s Local Services Ads category list, so regular Search is the paid option.

  • Keywords: “couples counseling the woodlands,” “anxiety therapist katy,” “EMDR therapist houston,” “teen counselor cypress tx,” “marriage counselor sugar land,” plus payer terms if you’re in-network.
  • Negatives: free, jobs, salary, careers, “how to become,” degree, CEU, internship, supervisor, “EMDR training,” “EMDR certification” (that’s clinicians shopping for courses), app, online chat, and Medicaid if you don’t accept it.
  • Crisis searches: don’t bid on them. Put 988 and emergency instructions on every page instead.
  • Sample ad: “Couples Counseling in The Woodlands | Licensed LMFTs, Evening Hours | In-Person or Telehealth in Texas.” No promised outcomes.

Follow Texas BHEC advertising rules

The Texas Behavioral Health Executive Council (BHEC) regulates Texas therapists, and each profession’s rules set advertising standards for your site, profiles and ads.

  • LPCs: 22 TAC §681.49 bars advertising that is false, misleading or “not readily verifiable,” representations that create unjustified expectations about results, and testimonials that are false or misleading. Ads and directory listings must state licensure status, such as “LPC.”
  • LPC Associates: the same rule says associate status must appear on all advertising, with the associate’s name followed by the supervisor’s name. Group practices miss this constantly.
  • Your agency is your problem: §681.49 also makes licensees responsible for statements made by people they hire to advertise. That includes us.
  • Psychologists: 22 TAC §465.6 says licensees may not solicit testimonials from current clients or engage in uninvited in-person solicitation.
  • Ethics codes go further: the current ACA Code of Ethics, C.3.b says counselors don’t solicit testimonials from current or former clients, and the APA Ethics Code, Standard 5.05 bars soliciting them from current clients. LMFTs and LCSWs have their own chapters and codes to check.

In practice, we write therapy websites with no client testimonials at all. This isn’t legal advice; have ethics counsel review anything you’re unsure about.

Keep tracking and reviews HIPAA-safe

Tracking pixels and review replies are where therapy practices create privacy problems by accident.

The federal rules on tracking moved in 2024. In American Hospital Association v. Becerra, a federal court in Fort Worth vacated the part of HHS’s tracking guidance that treated an IP address plus a visit to a public health page as protected information, but the rest of the March 2024 bulletin stands, per Holland & Knight’s summary of the ruling. Logged-in pages, intake forms and scheduling flows remain risky.

  • No ad pixels on intake forms, client portals or booking confirmations without a compliance sign-off.
  • Count conversions without content. A tracked call or a “form submitted” event without the form fields is enough. Our GA4 and Google Ads tracking guide shows how.
  • Expect Meta to limit you. Search Engine Land describes Meta’s tiered restrictions on health and wellness advertisers, which can block lower-funnel optimization. Meta’s personal attributes policy also forbids ads implying the viewer has a condition, with “Depression getting you down? Get help now.” given as a rejected example.

Reviews are harder. If you can’t ask clients for testimonials, you also shouldn’t be running review-request campaigns to them. Let reviews arrive on their own. A Dallas dental practice paid $10,000 to settle with HHS’s Office for Civil Rights in 2019 after Yelp replies disclosed patient details, per TMLT’s write-up. BHEC’s social media guidelines for MFTs also list derogatory online remarks about a client as unprofessional.

A reply that works for five stars or one: “Thank you for sharing this. Our policy is not to confirm or discuss whether anyone is a client. Please call the office if you’d like to talk with our practice director.” See our Google reviews guide for more.

Decide insurance or private pay before you write the site

Your payment model changes your keywords and pages, so decide it first.

  • In-network practices win on insurance searches. Build a page per major payer you accept and keep payer directories current.
  • Private-pay practices win on specialty depth, evening hours and clinician credentials. Publish fees, explain superbills and out-of-network benefits, and lead with specialties like EMDR and couples work.
  • Good faith estimates: under the No Surprises Act, practices must give uninsured and self-pay clients a written estimate and post a prominent notice in the office and on the website that one is available, per the American Psychiatric Association’s summary. Put it on your fees page.

Market telehealth across Texas, carefully

A Texas license lets you serve clients anywhere in Texas by telehealth, so statewide pages and ads are fair game.

BHEC’s 22 TAC §882.23 says practice happens where the client is located, and telehealth to a client outside Texas is subject to that other state’s rules. Psychologists have more room: Texas joined PSYPACT through HB 1501 in 2019. Counselors don’t yet. Texas isn’t on the Counseling Compact map, and SB 498 died in 2025, per the Texas Counseling Association’s session report. Social work compact bills also died.

So a Woodlands practice can run telehealth ads to Austin, Dallas and the Rio Grande Valley but should exclude other states and confirm Texas residency at booking.

Speed up intake

The practice that calls back first usually books the first session, because most people searching for a therapist contact several at once.

  • Answer live during business hours, or use an intake coordinator.
  • Same-day callback for voicemails and forms, with a short consult call script that covers fit, fees, availability and next steps.
  • Match quickly. In a group practice, intake should know who has openings this week, by specialty.

Mistakes we see

  • Testimonials on the homepage. Even anonymous ones. Under the codes above, take them down.
  • Associates listed like fully licensed clinicians, without “LPC Associate” and the supervisor’s name.
  • Meta pixel on the intake form because a website template installed it by default.
  • Ads that diagnose the reader, which Meta’s personal attributes policy rejects.
  • Bidding on “EMDR” alone and paying for clinicians looking for training.
  • Running ads for a clinician who’s full, then sending new clients to a six-week waitlist.

Measure cost per intake, not cost per lead

The number that matters is cost per first session attended, by source, because a therapy “lead” has a long way to go before it’s a client.

Track inquiries, consult calls held, first sessions booked and first sessions attended in a spreadsheet or your EHR’s referral field, monthly by source. Frequently asked questions

How much should a therapist spend on Google Ads in Houston?

A solo private-pay therapist can test with about $600 to $1,500 a month in ad spend focused on one or two specialties. Group practices often spend $2,000 to $6,000. There’s no counseling category in the 2026 WordStream benchmarks; the closest healthcare categories show median clicks of about $4.76 to $6.17. Judge results by cost per first session attended.

Is Psychology Today worth it for therapists in Houston?

Usually yes, at $29.95 a month per clinician, as long as it isn’t your only source of clients. Some therapists have reported fewer referrals since 2024, while Psychology Today says overall traffic is steady. Give your profile its own tracked phone number and check after three months how many first sessions it actually produced.

Can therapists use client testimonials in Texas?

Generally, no. Texas psychologists may not solicit testimonials from current clients under 22 TAC 465.6, and the ACA Code of Ethics says counselors don’t solicit them from current or former clients. Texas LPC rules also bar misleading testimonials. Most practices skip testimonials and describe their clinicians and methods instead. Check your own board rules and code.

Can therapists run Google remarketing ads?

No, not for mental health services. Google treats counseling for conditions like depression, anxiety and addiction as a sensitive health category, so you can’t use your own remarketing lists or Customer Match. Search campaigns built on specialty and location keywords still work well. Addiction treatment ads in the US also need LegitScript certification.

Can a Texas LPC see clients in other states by telehealth?

Only if the other state allows it. Texas rules say telehealth to a client outside Texas is subject to that state’s regulations, and Texas isn’t part of the Counseling Compact. Psychologists can use PSYPACT, which Texas joined in 2019. Most Texas counselors should target ads to Texas only and confirm residency at intake.
