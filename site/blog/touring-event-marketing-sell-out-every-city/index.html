Touring Event Marketing: How to Sell Out Every City on the Route

Touring event marketing for family exhibitions, fan conventions and shows: city launch timelines, budget per city, channel mix, pacing and when to move spend.

Monday morning tour call. The dinosaur expo has 14 stops on the spring route. Last weekend’s city sold 91% of capacity. This weekend’s is at 64% with five days to go. The city after that opens in 19 days and sits at 22%. Same creative, same budget formula, same ticket price, three completely different weeks.

Someone suggests adding a couple grand everywhere. Most tours do exactly that, and it’s usually wrong.

Selling out a touring event city by city comes down to treating every stop as its own small launch with its own calendar, budget and pacing target, then moving money between cities based on how each one is tracking against a curve you built from past stops. The creative and the playbook can be shared across the route. The decisions can’t.

This is written for marketing leads at touring family exhibitions, fan conventions, LEGO-style building festivals, bubble and science shows, holiday light experiences and similar productions that play a city for a weekend to a few weeks and move on. If you run one event in one town, our Houston event ticket playbook is the better fit.

Why a tour is harder to market than a single event

Every city behaves differently, often for reasons that have nothing to do with your ads:

  • Familiarity. A city that hosted you two years ago has a past-buyer list and word of mouth. A first-time market has neither.
  • Venue. A convention center downtown with paid parking sells differently from a fairground hall off the interstate with free lots.
  • Drive radius. Families will drive farther for a two-hour exhibit on a Saturday than for an evening show on a school night.
  • What else is happening that weekend. Spring break, a big rodeo, a college football home game, a church festival. You find these out late if nobody looks.
  • Lead time. Late venue confirmations mean some cities get ten weeks of runway and others five.

The budget side is messy too. Ad platforms reward stable campaigns, and tours keep starting and stopping them, so each new city starts learning from almost nothing. Proven creative is what carries over.

The city launch timeline: announce, presale, on-sale, last chance

Every stop on the route should run on the same skeleton, anchored to its own dates. We plan backward from opening day. The phases below assume a stop that plays one to three weekends. Longer runs repeat the final-push pattern every weekend.

The heavy back end isn’t a guess. Per Eventbrite data reported by Pollstar, 57% of U.S. tickets sold from January to April 2024 were bought a week or less before the event. That was music data. Family exhibitions in accounts we’ve run follow a similar shape, because parents pick a weekend activity days ahead, not months.

Our rule across a route: no first-time city announces later than about seven weeks out. Return markets with a big past-buyer list can get away with less.

How to set a budget per city

Most tours set a flat number per stop, something like $15,000 a city. It’s easy to manage and nearly always wrong, because a stop with 30,000 sellable tickets and a stop with 9,000 shouldn’t get the same money.

Start from each city’s gross potential instead:

  1. Sellable capacity. Sessions or time slots times capacity per session, minus comps and holds.
  2. Gross potential. Sellable capacity times your average all-in ticket price.
  3. Target gross. Gross potential times your realistic sell-through goal (we usually plan to 80% to 90% and treat anything above as upside).
  4. Paid media budget. Target gross times the share of revenue you’re willing to spend on paid media. For touring family shows, a planning range of 8% to 15% is common in our experience, higher for first-time markets and lower for return markets with a strong list.

Then set a target cost per order. If the average order is four tickets at $29 all in, an order is worth $116. At a 12% media share, you can afford roughly $14 in ad cost per order. Anything consistently above that in a city means the problem is creative, offer, date or tracking, and more money won’t fix it.

The channel mix: Meta, Google, TikTok and streaming

The channels don’t change much from city to city. Their weights do, by phase and by market size.

Meta (Facebook and Instagram) carries most touring family shows. That’s where parents are, and it’s where the purchase pixel data from your ticketing platform does the most good. A few structure choices that matter on a tour:

  • One campaign per city, with the Sales objective optimized for Purchase. Name it consistently, like tour26_tulsa_sales, so reporting rolls up.
  • Location targeting by the ZIP codes and towns inside a realistic drive, not a lazy 50-mile circle. A Saturday exhibit pulls from much farther than a weeknight show.
  • Exclude overlapping metros when two stops are close together on the route. Otherwise one city’s budget pays to sell the other city’s tickets, and your pacing data gets muddy.
  • Reuse winning creative across cities, swapping only the city, venue and dates.

Google Ads catches the searches your other channels create. Run brand search on the show name plus each city (“dinosaur expo Tulsa,” “bubble show tickets Omaha”), category searches (“dinosaur exhibit near me,” “LEGO event this weekend”) and planner searches (“things to do with kids this weekend [city]”). Google’s location targeting help explains the Presence and “Presence or interest” options; for a tour stop, interest-based targeting can catch grandparents in another state planning a visit, so we usually leave it on for brand terms and use Presence for the broad planner terms. Brand search also keeps resellers from buying the top spot on your own show name.

TikTok earns budget where the audience skews younger parents and where you have good creator content. Spark Ads let you run a creator’s own post as an ad; per TikTok’s Spark Ads guide, the creator generates an authorization code in the app or you link the account through Business Center. Creator posts from local parents usually beat polished brand video on TikTok.

Streaming TV and YouTube make sense in larger markets and longer runs. Streaming was 49.6% of TV watch-time in August 2026 and YouTube alone took 15.8%, per Nielsen’s August 2026 Gauge, so a ZIP-targeted :15 or :30 now reaches the family couch the way broadcast used to. We put CTV in the middle phase, where it builds the branded searches you then harvest in the last two weeks. Our streaming TV explainer covers costs and measurement.

Local media and influencer families

Paid media sells most of the tickets, but local voices make it cheaper. In our experience, a city with a morning-show segment and a few local parent creators posting shows a lower cost per order than one with ads alone.

For each city, about eight weeks out:

  • Local TV and radio. Pitch morning shows with something visual and a hook (an animatronic in the studio, a kid-builder contest). Send the press kit with b-roll, dates, prices and a contact who answers on weekends.
  • Family calendars and parent groups. Get listed early on the metro’s family-events sites, and ask big parent Facebook groups about a giveaway post.
  • Creator families. Pick three to six local parents with engaged followings in that metro, not one big national account. Our guide to finding the right influencers covers vetting.

Give creators comp tickets for opening weekend and a short brief. Ours fits on one page: the dates and venue, the two or three moments worth filming, the all-in price and the promo code, the deadline for posting, and what not to say. Then ask for whitelisting rights so you can put paid budget behind the best posts as Spark Ads on TikTok or partnership ads on Instagram, which Instagram’s partnership ads page describes as brands running a creator’s content as an ad to reach beyond the creator’s followers.

Disclosure isn’t optional. The FTC’s Endorsement Guides FAQ treats free products and perks as connections that need disclosure, says a vague disclosure isn’t enough when free tickets are involved, and says the disclosure “has the best chance of being clear and conspicuous if it’s included in the video itself.” Put “#ad” or “we got free tickets from [show]” in the brief, and check the posts. Our post on FTC rules for influencer partnerships has more.

The pacing dashboard: tickets sold vs. days out

This is the tool that turns a Monday tour call from opinions into decisions. You need one chart per city, and one curve that says where a typical city should be at each point.

Build the curve from your own history. Take every past stop that sold at least 80% of target, and for each one calculate cumulative tickets sold as a share of final tickets at 56, 42, 28, 21, 14, 7 and 3 days before opening. Average them. If you have different show types (a weekend-only stop vs. a three-week run), build a curve for each.

Then for every live city, every morning:

  • Expected to date = target tickets × curve share at today’s days-out.
  • Pace index = tickets sold to date ÷ expected to date. 100 means on curve. 120 means ahead. 75 means trouble.
  • Cost per order to date = paid spend in that city ÷ online orders in that city (from the ticketing platform, not the ad platform).
  • Sell-through by session. Saturday 11 a.m. can be sold out while Thursday 4 p.m. is at 30%. Totals hide that.

When to add spend, and when to cut it

Moving money between cities is the whole advantage of running a tour as one program. Write the rules down before the route starts, so nobody makes them up at 9 p.m. on a Thursday.

The trickiest one is the city that’s behind. The instinct is to throw money at it. Run the diagnosis first, in this order:

  1. Tracking. Are purchases still reaching Meta and Google? A broken pixel looks exactly like a bad market.
  2. Offer. Is the all-in price in the ad? Is there a reason to buy now (early bird ending, a slot selling out)?
  3. Creative. Is the city’s version running the hooks that won elsewhere, or a tired variant?
  4. Geography. Is the targeting map drawn around where buyers actually live? Pull ZIP codes from the ticketing report and compare.
  5. Calendar. Did something big land on your weekend? If so, shift budget to the other weekend or weekday slots rather than fighting it.

If all five check out and the city is still under 80 on pace at 21 days out, add money in measured steps (20% to 30% a few days apart) and watch cost per order. If cost per order climbs past your ceiling as budget goes up, stop. You’ve hit the ceiling of demand at that price, and the next lever is price tiers, group sales or added value, not media.

Cutting matters too. Pull sold-out sessions from the ads that day, and when a weekend passes 95%, move the creative to the next weekend or city.

Weather and date competition

A tour runs into each city’s calendar and climate. Some of it you can plan for months ahead. Some of it you find out about Tuesday.

Check each city’s calendar before you confirm dates, and again at announce: school spring breaks and holidays, big rodeos and state fairs, college and NFL home games, marathons that close roads near the venue, and other family shows playing the same month. Here at home, RodeoHouston runs March 2 to 21, 2027 at NRG Park, per 93Q Country. A family exhibition in Houston during those three weeks is competing with the biggest family outing of the year for weekend time and spending money. You can still play it. Price and message it differently, lean on weekday sessions and school groups, and don’t expect pacing to match a quiet month.

Weather cuts both ways. For an indoor show, a rainy or brutally hot weekend can be the best sales weekend of the run, so build a “beat the heat” or “rainy day plan” ad set and keep it paused until the forecast turns. For outdoor stops, weather is a risk to the whole date. In the Gulf states, hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30 with the peak around September 10, according to NOAA’s National Hurricane Center. Have a written reschedule plan and pre-built ads and emails ready, including the refund and transfer rules, so you can switch the message within hours if a storm hits.

Late buyers check the forecast, so say on the landing page what happens in bad weather.

Venue partners: what to ask for before you sign

The venue’s email list, social accounts, marquee and ticketing setup are all part of your marketing. Most of it is negotiable before the deal is signed and much harder after.

What we’d put on the list for every venue:

  • Ticketing access. Which platform the venue uses, who controls the event page, and whether you can place your own Meta, Google and TikTok tags on the confirmation page. On Ticketmaster, tags are placed through the TM1 account holder’s pixel tool, which is often the venue, not the touring show. Ask before on-sale, not after.
  • Daily sales reporting by session and by ZIP code, delivered automatically. You can’t pace a city on weekly PDFs.
  • Venue email and social. A dedicated email to their list at announce and in the final week, plus a set number of posts and stories.
  • On-site and outdoor assets. Marquee, digital screens, lobby posters, and any outdoor boards they control.
  • Group sales. Who handles school, scout and church groups, and whether the venue’s group desk will work your show.

Answer the ticketing question first. If purchases can’t reach your ad accounts, you’re running that city half blind.

Pricing in ads, and mistakes we see on tours

One rule now applies to every city. Since May 12, 2025, the FTC’s fees rule has required any business that “offers, displays, or advertises live-event tickets” to show the total price, mandatory fees included, more prominently than other pricing information, per the FTC’s fees rule FAQ. Taxes can come later as long as they’re disclosed before payment. If an ad says “tickets from $24,” $24 needs to be the all-in price of a ticket somebody can actually buy.

The mistakes we see most often on touring campaigns:

  • Flat budgets per city regardless of capacity or market history.
  • One national campaign with every city lumped together, so nobody can tell which city is working.
  • Spending evenly across weeks instead of saving 40% or more for the final two.
  • Judging cities by ad-platform purchases instead of ticketing-platform sales.
  • Creator posts with no disclosure, or comp tickets with no brief.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should a touring event start marketing in each city?

For most touring family shows and exhibitions, announce each city about eight to ten weeks before opening, run a short presale to past buyers and signups, open the public on-sale about seven weeks out, and save roughly 40 percent of the paid budget for the final fourteen days. Return markets with a big past-buyer list can work with a shorter runway. First-time markets rarely can.

How much should a touring show spend on ads per city?

Base it on each city’s gross potential instead of a flat number. Multiply sellable capacity by the average all-in ticket price, apply a realistic sell-through goal, then apply a media share. Many touring family shows plan somewhere between 8 and 15 percent of target gross for paid media, higher for first visits and lower for return markets. Then set a maximum cost per order and hold to it.

What is a pacing dashboard for ticket sales?

A pacing dashboard compares each city’s tickets sold to date against where a typical city should be at the same number of days before opening. You build the expected curve from past stops, then calculate a pace index: tickets sold divided by expected tickets to date. A pace index of 100 is on track, above 110 is ahead, and below 80 needs a diagnosis before more spend.

Should a touring event use influencers in every city?

Usually yes, but local ones. Three to six parent creators with real followings in that metro tend to beat one big national account for family shows. Give them comp tickets, a one-page brief and a posting deadline, ask for rights to run their posts as ads, and make sure every post discloses the free tickets in the video itself, as the FTC’s endorsement guidance recommends.

When should we move ad budget from one tour city to another?

Move money when one city is ahead of pace with a low cost per order and another is behind for reasons more media can fix. Before adding budget to a lagging city, check tracking, the offer, the creative, the targeting map and the local calendar. Add in steps of 20 to 30 percent and stop if cost per order climbs past your ceiling.
