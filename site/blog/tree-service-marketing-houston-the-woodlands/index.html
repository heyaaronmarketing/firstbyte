Tree Service Marketing in Houston and The Woodlands: Storm Calls, Trimming Work and Trust

The morning after a bad storm line, a two-crew tree company in Spring has 60 voicemails before 7 a.m. A pine is lying across a roof in Panther Creek. A water oak has a driveway in Kingwood blocked. Somebody in Cypress wants a price on “the big limb hanging over the kids’ room,” and three people just want to know if you’re real.

Six weeks later the same owner is staring at an empty October calendar, wondering where everyone went.

Tree service marketing in Houston has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. When a storm hits, you need to be findable, answer fast and look trustworthy to people who are scared and being door-knocked by trucks with out-of-state plates. The rest of the year, you need a steady pipeline of trimming, removals and stump grinding that keeps crews busy without a hurricane doing your sales for you.

This post covers both: the Google setup, the ad campaigns we’d split, the proof that wins the call, and a seasonal calendar built around Houston weather and the oak pruning rules.

Two businesses in one truck

Most tree companies market themselves as one thing. They’re really two.

Emergency work is a tree on a house, a car, a fence or a power drop. The customer wants someone today, will pay a premium, and decides in minutes based on who answers and who looks legitimate. Price shopping is light. Trust is everything.

Planned work is trimming, crown cleaning, deadwood removal, clearance over the roof, removals of dying trees, stump grinding, lot clearing and plant health care. These customers get two or three bids, read reviews, look at your photos and take a week or a month to decide.

The two need different keywords, different landing pages, different ad schedules and different budgets. When they share one campaign and one “Tree Services” page, the emergency budget gets spent on someone price-checking crape myrtle trimming in March, and the storm calls hit a page that doesn’t mention emergencies at all.

Keep that split in mind. It shows up in every section below.

What Houston storms do to tree demand

Houston gets tree emergencies from more than hurricanes. The May 16, 2024 derecho is the clearest recent example. A National Weather Service survey found straight-line winds peaking around 100 mph in downtown Houston, at least seven people died, and more than 800,000 Houston-metro customers lost power, per FOX Weather’s report on the derecho. Falling trees were among the causes of death named by city officials.

Seven weeks later, Hurricane Beryl knocked out power to 2.26 million CenterPoint customers, and the utility reported severe tree damage across its system, according to T&D World. In The Woodlands alone, the fire department logged 35 trees on homes and 32 trees blocking roads on July 8, per Community Impact, which quoted a Panther Creek resident saying he’d never seen a pine uprooted like the one that hit his house. Afterward, Community Impact reported that the township approved an additional $2 million for debris removal while the county worked to move around 1 million cubic yards of storm debris.

North Houston’s tall pines are a big part of why. Loblollies over a roofline are the call nobody wants to make and everybody makes eventually. That’s also your planned-work pitch: a lot of storm damage is preventable with clearance pruning and removals of trees that were already leaning or declining.

Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30. Spring brings its own squall lines, and winter freezes snap limbs too. So “storm season” in tree marketing covers most of the year, and your setup needs a switch you can flip in an afternoon.

The storm-chaser trust gap

After every big Houston storm, trucks show up from other states with chainsaws, a magnetic sign and a phone number. Some do honest work. Some take a deposit and disappear. Homeowners know this now, and it changes how they pick a tree company.

The post-Beryl advice from Houston Landing’s post-Beryl scam guide was blunt: make sure the contractor is licensed and insured, get several quotes, don’t pay upfront, be wary of pressure to sign quickly, and don’t sign over insurance checks to start repairs. And the Texas Attorney General’s office has reminded Texans that state law lets them cancel a contract with a door-to-door seller within three days of signing.

Your marketing should answer the questions a nervous homeowner is already asking. Homeowners can’t look up a tree crew on TDLR the way they can an electrician, so the proof has to come from you.

Put these on your website, your Google Business Profile and your estimates:

  • A local street address or at least a clear home base (“based in Spring since 2011, serving north Harris and south Montgomery County”).
  • Your certificate of insurance, available on request the same day, with general liability and workers’ comp named. Say so on the emergency page in plain words.
  • Photos of your actual trucks, crane or grapple saw, and crew in branded shirts. Stock photos of a climber in spotless gear read as fake.
  • A payment policy that says no full payment before the work is done, and that you’ll provide photos and an itemized invoice for the insurance claim.
  • Reviews that mention storms by name. “Came out the day after Beryl and got the pine off our roof” is worth more than ten “great job” reviews.

You’re selling the opposite of a storm chaser. Make that obvious without ever naming or mocking anyone.

ISA certification and insurance: the credentials that sell

The ISA Certified Arborist credential is the closest thing tree care has to a license homeowners recognize. Candidates generally need three years of full-time tree care experience (a related degree can substitute for some of it), must pass the exam, and recertify every three years with 30 continuing education units, per Jobber’s summary of the requirements. Customers can confirm a certification with ISA’s public lookup tool.

The Texas Trees Foundation tells homeowners to look for ISA certification and to ask for proof of liability and workers’ compensation insurance, with the insurer’s phone number if they want to check. So the people researching you are being coached to ask. Answer before they do.

  • Name your certified arborists on the site with their certification numbers and a photo. If the owner is certified, say it in the first screen of the homepage.
  • Put “ISA Certified Arborist on staff” in your Google Business Profile description and in ad copy, but only if it’s true today. Expired certifications are easy to check.
  • Offer a written assessment for removals of large or high-value trees. Homeowners in The Woodlands, Memorial or the Heights often want a second opinion before cutting down a 60-year-old oak.
  • Show the equipment that limits risk to their property: crane work, rigging, ground protection mats.

Certification and insurance won’t fill your calendar by themselves. They decide who wins the call once three companies show up in the same search.

Oak wilt and the February-through-June rule

Texas A&M Forest Service advises that oaks shouldn’t be pruned or wounded from February through June, and that wounds on oaks should be painted at any time of year, since fresh wounds attract the beetles that spread oak wilt. Oak wilt has been confirmed in 76 Texas counties and is hitting Central and West Texas hardest, per the Forest Service’s oak wilt FAQ.

The Houston area isn’t the epicenter, and we wouldn’t scare anyone into thinking it is. But the pruning guidance is a real scheduling fact, and your customers read the same headlines. A homeowner in Montgomery or Magnolia with live oaks they love wants a crew that knows the rule and explains it.

Marketing uses for it:

  • A page titled something like “When to trim oak trees in Houston and Montgomery County” that explains the guidance, your wound-painting practice and how you schedule around it.
  • An email and social push in late fall and January to book oak work before February. That fills what is often a slower stretch.
  • Training whoever answers the phone to say “we’ll schedule your oak for July” instead of quietly doing it in April.

It shows expertise in a way a homeowner can verify, which is the whole point.

Google Business Profile and Local Services Ads

The map pack is where “tree removal near me” gets decided. Set the profile up for both of your businesses.

  • Primary category: Tree service. Add secondary categories only for real services from Google’s list, such as an arborist or stump-related category if it fits.
  • Services: list emergency tree removal, storm cleanup, tree trimming, tree removal, stump grinding, lot clearing and plant health care as separate entries with one-line descriptions.
  • Hours: if you really answer at 2 a.m. after a storm, say 24 hours. If you don’t, don’t. Angry callers who reach voicemail leave reviews.
  • Photos: before-and-after shots by neighborhood, crane jobs, crews in gear. Add a batch after every storm week.

Our Houston map pack guide covers the ranking factors in detail.

Local Services Ads are worth a test. “Tree services” is on Google’s Local Services Ads category list, so tree companies can run the pay-per-lead ads that sit above regular search results once they pass Google’s screening. You pay per lead, can dispute junk leads, and your rank leans heavily on reviews and how fast you respond.

Two cautions from storm weeks. Set a weekly budget you can actually service, because after a hurricane LSA can send more leads in a day than you can answer, and every unanswered one hurts your ranking. And pause the moment you’re booked out for two weeks. We compare LSA and regular search ads in Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads, and the badge question in Google Guaranteed vs. Google Verified.

Google Ads: an emergency campaign and a trimming campaign

This is where the two-businesses idea pays off. Build two campaigns with separate budgets.

Emergency campaign. Keywords like “emergency tree removal,” “tree fell on house,” “tree on roof,” “fallen tree removal near me,” “storm tree removal Houston,” “24 hour tree service.” Call-focused ads, call assets on, and a landing page that loads fast on a phone with a giant tap-to-call button, your insurance line and photos of crane jobs. Keep it running year-round at a low budget so it has history, then raise the budget the day a storm is forecast, before landfall. Bids spike after a storm when every company in Texas turns on ads.

Sample ad:

Tree on Your Roof? Local Crew Today | ISA Certified, Fully Insured | Crane and Grapple Saw Removal. Spring-Based Since 2011. Photos and Itemized Invoice for Your Claim. No Payment Before Work.

Planned-work campaign. Keywords by service and town: “tree trimming The Woodlands,” “tree removal Spring TX,” “stump grinding Cypress,” “pine tree removal Conroe,” “oak tree trimming Kingwood,” “tree removal cost.” Send each ad group to the matching service page, which should show starting prices or typical ranges and a “send us photos for a quote” form. Schedule it for business hours.

Negatives keep both campaigns clean:

A few settings matter more than people think. Use “Presence: people in or regularly in your included locations.” Draw the radius around where you’ll actually drive a crane. Track calls over 60 seconds as conversions, plus form submissions, using our call tracking guide. Job-site marketing, Nextdoor and reviews

A crane in a cul-de-sac is the best ad you’ll ever buy. Every neighbor watches. Most of them have a tree they’ve been worrying about.

  • Yard sign at every job, with permission, for the day and a few days after.
  • Door hangers on the five houses each side: “We’re working at 14 Elm today. If you’ve got a limb over your roof, we can look while we’re here.”
  • A neighborhood-specific post on your Business Profile and Nextdoor page with before-and-after photos (no house numbers).

Nextdoor fits tree work better than most categories because neighbors ask “who did your trees?” constantly. Our Nextdoor advertising review covers when paid placements make sense.

Reviews are the other half. Text the review link the evening the job finishes, when the yard is clean and the customer is relieved. A simple script for the crew lead: “If we did right by you today, a Google review helps a small local company a lot. I’ll text you the link.” Don’t offer discounts for reviews and don’t send the link only to happy customers; both break Google’s rules. Our complete guide to Google reviews has more templates.

A seasonal plan built around Houston weather

Tree demand in Houston follows weather, the oak pruning window and the city’s heavy trash schedule. Inside the City of Houston, heavy trash, including bulk and tree waste, is now collected every other month in even-numbered months, per the city’s Solid Waste Management Division. A homeowner who saws up a limb in July waits until August for pickup. “We haul everything away the same day” is a real selling point for planned work.

  • November to January: book oak pruning before February, removals of dead trees from the past summer, holiday-season “clean up before guests” offers. Email past customers.
  • February to April: pine and non-oak pruning, spring storm readiness, stump grinding. Raise the planned-work budget.
  • May: pre-hurricane-season clearance pruning over roofs and power drops. This is the month to market prevention hardest.
  • June to November: emergency campaign on standby with a storm budget ready, oak work resumes in July, steady trimming in between.

Most companies only market in the busy weeks. The ones that grow keep a baseline running all year and use storms as surges, not as the plan.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more customers for my tree service?

Split your marketing into emergency and planned work. Set up your Google Business Profile with the Tree service category, real photos and storm-specific reviews, then run separate Google Ads campaigns for emergency removal and for trimming, removal and stump grinding by town. Show your ISA certification and insurance everywhere, use yard signs and door hangers at every job, and keep a baseline running all year instead of only after storms.

Are Local Services Ads available for tree services?

Yes. Tree services is one of the categories on Google’s Local Services Ads list, so tree companies can run pay-per-lead ads above regular search results after passing Google’s screening. Availability can vary by area, so start the sign-up with your address to check. Set a weekly budget you can actually answer, because storm weeks can flood you with leads, and slow responses hurt your ranking.

When should you not trim oak trees in Texas?

Texas A&M Forest Service advises against pruning or wounding oaks from February through June, since fresh wounds attract the beetles that spread oak wilt, and recommends painting oak wounds at any time of year. Oak wilt is most severe in Central and West Texas, but the guidance is a good practice for oaks anywhere. Tree companies can use it to book oak work in winter and summer.

How can homeowners tell a real tree company from a storm chaser?

Look for a local address and history, proof of liability and workers’ compensation insurance, an ISA Certified Arborist you can verify with ISA’s lookup tool, and reviews that mention past local storms. Avoid anyone who wants full payment upfront or asks you to sign over an insurance check. Texas law also lets you cancel a door-to-door contract within three days of signing.

Should a tree service run Google Ads all year?

Yes, at least at a baseline. A year-round planned-work campaign keeps crews busy with trimming, removals and stump grinding between storms, and a small always-on emergency campaign builds history so it performs when you raise the budget before a storm. Companies that only advertise after storms pay the highest click prices and compete with every out-of-town crew at once.
