Tutoring Center Marketing in Houston: Getting More Students Through the School Year

Tutoring center marketing in Houston: STAAR, SAT and ACT seasons, district calendars, Google Ads keywords, assessments, honest results claims and TEFA.

It’s 9:40 on a Tuesday night in late September. A mom in Cypress has just opened her eighth grader’s first progress report and found a 68 in math. She types “math tutor near me” into Google, opens four tabs, and fills out the form on the one site that tells her what happens next and roughly what it costs. The other three never hear from her.

That search, and what your center does in the 24 hours after it, is most of tutoring center marketing.

To get more tutoring students in Houston, plan your marketing around the school and testing calendar instead of spreading it evenly across the year. Run Google Ads on the specific phrases parents type, with a landing page for each subject and test. Make a diagnostic assessment the main conversion and follow up fast. Ask for reviews honestly, keep results claims you can back up, and get ready for the state’s new education savings accounts if your tutors qualify.

This guide is for independent learning centers and franchise owners across Greater Houston, from Katy and Cypress to The Woodlands, Pearland and inside the Loop.

The STAAR windows come from TEA’s 2026-2027 testing calendar, the SAT dates from College Board’s SAT calendar, and the ACT dates from ACT’s registration page. One big change is coming. Under House Bill 8, signed in September 2025, STAAR will be replaced starting in the 2027-28 school year by three shorter tests given at the beginning, middle and end of the year, with results back in about two days, per Texas Public Radio. That shifts tutoring demand. Instead of one spring panic, parents will get three score reports a year, each one a reason to call you. Start building pages and campaigns around “beginning of year test” results now, and keep STAAR pages up through spring 2027.

The assessment is your conversion

Most tutoring centers sell through a diagnostic assessment followed by a parent conference. That’s the right model. Most centers just make the assessment hard to book.

What the assessment page needs:

  • What it is and how long it takes (“a 45-minute skills check, then 20 minutes with the parent”).
  • What it costs, or that it’s free. Some centers charge a small fee and credit it toward enrollment, which tends to improve show rates.
  • Online booking with real time slots, including evenings and Saturdays.
  • What the parent gets: a written summary of strengths, gaps and a recommended plan.
  • A price range for ongoing programs. Parents who can’t find any number assume it’s expensive and keep shopping.

Then measure it like a funnel: leads, assessments booked, assessments completed, enrolled. If plenty of parents book and few show, fix reminders and the booking flow. If they show and don’t enroll, the parent conference is the problem, and that’s a sales training issue rather than a marketing one.

Speed matters more than most center directors think. A parent who filled out a form at 9:40 p.m. will often book wherever answers first the next morning. An automatic text within a minute (“Thanks, Maria. Here’s a link to pick an assessment time, or reply with a good time to call”) catches a lot of them. Our post on speed to lead covers the setup, and our guide to tracking forms and calls shows how to send booked assessments back to Google Ads as conversions.

Reviews and results claims: keep them honest

Tutoring is sold on outcomes, which makes it easy to cross a line.

Reviews. The FTC’s rule on consumer reviews and testimonials took effect October 21, 2024. It bans fake reviews, buying positive reviews, undisclosed insider reviews (staff or tutors’ relatives), review suppression, and incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment, with civil penalties of up to $51,744 per violation, adjusted for inflation, per Morgan Lewis’s summary of the FTC’s reviews rule. Google is stricter still and bans any incentive for a review on Maps, per Google’s prohibited content policy. So no gift card for a review, ever, and no asking only the families whose kids improved.

The cleanest ask: at the end-of-term parent conference, after you’ve shown the progress report, send every family the same review link by text.

Results claims. “Our students improve two letter grades” or “average SAT score increase of 150 points” are claims the FTC expects you to back up. The FTC’s Endorsement Guides FAQ explains that when an endorsement shows exceptional results, an advertiser without proof that those results are typical must make clear what people can generally expect. A testimonial from the one student who jumped 300 points needs that context.

What to do in practice:

  • If you publish an average improvement, calculate it from every student in the program over a defined period, including the ones who didn’t improve, and say how you measured it.
  • Name the test and the time frame (“students who completed our 8-week SAT course in 2025-26, first official score vs. second”).
  • Skip “guaranteed” unless you offer a real, written guarantee with clear terms.
  • Get written permission from parents before using a student’s name, school, photo or scores.

Franchise vs. independent: who controls what

The big national tutoring and math-center brands have name recognition, national ad funds and polished curriculum marketing. Independent centers have flexibility and usually a founder who knows every family. Both can win locally, but they play different games.

If you own a franchise:

  • Read your agreement for who owns the Google Business Profile, the local landing page, the Google Ads account and the call tracking numbers. In some systems corporate controls all of it; in others, owners run local ads within brand rules.
  • Find out what the national fund actually buys in Houston. Often it’s brand awareness plus a lead-routing system, and local search is still up to you.
  • Check territory rules before you set ad radiuses. Bidding into a neighboring franchisee’s ZIP codes causes problems.
  • Make your location page unique: your director, your tutors, your hours, your schools. Corporate templates that read identically across 40 locations don’t rank well for “tutoring near me.”

If you’re independent:

  • Lean into specifics a franchise can’t easily copy: certified teachers from local districts, small groups, flexible scheduling, and test prep tied to the high schools you serve.
  • Use your founder. A two-minute video of the director explaining how the assessment works converts better than any brand ad.
  • Build pages for each nearby school and district, like “Tutoring for Conroe ISD students near Oak Ridge High School,” with real content about the courses and tests those students face.

Texas Education Freedom Accounts: an opening for qualified tutors

Texas created Education Freedom Accounts through Senate Bill 2 in 2025, funded with $1 billion. For 2026-27, the program’s official site lists $10,474 for private school students, up to $30,000 for students with a disability and an IEP, and up to $2,000 for homeschooled and other students. More than 274,000 applications came in and over 100,000 awards had been made as of our check in November 2026. Tutoring is an allowed expense, per the Comptroller’s March 2026 release.

That $2,000 bucket is the interesting part for tutoring centers. It covers homeschoolers and other participating students, and many of those families will spend some of it on tutoring and test prep.

Whether your center can take that money depends on approval. Under Section 29.358 of the enrolled text of SB 2, a private tutor or teaching service has to show the comptroller that its tutors are current or retired educators at an accredited school, hold a relevant license or accreditation from a state, regional or national body, or have taught or tutored at a higher education provider. The provider also has to submit a national criminal history review (or authorize one), and the comptroller can only approve providers located in Texas or vendors registered to do business here. Section 29.359 lists tutoring fees and costs related to academic assessments among allowed expenses.

The program’s providers and vendors page says vendor applications are accepted on a rolling basis through Odyssey, the program’s platform, and the Comptroller reviews each application and offering before it appears in the marketplace. The state issued its final program rules in November 2025, and families receive funds in installments (July 1, October 1 and February 1 for private school students).

How we’d market it:

  • Apply first. Don’t say “TEFA-approved” or “accepts Education Freedom Account funds” anywhere until the Comptroller has approved you.
  • Once approved, build a page explaining in plain English how a family uses their account with you, which programs qualify and what the $2,000 covers at your prices.
  • Target homeschool families directly: co-op groups, homeschool Facebook groups (as a member, following their rules), and Google Ads on “homeschool tutoring Houston” and “homeschool math tutor.”
  • Watch for the 2027-28 application window. The state hadn’t announced dates when we checked, and families can join an interest list on the program site.

These are examples, not quotes. The real test is cost per enrolled student, measured against what a typical student pays over their time with you. A family that enrolls in September and stays through STAAR in April is worth a lot more than a one-month SAT cram, so track both.

Track these weekly: leads by source, assessments booked and completed, enrollments, and average months enrolled. Without the last one you’ll overvalue cheap test-prep leads and undervalue the families who stay all year.

Two mistakes we see across education businesses. Ads pointing to a homepage that lists eight programs, and pricing hidden until the parent conference. Fix those two before you raise the budget.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get more students for my tutoring center?

Plan around the school calendar. Advertise hardest in August, after the first report cards in fall, before STAAR in spring, and ahead of SAT and ACT dates. Run Google Ads on specific searches like algebra tutor near me or SAT prep plus your town, send each to its own page, make a diagnostic assessment easy to book online, and follow up within minutes. Then ask every family for a review.

What keywords should a tutoring center bid on in Google Ads?

Bid on subject and grade phrases such as math tutor for 5th grader, test prep phrases like SAT prep The Woodlands, state test phrases like STAAR tutoring Houston, and location phrases like tutoring center Katy. Add negatives for tutoring jobs, become a tutor, free answers, worksheets and homework help sites, because many tutoring searches come from job seekers and students looking for free help.

Can tutoring centers accept Texas Education Freedom Account funds?

Tutoring is an allowed expense, but a provider must be approved by the Texas Comptroller first. Under SB 2, tutors must be current or retired educators at an accredited school, hold a relevant license or accreditation, or have taught at a higher education provider, and the provider must complete a national criminal history review. Vendor applications are accepted on a rolling basis through the program's platform.

Is STAAR going away in Texas?

Yes, on a timeline. House Bill 8, signed in September 2025, replaces STAAR starting in the 2027-28 school year with three shorter tests given at the beginning, middle and end of the year, with results back in about two days. STAAR still runs in 2026-27, with end-of-course tests in December and June and spring testing windows in April 2027.

Can I advertise average score improvements for my tutoring program?

You can if you can prove them. The FTC expects advertisers to substantiate results claims, and testimonials showing exceptional results need context about what students generally achieve. Calculate any average from every student in a defined program and period, name the test and time frame, avoid the word guaranteed unless you offer a written guarantee, and get parent permission before using a student's name or scores.
