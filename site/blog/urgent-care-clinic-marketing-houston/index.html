Monday, 8:05 a.m., second week of January. Your Cypress clinic’s online check-in queue is already full, with a two-hour wait and a lobby of coughing kids. Your Tomball clinic, 15 minutes away, has one patient. Google is showing the Tomball listing as “Closes 5 p.m.” because someone never updated it after you extended hours in the fall. A mom in Spring searches “urgent care near me,” sees a freestanding ER with a shorter wait, and goes there. She’ll find out about the facility fee in March.

Urgent care marketing in Houston is mostly operations wearing a marketing hat. To get more patients you need accurate Google listings for every location, a check-in system people can see and trust, and ads that run only when a location can take patients. Then you have to be clearer than anyone else about what you are, what you take and what it costs.

This guide is for independent urgent care operators with one to a dozen locations across Greater Houston: The Woodlands, Spring, Katy, Cypress, Pearland, Sugar Land, Humble, Kingwood and the rest.

The Houston urgent care market: independents, hospital systems and freestanding ERs

The country added 611 urgent care centers in 2025 and ended the year at 15,309, a net gain of 277 after closures and consolidation. Texas led every state with 66 new centers, per the Urgent Care Association’s 2025 recap.

In Houston you’re competing with three groups: other independents and franchises, hospital-system urgent cares with big brands and in-network contracts, and freestanding emergency rooms, which look a lot like urgent cares from the street.

Hospital systems win on brand, network and referrals into their specialists. You can win on convenience: shorter waits, accurate hours, easy online check-in, clear self-pay prices, and a location page that tells a parent exactly what you treat. Those are things a big system often does worse, because its urgent cares are a small part of a large website.

Freestanding ER confusion, and how to use it honestly

Texas was the first state to license independent freestanding emergency rooms, and just over 200 had opened by 2019, some looking like urgent care clinics while charging ER prices, according to the Texas Tribune. The same article cited a 2018 AARP Texas survey finding 77% of freestanding ERs said online that they accepted insurance while being out of network with all major plans.

The Legislature responded. HB 2041, effective September 1, 2019, requires freestanding emergency facilities to post a notice that the facility “charges rates comparable to a hospital emergency room and may charge a facility fee,” and to disclose network status at the entrance, in treatment rooms, at payment locations and on the website (on the home page or a prominent “Insurance Information” page). It bars them from saying they “take” or “accept” an insurer unless they’re in network, and from using an out-of-network insurer’s name or logo in marketing. A companion bill, HB 1941, let the attorney general go after “unconscionable” ER charges, defined in the article as more than 200% above the average hospital charge for similar treatment.

For an urgent care, the lesson is that patients in Houston are already confused, and many have been burned by a surprise bill. Clarity is a selling point:

Put “We are an urgent care clinic, not an emergency room” on every location page, with a short list of what you treat and a separate list of symptoms that mean “call 911 or go to an ER.” Show self-pay prices for a basic visit. List the insurance plans you’re actually in network with, and update that list when contracts change. Never imply ER capability you don’t have. The goal is a parent who trusts you to tell them when they need the ER, and who comes back for everything else.

Google Business Profile for every location, with hours that are actually right

Most urgent care visits start in Google Maps. Your profiles are the most important marketing you own, and one wrong hour can send a whole evening of patients elsewhere.

Google’s Business Profile guidelines say each location gets one profile, no more, and that you can set special hours for holidays and events. Doctors and other public-facing practitioners can have their own profiles, separate from the location’s. For a multi-location group, the practical checklist:

The hours problem deserves its own paragraph. Urgent cares often stop checking in patients before they close. If your doors close at 8 p.m. but you stop taking walk-ins at 7:30, say that on the location page and in the profile description, and make sure the online check-in shuts off at the same time. A patient who drives from Atascocita to a closed door leaves the kind of review you’ll be answering for a year.

Set holiday hours weeks ahead for Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year’s, Memorial Day, July 4th and Labor Day, and update for hurricane closures the same hour you decide. Our multi-location local SEO guide and map pack guide go deeper on ranking each location.

Online check-in and wait times

Online check-in has become table stakes for urgent care. Patients want to wait at home, not in a lobby with flu season in full swing. A visible wait time or next available slot on your site is one of the strongest conversion tools you have.

What we’d get right:

  • Put the check-in link in each Google Business Profile as the appointment link, pointing to that specific location.
  • Show the current wait or the next available time on each location page, and on the locations list so people can pick the shortest one.
  • Make the check-in flow short. Name, date of birth, phone, reason for visit. Insurance details can wait until arrival.
  • Send a text when it’s time to head over, and another if the wait changes.
  • Spread demand. When Cypress is backed up and Tomball is empty, the site and the front desk should say so. “Our Tomball clinic has a 15-minute wait right now” fills empty rooms for free.

One caution, which we’ll come back to: the check-in flow is where patients type in health information, so it’s the worst possible place for an advertising pixel.

Google Ads for “urgent care near me”

The search terms are short and urgent: “urgent care near me,” “urgent care open now,” “walk in clinic Katy,” “urgent care Pearland,” “strep test near me,” “stitches near me,” “x-ray urgent care.” Most of the volume is near-me and open-now searches on phones, so ads need to be tightly tied to location and hours.

How we’d set it up for a group with several clinics:

  • One campaign per location with a radius drawn around realistic drive times (often 5 to 8 miles in Houston traffic), and location assets so each ad shows the right address.
  • Ad schedules that match check-in hours, ending 30 minutes before the last patient is accepted. No ads at 9 p.m. for a clinic that closed at 8.
  • Service ad groups for the things people search by name: x-rays, stitches, strep and flu tests, COVID tests, sports physicals, DOT physicals, drug screens, IV fluids if you offer them.
  • Call assets with call reporting, since many urgent care patients call to ask about wait times or insurance first.
  • A pause rule for days when a location is at capacity or short-staffed. Paying for clicks to a two-hour wait does nothing but generate bad reviews.

Negatives to start with: jobs, hiring, nurse, medical assistant, salary, “urgent care near me hiring,” vet, animal, pet, emergency vet, dental (unless you do dental), eye doctor, “free clinic,” and the names of hospital systems if their brand searches keep showing up. Seasonal demand: flu season, back-to-school and sports physicals

Urgent care demand follows a calendar you can plan around. Per the CDC, flu season usually happens in fall and winter, and most of the time activity peaks between December and February. February was the most common peak month from the 1982-83 season through 2023-24.

The other big wave is late summer. UIL Section 1205 requires a physical exam signed by a physician (or a PA, APRN or chiropractor) upon entering the first and third years of high school, plus a medical history form every year before any practice. Fall sports start in late summer, so July and early August is when parents search “sports physical near me.”

A rough marketing calendar we’d use for a Houston group:

  • June to August. Sports and school physicals: a dedicated page with price, what form to bring (the UIL form or the district’s), and walk-in hours. Search ads on “sports physical [town]” and Meta posts to local parents.
  • September to October. Flu shots and the start of respiratory season. Push online check-in.
  • November to March. Flu, strep and RSV season. Increase search budgets, keep wait times visible, and use load-balancing messages between locations.
  • Hurricane season, June through November. After storms, people show up with cuts, sprains and heat-related problems from cleanup. Keep a storm-hours plan for your profiles and site.

Occupational medicine and employer accounts

Walk-in volume rises and falls with flu season. Employer work is steadier and books during your slow hours. Houston has a lot of it, between the refineries and plants along the Ship Channel, the Energy Corridor, construction crews across the suburbs, and trucking and logistics companies along every highway.

The common services: pre-employment physicals, drug and alcohol screens, DOT physicals, workers’ comp injury care, and return-to-work evaluations. DOT physicals have a specific rule. Since May 21, 2014, new commercial driver physicals have to be performed by a medical examiner listed on the National Registry of Certified Medical Examiners, per FMCSA. If your providers are on the registry, say so on the page and in your Google profile’s services.

Employer marketing looks more like B2B sales than consumer advertising:

  1. Build an employer services page: services, turnaround on results, billing terms, and a direct contact.
  2. Make a list of 50 employers within ten minutes of each clinic. Safety managers and HR are your buyers.
  3. Offer a dedicated check-in path for employer-sent patients so they don’t sit in the flu lobby.
  4. Report results fast. Employers stay with the clinic that gets drug screen results back quickly and never loses paperwork.

Insurance pages and price transparency

“Do you take my insurance?” is the most common question urgent cares get. Answer it on the site so the front desk doesn’t have to answer it 80 times a day.

  • Build an insurance page listing the plans you’re in network with, by name, including Medicare, Medicaid managed care plans and marketplace plans if you take them. Date it and keep it current.
  • Add a sentence on what to expect: copays, deductibles, and that coverage depends on the patient’s specific plan.
  • Publish self-pay prices for a basic visit and common add-ons (strep test, x-ray, stitches, physicals). Houston has a large uninsured and high-deductible population, and a visible price wins those patients.
  • Add a Spanish version of your insurance and pricing pages. Our post on Spanish-language marketing in Houston explains why it matters here.

There’s also a federal rule to follow. Under the No Surprises Act, providers have to tell uninsured and self-pay patients about their right to a good faith estimate and give one in writing when it’s requested or when a service is scheduled at least three business days ahead. The notice has to be posted on your website and in the office, and patients billed at least $400 more than the estimate can use a dispute process, per CMS’s No Surprises Act summary for providers. Walk-in visits rarely trigger the scheduling clause, but any discussion of cost counts as a request, so the website notice and a front-desk process belong in place.

HIPAA and tracking pixels on check-in pages

This is where urgent care marketing gets into legal trouble most often. HHS’s bulletin on online tracking technologies, first issued in November 2022, says tracking tools on pages where patients schedule appointments or enter health information can collect protected health information, and that vendors handling PHI generally need a business associate agreement. In June 2024, a federal court in the Northern District of Texas, in American Hospital Association v. Becerra, vacated the part of the guidance dealing with an IP address linked to a visit to a public health page. The rest, including the parts about scheduling and patient portals, still stands.

What that means in practice for an urgent care:

  • No Meta pixel, TikTok pixel or similar ad tags on the online check-in flow, the patient portal, or any page where someone types a reason for visit.
  • Count conversions without health details. A “check-in completed” event with no reason-for-visit data, sent from a vendor that will sign a BAA, is the safer route.
  • Audit your tag manager. Many urgent care sites we look at have tags someone added years ago and forgot.
  • Ask your check-in vendor what tracking it supports and whether it signs BAAs before you connect it to ad platforms.

What to track, and a first 90 days

The metric that runs an urgent care is visits per location per day. Marketing should report against it, by location, along with cost per visit by channel, online check-in share, review rating and count by location, and the percentage of hours each location was at capacity.

Frequently asked questions

How do urgent care clinics get more patients?

Start with an accurate Google Business Profile for every location, with correct hours, holiday hours and an online check-in link. Show wait times on your site, list the insurance plans you’re in network with and publish self-pay prices. Run Google Ads per location only during check-in hours. Then add seasonal campaigns for flu season and sports physicals, plus employer services like drug screens and DOT physicals.

What is the difference between urgent care and a freestanding ER in Texas?

Freestanding emergency rooms are licensed emergency facilities. Texas requires them to post that they charge rates comparable to a hospital emergency room and may charge a facility fee, and to disclose their network status. Urgent care clinics treat non-emergency illnesses and injuries, usually at much lower cost. Urgent cares should say clearly that they aren’t ERs and list symptoms that need emergency care.

Is it OK to put a Facebook pixel on an urgent care website?

Not on pages where patients check in, schedule, log in or describe symptoms. HHS guidance says tracking tools on those pages can capture protected health information, and a 2024 court ruling only vacated the part about public pages. Keep ad pixels off check-in and portal pages, track conversions without health details, and use vendors that sign business associate agreements.

When is the busiest season for urgent care in Houston?

Respiratory illness season, roughly November through March, is the biggest wave. The CDC says flu activity most often peaks between December and February, with February the most common peak month. Late July and August bring a second wave of sports physicals, since UIL requires a physical in the first and third years of high school plus a yearly medical history form.

Do urgent cares have to give price estimates?

Under the No Surprises Act, providers must tell uninsured and self-pay patients about their right to a good faith estimate, post that notice on their website and in the office, and provide a written estimate on request or when a service is scheduled at least three business days ahead. Publishing self-pay prices for common visits also helps convert patients comparing options.
