“Why am I still paying every month for a website that’s already built?”

We hear some version of that from a Houston business owner most weeks. It’s a fair question, and the honest answer is that some of those monthly charges are real costs, some are insurance, and a few are padding. This guide splits them apart, with prices from the platforms’ own pages as of late November 2026.

The short answer: website maintenance for a typical small-business site in Houston or The Woodlands costs about $20 to $50 a month on a hosted builder like Squarespace or Wix, where updates are included. A WordPress site with a professional care plan usually runs $100 to $350 a month in our experience. Add a domain at roughly $11 to $25 a year. SSL should cost nothing.

The cheapest option is rarely the one that costs least over three years. The rest of this post is about why.

Domain names: the cheapest line, and the one that causes the most trouble

A .com domain doesn’t cost much. Verisign, which runs the .com registry, raised its wholesale price from $10.26 to $10.97 on Nov. 1, 2026, per Webhosting.today. Registrars add their markup on top. Cloudflare Registrar says it sells at cost with no markup, while plenty of the big consumer registrars advertise a cheap first year and renew at two or three times that.

The price isn’t the real risk, though. Ownership is. When a web designer registers your domain in their own account, they control your website address and your email. When they retire, move to Austin or stop answering, you find out how much that matters. It happens more often than you’d think: the renewal notice goes to a designer’s old inbox, the domain lapses, and the website and email go dark together.

Three things to check today. Make sure the domain is registered in your business’s name, with an email address you control. Turn on auto-renew with a card that won’t expire next month. And write down the registrar login somewhere other than your office computer. If someone else has it now, our post on getting your domain back from a web designer walks through ICANN’s process.

Do you need to pay for an SSL certificate?

Almost never, for a small business. Let’s Encrypt issues trusted certificates at zero cost, and most hosts and every major website builder include free, auto-renewing SSL. If someone is billing you $80 to $150 a year for “SSL,” ask what you’re getting that the free certificate doesn’t give you. For a brochure site or a lead-gen site, the answer is usually nothing.

What you do need is for SSL to work everywhere, all the time. In October 2026, Chrome 154 turned on “Always Use Secure Connections” for public sites by default, which means Chrome now shows a warning and asks visitors to confirm before loading a site over plain HTTP, per Google’s Chrome security team. A lapsed certificate or an old page that still loads over HTTP now costs you visitors at the door, before they see a thing.

This is a maintenance task, not a purchase. Someone should check that the certificate renews, that every page redirects to HTTPS and that your forms submit securely.

For maintenance costs, the key difference is simple: on Squarespace and Wix, the company does the updates. On WordPress, somebody on your side does, or nobody does.

Updates and security: what you’re actually paying for

Most of a WordPress care plan is updates, and the numbers explain why.

Patchstack’s 2026 security report counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, up 42% from 2024. Of those, 91% were in plugins, 9% in themes, and only six were in WordPress core. Nearly half (46%) had no fix available from the developer when they were made public. For heavily exploited flaws, the weighted median time to mass exploitation was five hours.

Five hours. That’s why “we update plugins every quarter” isn’t a maintenance plan. It’s also why the count of plugins on your site matters more than the brand of your host. A site with 12 well-maintained plugins is far easier to keep safe than one with 45, a few of them last updated back when the iPhone 8 was new.

Then there’s the software underneath WordPress. PHP 8.2 reaches the end of its security support on Dec. 31, 2026, and PHP 8.3 follows on Dec. 31, 2027, per php.net. WordPress.org’s requirements page recommends PHP 8.3 or newer. When a host moves your site to a newer PHP version (and they will), an old theme or an abandoned plugin can break with it, which usually shows up as a blank white page or a contact form that quietly stops sending. If your site hasn’t been touched since 2022, that upgrade is coming for it within the next year.

Backups, and the Houston reason to keep them off-site

Most hosts include automatic backups. Good. The questions that matter are where the backups live, how long they’re kept, and whether anyone has ever restored one.

A backup that sits on the same server as the site disappears with the site if the server account is compromised or cancelled. Keep at least one copy somewhere else, such as cloud storage you own, and keep 30 days of history so you can go back to before a hack you didn’t notice for a week.

Houston adds its own reason. When Hurricane Beryl hit in July 2024, CenterPoint had about 2.26 million outages at the peak, and some customers were still dark more than a week later, per the Texas Tribune. Websites on commercial hosting stayed up. Sites (and email servers, and the only copy of the logo files) running on a machine in a back office in Pearland or Humble did not. If anything about your website lives on hardware in your building, fix that before next June.

Test a restore once a year. It takes an hour on a staging site, and it’s the only way to know the backups actually work.

What’s in a website care plan? Typical tiers and prices

Agencies and freelancers package maintenance as monthly care plans. Names vary, but the tiers usually look like this. The prices are ranges from what we see across the Houston market and our own work, not a published benchmark.

TierTypical monthly rangeWhat’s usually includedGood fit
Basic$75 to $150Updates, daily backups, uptime monitoring, security scans, a monthly reportBrochure sites that rarely change
Standard$150 to $350Basic plus off-site backups, an hour or two of edits, form and speed checks, PHP upgrade testingMost service businesses getting leads from the site
Growth$400 to $1,000+Standard plus new pages, landing pages for ads, conversion tracking checks, SEO fixesSites with ad spend or lots of service-area pages

Two things separate a good plan from a bad one. The first is whether updates are tested. Clicking “update all” on a live site is how forms break on a Friday afternoon. Good plans update on a staging copy first, or at least check the key pages right after.

The second is whether anyone checks the stuff that makes you money. A site can be fully updated and still have a contact form sending leads to an inbox nobody reads. We’d want the monthly report to include a test form submission and a test click-to-call, along with a check that your form and call tracking still fires.

Hourly work outside a plan usually runs somewhere in the $75 to $175 range around Houston, again in our experience. A hacked-site cleanup almost always costs more than a year of the basic tier.

What breaks when nobody maintains a website

Unmaintained sites rarely crash all at once. They decay, and the owner usually notices last.

The expensive part is the stretch in the middle, when the site looks fine to you and has quietly stopped working for customers. A form that stopped sending in April doesn’t announce itself. You just have a slow summer and blame the heat.

Speed slides too. Images get added at full camera size, scripts pile up from old ad campaigns, and the plugin that once made the site fast is now three versions behind. Who should own the logins?

You should. Your vendor should have access, under their own user account, that you can remove in two minutes. Shared passwords and “the developer set it all up” are how businesses end up negotiating to get their own website back.

Keep this list in a password manager the business owns, with at least two people at the company who can open it. When you change vendors, remove the old vendor’s users the same day and change any shared passwords. Our post on who owns your marketing accounts when you switch agencies covers Google Ads, Analytics and Business Profile in the same way.

Is it cheaper to maintain a website yourself?

On a hosted builder, yes, usually. Squarespace and Wix handle the software, so your job is content: hours, prices, photos, the occasional new page. An owner who’s comfortable with the editor can do that in an hour or two a month.

On WordPress, DIY is cheaper only if you actually do it. That means checking for updates weekly, updating carefully, keeping off-site backups, watching for PHP changes from your host, and testing your forms after every change. Call it two to four hours a month for a simple site, in our experience, plus the occasional evening when an update breaks something.

The math most owners skip is what an hour of their time is worth, and what a broken form costs during your busy season. A Conroe HVAC company that misses a week of leads in July has lost more than a year of care-plan fees. A Montgomery County photographer whose site is mostly a portfolio probably hasn’t.

Frequently asked questions

How much does WordPress website maintenance cost per month in Houston?

Hosting alone runs about $18 to $45 a month at regular renewal prices for a small WordPress site. A professional care plan covering updates, backups, monitoring and small edits usually costs $100 to $350 a month for a Houston small business in our experience, and more if it includes new pages or ad landing pages. Doing it yourself costs less in cash but takes a few hours a month.

Do I need to pay for an SSL certificate for my small business website?

Usually not. Free certificates from Let’s Encrypt are trusted by every major browser, and most hosts and website builders include free SSL that renews on its own. What matters is that it stays valid and every page loads over HTTPS, because Chrome now warns visitors before loading plain HTTP public sites by default. Paid certificates rarely add anything a local business needs.

What happens if I stop paying for website maintenance?

On Squarespace or Wix, the platform keeps updating the software, so the main risk is stale content. On WordPress, plugins and themes fall behind, security holes stay open, and a forced PHP upgrade from your host can break the site. Forms often fail silently first. If you cancel a care plan, make sure you have admin access, the domain login and a recent off-site backup.

How much does a .com domain cost per year in 2027?

Verisign raised the wholesale price of a .com domain to $10.97 on November 1, 2026, and registrars add their own markup on top. At-cost registrars charge close to the wholesale price, while many consumer registrars advertise a cheap first year and renew at $20 or more. Budget from the renewal price, and make sure the domain is registered in your business’s name.

Can my web designer keep my domain if I stop working with them?

If the designer registered the domain in their own name or account, they control it, and getting it back may take cooperation or a formal transfer process. That is why the domain should be registered to your business, using an email address you control, from day one. If the designer already holds it, ask for a transfer in writing and document the request.
