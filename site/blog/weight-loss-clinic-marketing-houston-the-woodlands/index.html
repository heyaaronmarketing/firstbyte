Scroll Instagram in Kingwood for ten minutes and you’ll see it: “$199 semaglutide, no insurance needed,” a smiling stock model, a countdown timer. Some of those ads come from out-of-state telehealth brands. Some come from clinics off FM 1960 that will be getting a letter. Your clinic is competing with both, for the same patient, with a fraction of their budget.

You won’t out-shout them on price. Weight loss clinic marketing in Houston and The Woodlands works when you compete on the things an app can’t fake, starting with a local physician who actually sees patients and follow-up visits that happen in a building. A straight answer about cost helps more than most clinics expect.

On the ad side, you need to know what Google, Meta, the FDA, the FTC and the Texas Medical Board each allow in a GLP-1 ad in 2026. Most clinics get at least one of those wrong, and a few manage all five.

Below is the playbook we’d run for a physician-led medical weight loss clinic north of Houston, rules first.

Who is actually buying GLP-1 programs, and where

KFF’s November 2025 poll found 12% of US adults currently taking a GLP-1 drug and 18% who had ever taken one. More than half of users (56%) said the drugs were difficult to afford, and 27% of insured users paid the full cost themselves.

The interesting number for you is where people got them. Most (76%) got them from a doctor, but 17% went through an online provider or website and 9% through a medical spa or aesthetic center. That 26% is your competitive set, and it’s why your ads keep running into theirs.

Price is the first question, so hiding it costs you leads.

And because a big share of patients have already tried an app or a med spa, “in-person visits with a physician who checks your labs” actually means something to them. Plenty of the people calling you are on their second attempt, and they remember what the first one lacked.

Can you advertise GLP-1 on Google?

Yes, with limits that changed in late 2025. Google’s October 2025 policy update and its current restricted drug terms page say that for US campaigns, “you don’t need to be certified in order to use prescription drug terms in ads and landing pages.” So an ad or page can say semaglutide or tirzepatide. But “you must be certified in order to keyword-target these terms,” and certification is still required for online pharmacies, telemedicine providers and drug manufacturers.

In a real account, that plays out like this:

  • Bid on the service, not the molecule. “Medical weight loss near me,” “weight loss clinic the woodlands,” “weight loss doctor spring tx” and “glp-1 weight loss program” are where we start. Bidding on “semaglutide near me” or “ozempic clinic” without certification will draw disapprovals.
  • Under Google’s healthcare and medicines policy, US telemedicine providers need LegitScript accreditation plus Google certification. If your program prescribes after a video visit with no in-person component, talk to us and your attorney about whether that applies before you launch.
  • No hCG. Google’s unapproved substances list specifically includes products containing hCG “in relation to weight loss or weight control.” That old hCG diet page from 2014 still sitting on your site can get ads disapproved.
  • Health is a sensitive interest category. Google’s Health sensitive-interest category page covers chronic conditions and injections, and blocks advertiser-curated audiences like Customer Match, so whatever retargeting you do happens through content and email.

Look at the search terms report and you’ll see how often “ozempic” searches still match your broad keywords. In uncertified accounts we add the brand drug names as negatives and move on. Our search marketing team sets this up for clinics every month.

How to advertise semaglutide without getting a warning letter

The compounding boom grew out of shortages, and the shortages are over. Per Foley & Lardner, FDA declared the semaglutide shortage resolved on February 21, 2025, with enforcement discretion ending April 22, 2025 for 503A pharmacies and May 22, 2025 for 503B outsourcing facilities. Whether a pharmacy can still compound for a specific patient is a legal and pharmacy question. How you advertise it is a marketing question, and that’s where FDA has been looking.

On September 9, 2025, FDA and HHS announced a crackdown on misleading drug advertising, and Holland & Knight reported warning letters to several GLP-1 telehealth companies. Phrases FDA flagged included “Semaglutide (the same ingredient in Wegovy & Ozempic),” “TIRZEPATIDE (GENERIC MOUNJARO)” and “Clinically proven ingredients.” In February 2026, Fierce Pharma reported another 30 letters, citing claims of the same active ingredients as the brand drugs, claims of clinical trial testing and implied FDA approval. The letters state plainly: “Compounded drug products are not FDA-approved.”

Copy rules we follow for any clinic that offers compounded medication:

  • Never call a compounded product “generic Ozempic,” “the same as Wegovy” or anything similar.
  • Never say or imply it’s FDA-approved or clinically proven.
  • Lead with the program (physician visits, labs, dosing, nutrition support), not the drug.
  • If you offer both brand-name and compounded options, say which is which on the pricing page, in plain English.

We’re not your regulatory counsel, and FDA’s position keeps moving. A healthcare attorney should look at your pricing page at least once a year (twice wouldn’t hurt, the way 2025 went).

Meta’s rules for weight loss ads

Meta is where most clinics burn money, usually on rejected ads. The health and wellness ad policy requires weight loss ads to target people 18 or older, bans a “side-by-side comparison after the use of a product or transformation for weight loss,” and prohibits ads that “imply or attempt to generate negative self-perception.” Add the personal attributes policy, which bans ads that assert or imply a health condition about the viewer, and the usual weight loss headlines are gone: “Tired of your belly fat?”, “Struggling to lose weight?”, before-and-after splits.

Prescription drugs are another layer. Meta’s prescription drug policy requires prior written permission for ads promoting prescription drugs, which it grants to online pharmacies, telehealth providers and drug manufacturers, and bars targeting under-18s. LegitScript certification is the usual route those businesses use to apply. For a brick-and-mortar clinic without that permission, the safe approach is to advertise the medical weight loss program and the consult, and keep drug names off Meta.

Tracking got harder as well. Since early 2025, Meta has been restricting which conversion events health advertisers can send, and Cardinal Digital Marketing’s write-up describes condition-specific providers losing lower-funnel events altogether. Weight loss clinics tend to land in the stricter tiers, so Meta’s instant forms (which keep the lead on-platform) and a fast callback often beat a pixel-optimized landing page. What the FTC and Texas Medical Board expect from your claims

The FTC’s final order against NextMed is the case every GLP-1 marketer should read. The FTC alleged that the telehealth company sold weight loss programs with undisclosed costs and membership commitments, made unsupported claims about how much weight clients lost, used fake testimonials and distorted reviews, and didn’t process cancellations and refunds promptly. The December 2025 order included $150,000 for consumer refunds and requires competent and reliable evidence for any average or typical results claim.

Texas adds its own layer. Under 22 TAC §164.3, the Medical Board treats these as deceptive advertising:

  • Testimonials without a note on the person’s credentials and a disclosure of any compensation they received
  • Models or actors who aren’t identified as such (that stock photo of a smiling woman on a scale)
  • “Free consult” claims when a third party is billed
  • Claiming a unique or exclusive skill you can’t back up
  • Hiding medical directorship or delegation to non-physicians when care runs on standing orders away from the physician’s main practice

If nurses or medical assistants give injections under delegation, 22 TAC §169.28 also requires the delegating physician’s name and license number posted where patients can see it, and staff name tags showing credentials.

We use a blunt rule with clients. Every number in an ad (“patients lose an average of 15%”) needs your own documented patient data or a cited study, plus a clear “results vary.” If you can’t produce it in an afternoon, don’t print it. Ranking for “medical weight loss near me”

Ads stop the day you stop paying. Your Google Business Profile and site pages keep working, and for “medical weight loss near me” the map pack usually gets the first look anyway.

Business Profile. “Weight loss service” is the category most clinics in this space use as primary; if you’re a primary care or internal medicine practice adding a program, keep your medical category primary and add weight loss as secondary. Check the options in your own dashboard, since Google edits its category list. List your program as a service with a starting price, name the physician in the description, and post photos of the real clinic and staff. If you have a physician who sees most weight loss patients, an individual practitioner profile can rank alongside the clinic’s.

The consult funnel: from ad click to first dose

A weight loss lead cools off fast. Someone who filled out your form at 9 p.m. has probably also filled out two telehealth forms, and one of those will text them by 9:05.

The funnel we’d build:

  1. A short form or a call. Name, phone, email, best time. Keep health questions out of the ad form and collect them in your HIPAA-compliant intake.
  2. Callback inside 15 minutes during business hours, and a text that goes out automatically after hours with a booking link.
  3. A paid consult. A modest fee, credited toward the first month, cuts no-shows. (If a third party is billed, don’t call it free.)
  4. Labs and the visit, where the physician or APRN decides candidacy. Some people will hear no. Your marketing should never promise everyone qualifies.
  5. First dose and week-one call. Nausea in week one is where many patients quit, and a nurse call on day three keeps a surprising number of them.

Measure each step weekly: leads, contacted, consults booked, shows, starts. If 60 leads become 8 starts, the leak is usually between “lead” and “contacted,” not in the ads. Our guide to landing pages covers the form side.

Retention: months two through twelve are the business

A GLP-1 program’s profit is in the months after the first. Clinics that market well and retain poorly end up buying the same number of new patients every month just to stand still.

What keeps patients enrolled:

  • Scheduled check-ins tied to dose changes, not just refills
  • Body composition or measurement tracking, so progress shows even when the scale stalls
  • Nutrition and strength support, in person or in small groups
  • A plateau script for staff, written with your physician, so nobody improvises medical advice
  • A maintenance plan for patients who reach their goal, so “done” doesn’t mean “gone”
  • Billing that matches the sign-up page exactly, with an easy cancel path

A hypothetical: a clinic in The Woodlands starts 25 patients a month at $399 a month. If average stay goes from 4 months to 7, that’s an extra $29,925 in revenue from each month’s group with no extra ad spend. Your price and margins will differ.

Ask for reviews at a milestone visit. At sign-up, nobody has anything to say yet. And keep reviews and photos within the rules above: no paid testimonials without disclosure, and on Meta, no side-by-side transformations at all.

Budget and timeline for a Houston weight loss clinic

Ranges we’d plan with for one location, illustrative and not a quote:

  • Google search: $2,000 to $6,000 a month in ad spend, focused on program and near-me terms in a tight radius around your office.
  • Meta: $1,000 to $3,000 a month, program-focused creative with instant forms, refreshed every three to four weeks.
  • A pricing page and program page in month one, then insurance, location and FAQ pages over the next quarter.
  • Someone on staff who owns callbacks. This is not optional, and it’s the line item clinics most often leave blank.

Search ads usually produce consults within the first couple of weeks. Meta takes a month or so of creative testing before it settles. Organic pages need three to six months to rank, sometimes longer in a crowded zip code.

We can’t predict how GLP-1 pricing from manufacturers and insurers will move next year, and we’re suspicious of anyone who says they can. So we’d build the brand on your physician and your follow-up. A $199 headline can be undercut by Friday.

Frequently asked questions

Can you advertise GLP-1 drugs on Google Ads?

Yes, for US campaigns. Since Google's October 2025 update, advertisers don't need certification to mention prescription drug terms like semaglutide in ads and landing pages, but they must be certified to keyword-target those terms. Telemedicine providers and online pharmacies still need certification, which for telehealth includes LegitScript. Most local clinics bid on program terms like medical weight loss near me instead.

Do I need LegitScript certification to advertise semaglutide?

It depends on your business model. Google requires LegitScript accreditation for US telemedicine providers, and Meta requires prior written permission for prescription drug ads, granted to online pharmacies, telehealth providers and manufacturers. A brick-and-mortar clinic that advertises its program without bidding on drug names usually doesn't need it, but confirm your model with your attorney before launching.

Can I use before and after photos in weight loss ads?

Not on Facebook or Instagram. Meta's health and wellness policy bans side-by-side comparisons after weight loss, and bans ads that create negative self-perception. On your own website, real patient photos can be used with written consent and a results-vary note, and Texas Medical Board rules require disclosing any compensation and identifying models who aren't actual patients.

How do weight loss clinics get more patients?

The clinics that grow steadily rank in Google Maps for medical weight loss near me, publish clear pricing, run search ads on program terms, and call new leads back within minutes. They also keep patients longer with scheduled check-ins, nutrition support and a maintenance plan. Retention often adds more revenue than extra ad spend because each patient stays for months.

Can I say compounded semaglutide is the same as Ozempic?

No. FDA warning letters in 2025 and 2026 cited phrases such as the same ingredient in Wegovy and Ozempic and generic Mounjaro, along with claims of clinical testing or implied approval. Compounded drug products are not FDA-approved. Describe your program, your physician oversight and your pricing instead, and label brand and compounded options clearly.
