Tuesday, 7:15 p.m., a living room in Kingwood. Your rep has measured 19 windows, laid out the sample corner on the coffee table and walked through the quote. The homeowner likes it. Her husband is in Dallas until Friday. “We’ll talk it over.”

That appointment cost you somewhere north of $300 in ad spend to set, plus the rep’s evening. It will probably never close.

Window and door replacement marketing in Houston and The Woodlands comes down to one number most companies don’t track honestly: cost per sat appointment, meaning a demo where every decision-maker was at the table. Leads are cheap to buy and expensive to waste. The companies that grow here pick narrow, high-intent searches, sell the problem Houston homeowners actually feel (heat, storms, noise, ugly 1980s aluminum frames), and run a confirmation process tight enough that the rep isn’t driving to Kingwood for half a household.

This is how we’d set up marketing for a local window and door company competing with the national brands from Katy to Conroe. It includes the 2026 changes to the tax credit pitch that a lot of websites still haven’t caught up with.

What Houston homeowners are really buying when they replace windows

Nobody wakes up wanting windows. They want the back bedroom to stop hitting 82 degrees at 5 p.m. in August. They want to stop hearing trucks on the Grand Parkway. They want the fogged double-pane units in the breakfast nook gone before they list the house in Cypress.

In the accounts we’ve run for home services companies around Houston, four motivations show up again and again in calls and form comments:

  • Heat and the electric bill. West- and south-facing glass in a Houston summer is the most common complaint by a mile. Single-pane aluminum frames from the 1970s and ’80s are still all over older parts of Houston, Spring and Humble.
  • Storms. Houston remembers. In the May 16, 2024 derecho, ABC13 reported that more than 2,500 windows or skylights were lost or damaged in downtown high-rises alone. Two months later, Hurricane Beryl knocked out power to more than 2.26 million CenterPoint customers, per Utility Dive. A homeowner who sat through either storm thinks about glass differently.
  • Noise. Houses backing onto I-45, the Hardy Toll Road or a busy stretch of FM 1960.
  • Resale and looks. Fogged seals, rotted wood doors, a front entry that looks like 1994.

Your ads and landing pages should pick one of these per campaign. “Energy efficient vinyl windows” is a product description. “Back bedroom too hot? We fix that one first” is a reason to call.

The federal tax credit pitch is over, so update your site

For three years, “30% federal tax credit!” carried a lot of window ads. That credit is gone for new jobs. Under the 2025 reconciliation law, the Section 25C energy efficient home improvement credit ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, per NAHB. Before the change it would have run through 2032.

For reference, the old rules were 30% of product cost with a $600 yearly cap for windows and skylights, inside a $1,200 annual limit for envelope improvements, and only for windows that met ENERGY STAR Most Efficient criteria, per ENERGY STAR’s tax credit page. Homeowners who installed in 2025 may still be claiming it on their 2025 return this spring. Nobody installing in 2026 gets it.

What we’d do this week:

  1. Search your own site for “tax credit,” “25C” and “IRS.” Rewrite every page that implies a current credit.
  2. Pause or edit ad copy, sitelinks and Meta ads that still mention it. Old Performance Max assets are a common hiding spot.
  3. Tell your reps. A homeowner who later learns there was no credit remembers who said there was.

Utility rebates are the next thing homeowners ask about, and here Houston is confusing. CenterPoint runs a window rebate page that pays per window, but it’s for natural-gas customers in the Northern Climate Zone with Minnesota installer numbers, per the CenterPoint window rebate page. It shows up when people in Spring search “CenterPoint window rebate,” and it doesn’t apply to them. CenterPoint’s Houston efficiency page lists programs for HVAC, smart thermostats, insulation and pool pumps, delivered through participating contractors because Texas law keeps CenterPoint from offering them directly to homeowners. It promotes ENERGY STAR windows as saving up to 31% in the Houston zone compared with noncertified products, but it doesn’t list a window rebate. If you want to mention incentives, say “we’ll check what’s currently available for your home” and leave the dollar figures out.

Impact windows and TDI windstorm rules belong in a coastal campaign

A lot of Houston window companies muddle this part of their marketing. They run one “hurricane impact windows” campaign across the whole metro, and the copy hints at code requirements that apply to some customers and not others.

The Texas Department of Insurance treats 14 first-tier coastal counties as a designated catastrophe area, including Brazoria, Galveston, Chambers, Matagorda and Jefferson, plus the part of Harris County east of State Highway 146, per TDI’s windstorm inspection page and TDI’s windstorm FAQ. In that area, new construction, alterations, additions and repairs have to be built and inspected to TDI’s specifications for the home to qualify for a Texas Windstorm Insurance Association policy. A WPI-8 is the certificate of compliance issued after an inspection during the work. TDI’s own list notes that simply replacing the glass in a window or door doesn’t require an inspection. Whether a full window replacement does is a question for TDI’s Windstorm Inspections Program (800-248-6032, option 3), and we’d have your operations manager get that answer in writing before marketing promises anything.

There’s also a selling point. TWIA offers an optional discount for existing homes that retrofit exterior openings with windborne-debris-resistant products, with each product inspected, per TDI’s windstorm FAQ. For a homeowner in League City or Dickinson paying TWIA premiums, “ask us whether impact windows could lower your windstorm premium” is a far better hook than “hurricane proof.”

So split it:

  • Coastal campaign (Galveston County towns like League City, Dickinson and Kemah, Brazoria County towns like Pearland and Alvin, and the parts of Seabrook, La Porte and Baytown east of SH 146, so check each address): impact windows, WPI-8 handling, insurance discounts, before-hurricane-season scheduling. Landing page explains the paperwork in plain English.
  • North and west campaign (The Woodlands, Spring, Conroe, Tomball, Magnolia, Cypress, Katy): impact glass sold on storm peace of mind, security and noise. No hint of a code requirement, because there isn’t one tied to TWIA there.

Don’t write “meets Texas windstorm code” unless the product and install method do, and you can show the documentation. Homeowners forward that line to their insurance agent.

Google Ads for window replacement: expensive clicks, so choose the searches

Window and door keywords sit in one of the pricier corners of home services. WordStream’s 2025 Google Ads benchmarks, based on U.S. search campaigns from April 2024 through March 2025, put the median home and home improvement cost per click at $7.85 and cost per lead at $90.92. In the window accounts we’ve seen, replacement keywords in Houston run well above that category median, especially for “near me” searches in the summer. Our Houston CPC benchmarks have more context.

The only door category is garage doors. A replacement company can’t lean on LSA the way a plumber can, which is one more reason regular search has to be tight. Bought leads vs. your own booked appointments

Most window companies in Houston have tried buying leads from a lead aggregator. The pitch is easy: pay per lead, no ad management. The reality, in our experience, is shared leads sold to three or four companies, homeowners who “just wanted a ballpark,” and phone numbers that were typed in by thumb on a phone at a red light. Our post on paid lead platforms goes into the math.

Your own leads cost more per lead and less per sale, usually. The difference is control: you choose the questions on the form, the area, and how fast someone calls back.

The parts that decide whether a lead becomes a sat appointment:

  • Speed. Call within five minutes during business hours. After an hour, the homeowner has usually booked whoever called first. See our speed-to-lead guide.
  • The all-owners question. Ask politely at booking whether everyone on the title will be there. “We like to have everyone who’ll make the decision see the samples, so nobody has to relay the details” works fine.
  • Confirmation. A text the day before and a call the morning of. Reschedule rather than send a rep to half a household.
  • Qualifying on the form. Number of windows (a range is fine), homeowner or renter, and timeline. Fewer leads, better appointments.

Track sales net of cancellations, too. In Texas, a sale over $25 made somewhere other than the seller’s place of business, including the buyer’s home, is a home solicitation transaction with a three-day right to cancel, and the seller has to tell the buyer about it orally and in writing, per the Texas Attorney General. If a lead source produces signed contracts that cancel on day three, it isn’t producing sales.

Showroom or in-home: what to promote

Some Houston window companies run a showroom off the Katy Freeway or I-45 and others are in-home only. Both work. The marketing changes, though.

With a showroom, your Google Business Profile can be a storefront with an address and posted hours, which helps you show up in the map pack for searches near that location. Put the showroom on the website with photos of the actual door wall and window displays, and offer “visit the showroom Saturday” as a second call to action for people who aren’t ready to have a stranger measure their house. The map pack guide covers the profile side.

In-home only, you’re a service-area business: hide the address, list the cities you serve, and lean harder on reviews and photos of completed jobs. Some companies now offer a short video consult first, with a phone walkaround of the house, then send the rep for measurements. It filters out the tire-kickers and cuts drive time from The Woodlands to Sugar Land.

Financing ads: the words that trigger disclosures

Financing sells windows. A $14,000 job feels different at a monthly number. It’s also where window ads most often break federal rules without anyone noticing.

Under Regulation Z, section 1026.24, if a closed-end credit ad states the amount or percentage of a down payment, the number of payments or the repayment period, the amount of any payment, or the amount of any finance charge, it has to also disclose the down payment terms, the repayment terms over the full loan, and the annual percentage rate (using that phrase or “APR”), including whether it can go up. So “Windows from $89 a month!” on a Meta ad without the rest of the terms is a problem. “Financing available, ask for details” isn’t.

Practical rules:

  • Get the approved ad language from your finance partner and use it word for word. Deferred-interest and “no payments for 12 months” promos carry their own disclosure rules.
  • Put the full disclosure on the landing page next to the offer, not in a footer.
  • On Meta, an ad whose main message is a credit offer may need to run under the Financial Products and Services special ad category. That locks age to 18 to 65+, includes all genders, removes ZIP targeting, requires at least a 15-mile radius and takes away lookalikes, per Jon Loomer’s guide to Meta’s special ad categories. We usually keep financing as a secondary line in the ad and the full offer on the landing page.

Reviews: how a local window company beats the national brands

The national window brands have bigger budgets, TV spots and brand recognition. A local Houston company has something they struggle to fake: a crew that lives here, an owner who answers the phone, and reviews that mention The Woodlands, Kingwood and Sugar Land by name. Our post on outranking national brands covers the search side.

Review volume is the lever. Window jobs are infrequent, so you can’t wait for reviews to show up on their own. Ask twice:

  1. At the end of install day, after the walkthrough, when the house is clean and the homeowner is happy.
  2. Two weeks later, after the first hot afternoon, when they’ve noticed the difference.

A text that works: “Hi Karen, it’s Mike from [company]. Thanks again for having our crew out Tuesday. If you have a minute, a Google review helps a small Houston company more than you’d think: [link]. And if anything isn’t right, text me back first and we’ll fix it.”

Stay inside Google’s prohibited content policy: no discounts or gift cards for reviews, no asking only the happy customers, and no discouraging negative ones. The Google reviews guide has more scripts and reply templates.

And photograph every job. Before-and-after pairs from a real Cypress house beat stock photos of a lake house in Minnesota on every channel we’ve tested.

HOA approvals in The Woodlands and the master-planned suburbs

A lot of jobs stall at the HOA. Homeowners in The Woodlands, Kingwood, Cinco Ranch, Bridgeland and most newer Montgomery County subdivisions live under deed restrictions, and a color change on window frames or a new front door style can need sign-off.

In The Woodlands, The Woodlands Township’s permitting page says any alteration or repair of a structure on a lot with an existing single-family home needs prior written approval, with applications filed through the Township’s Civic Access Portal. Some items in the Residential Development Standards are pre-approved, so check before assuming.

Turn that into marketing. A page titled something like “Window replacement in The Woodlands: what Covenant Administration needs” answers a question nobody else in the market answers, and it ranks for searches the national brands ignore. Offer to prepare the application with spec sheets and color samples. “We handle the HOA paperwork” is a line we’d put in ads in the master-planned areas.

Frequently asked questions

How do window companies in Houston get more leads?

Focus Google Ads on high-intent searches like window replacement plus a town name, load heavy negative keywords for cleaning, tint, car windows and Microsoft Windows, and send each search to a matching landing page. Build reviews with two requests per job, track calls by channel, and judge results on sat appointments with all decision-makers present rather than raw lead counts.

Is the federal tax credit for replacement windows still available?

No, not for new installs. The Section 25C energy efficient home improvement credit ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Under the old rules it covered 30% of qualifying window costs up to $600 a year. Homeowners who installed qualifying windows in 2025 may still claim it on that year's return, so window companies should remove current-credit claims from ads and websites.

Do impact windows require a windstorm inspection in Houston?

It depends on the address. TDI's windstorm program covers 14 coastal counties and the part of Harris County east of State Highway 146, where work must meet TDI specifications for TWIA coverage. Most of Houston, including The Woodlands, Katy and Cypress, is outside that area. TDI says glass-only replacement doesn't need an inspection; confirm full replacements with TDI's Windstorm Inspections Program.

What does Google Ads cost for a window replacement company?

WordStream's 2025 benchmarks put the median home and home improvement cost per click at $7.85 and cost per lead at $90.92, and window replacement keywords in Houston often run higher than that median. Many local window companies spend several thousand dollars a month on search. The number that matters is cost per sat appointment and per net sale after the three-day cancellation window.

Can window companies advertise monthly payments?

Yes, with full disclosures. Under Regulation Z, stating a monthly payment, down payment, number of payments or finance charge in an ad triggers required disclosures: down payment terms, full repayment terms and the APR. Use the exact language your finance partner approves, put the full terms beside the offer on the landing page, and expect Meta to treat credit-focused ads as a special ad category.
