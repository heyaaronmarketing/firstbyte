Three tabs open on the laptop. Wix, Squarespace, and a WordPress tutorial that mentioned “child themes” in the second paragraph and lost you there. Your nephew swears by Webflow. Your accountant’s site is on GoDaddy and looks fine.

So which one? For most small service businesses around Houston, Squarespace or Wix gets you a good-looking site you can edit yourself for roughly $30 to $40 a month. WordPress is the better call when SEO content, integrations and full ownership matter more than convenience, as long as someone is on the hook for updates. Design-led brands with a designer on call should look hard at Webflow. And if selling products online is the business, it’s Shopify.

There’s no single winner, and anyone who says otherwise probably sells one. We build and fix sites on several of these for businesses in The Woodlands, Spring and Conroe. Each one has a spot where it’s right and a spot where it quietly costs you money.

What each platform actually is, in plain English

The big split is between hosted builders and self-hosted software.

Wix, Squarespace, Webflow and Shopify are hosted builders. You pay a monthly or yearly fee, and the company handles servers, security patches, backups and the SSL certificate. You rent the house, furnished, and the landlord fixes the plumbing. You also live by the landlord’s rules.

WordPress comes in two flavors, which confuses everyone. WordPress.org is free, open-source software you install on hosting you pay for separately. That’s what people usually mean by “a WordPress site.” WordPress.com is a hosted service run by Automattic that behaves more like the builders. Per eSEOspace’s plan rundown, since April 2026 every paid WordPress.com plan, starting with the $4-a-month Personal plan, can install plugins, which narrows the old gap between the two.

Scale-wise, W3Techs counted WordPress on 39.5% of all websites as of October 31, 2026, followed by Shopify at 5.3%, Wix at 4.2%, Squarespace at 2.4% and Webflow at 0.8%. Popularity cuts both ways. You get more developers and plugins, and a lot more people trying to break into WordPress sites.

And if your marketing company built your site on its own proprietary platform, you’re in a fifth category with the most lock-in of all. Ask before you sign.

Wix vs WordPress for SEO (and where Squarespace, Webflow and Shopify land)

We do SEO for a living, so this may sound odd coming from us. The platform is almost never the reason a local business doesn’t rank. Thin service pages, no reviews, a weak Google Business Profile and zero local links lose far more rankings than any builder does.

URL control

WordPress and Webflow let you set almost any URL structure you want, including clean service-area pages like /ac-repair-spring-tx/. Wix used to force prefixes like /post/ on blog articles; it now lets you change those prefixes in Wix’s SEO Settings, though the structure must keep at least one variable. Squarespace gives you control of page slugs, with collection items (blog posts, products) living under their collection’s URL.

Shopify is the strict one. Pages live under /pages/, products under /products/, collections under /collections/, and Shopify’s own staff have confirmed for years that those segments can’t be removed. For a store, that’s fine. For a plumber who wants a clean page per suburb, it’s an irritation.

Redirects

All five can do 301 redirects, which you need the moment you rename a page or move platforms. WordPress needs a plugin (or server rules). Wix has a URL Redirect Manager. Squarespace uses a URL Mappings panel where you type rules by hand. Webflow and Shopify have redirect screens in settings.

Schema and technical extras

WordPress with an SEO plugin like Yoast or Rank Math gives you the most control over structured data (LocalBusiness, FAQ, Service). Webflow lets you add custom code per page and wire CMS fields into JSON-LD, which works nicely if someone on your side can write JSON-LD. Wix adds some markup automatically and lets you paste custom structured data per page. Squarespace generates basic markup and accepts code injection on higher plans, but it’s the least flexible of the group.

Our honest ranking for local SEO flexibility: WordPress, then Webflow, then Wix, then Squarespace, with Shopify strong for products and awkward for service pages. The gap between first and fourth is smaller than the forums make it sound, and we’ll take a well-written Squarespace site over a neglected WordPress one any day.

Which website builder is fastest?

Speed is one place the hosted builders really do win, though not for the reason most people assume.

Those numbers come from HTTP Archive’s Core Web Vitals technology report, via Search Engine Journal’s look at HTTP Archive data, using July 2025 data. The software itself is fine. WordPress sites are slow because so many sit on bargain shared hosting with a heavy page builder, eight sliders and 40 plugins, and a well-built WordPress site on decent hosting passes Core Web Vitals without drama. The builders simply stop you from making the worst choices.

Shopify and Webflow weren’t in that breakdown. In our experience both are quick out of the box until apps and scripts pile up.

On WordPress, budget for real hosting and a lean theme. On Wix or Squarespace, stop fighting the platform with giant uncompressed hero videos. Then run your own site through PageSpeed Insights; our guide on how fast your website should load explains which numbers to watch.

Who owns your website if you leave?

This is the question most owners never ask until they’re angry at a vendor.

With Wix, you own your content and your domain, but the site itself can’t leave. Wix’s own help center says a Wix site has to be hosted and operated on Wix’s servers. Leaving means a rebuild.

Squarespace has an export, and it’s partial. According to WPBeginner’s migration guide, basic pages, one blog and its posts, galleries, and text, image and embed blocks come across to WordPress. Product pages, events, audio and video blocks, styles and custom CSS don’t, and images have to be pulled over separately.

Webflow lets you export HTML, CSS and JavaScript, but only on a paid Workspace plan, and Webflow’s code export documentation spells out what’s left behind: CMS content, ecommerce, forms, site search and password protection. A blog-heavy Webflow site exports as a pretty shell. (CMS content can be exported separately as CSV.)

Shopify exports products, customers and orders as CSV files, which is what a store really needs. The theme stays.

Self-hosted WordPress is the only option where you can pack up the whole thing (files and database) and move it to any host. That portability is the biggest argument for WordPress, and we lean that way for any business that expects to outlast its current web vendor.

Whatever you choose, the domain matters more than the platform. If your web person registered the domain in their own account, you don’t fully own your web presence on any platform. Our post on who owns your marketing accounts when you switch agencies walks through getting the domain, analytics and ad accounts into your name.

Editing, security and the maintenance nobody budgets for

Think about who edits the site after launch. Usually the owner on a Sunday night, or an office manager with 11 other jobs.

Squarespace is the easiest for that person; it’s tidy and hard to break. Wix is more flexible, which cuts both ways: you can drag anything anywhere, and staff sometimes do, which is how mobile layouts end up with overlapping text. Webflow’s visual designer is built for designers. Its content editing mode is simple enough for staff updating text and blog posts, but layout changes belong to whoever built the thing. With WordPress, it depends entirely on the build. A clean block-editor site is pleasant. A site built in a page builder by someone who left in 2021 can be a minefield.

Security is where the trade-off gets real. On the hosted builders, patching is the company’s problem. On WordPress, it’s yours. Patchstack’s 2026 security report counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, 91% of them in plugins and only 6 in WordPress core. Worse, 46% didn’t have a fix from the developer by the time they were made public.

Millions of sites still run WordPress safely. The owners keep plugins few and reputable, run updates at least weekly, keep off-site backups and pick a host with a firewall. If nobody is doing that for your site today, you probably won’t hear about it until the site starts redirecting visitors to a pharmacy in another country.

Booking, forms, CRM and the tools you already use

For a lot of Houston-area businesses, the website is really a front door to some other system: ServiceTitan or Housecall Pro for home services, Toast or ChowNow for restaurant ordering, Mindbody or Vagaro for studios and salons, an EHR intake form for a medical practice.

Most of those tools give you an embed code or a booking link, and every platform here can take an embed. The differences show up at the edges.

  • Wix has its own bookings tools, and Squarespace sells scheduling as an add-on. Either is fine for a solo lash artist or massage therapist. A multi-tech HVAC company will outgrow both and should book through its field-service software.
  • WordPress has form plugins that connect to nearly any CRM. Wix and Squarespace forms work, but getting leads into a CRM often means Zapier or a native integration that may only exist for the big names. Webflow forms are solid (they just don’t survive a code export).
  • All five take Google Analytics 4 and Google Tag Manager. Call tracking and conversion tracking are easiest on WordPress and Webflow, where you control the code; Wix and Squarespace have specific places to paste code, and a few tracking setups fight with them.
  • Medical and legal practices: don’t trust any builder’s default contact form with sensitive information. Use your practice software’s intake tool and embed or link it.

Before you pick, write down every system your site has to talk to and check each one’s integration page.

Webflow vs WordPress: when the designer’s favorite makes sense

Designers love Webflow, and we get why. The code is clean, the hosting is fast, there are no plugins to update, and a skilled designer gets pixel-level control without calling a developer. For a brand-conscious business with a designer on retainer (a boutique hotel, a med spa, a real estate team selling the Hughes Landing lifestyle), it’s often the right call.

The catches are practical. Pricing now includes a site plan plus, for whoever builds and maintains the site, a Workspace plan; per Memberstack, Workspace Core is $19 a month on yearly billing and is also where code export lives. The Premium site plan includes 50GB of bandwidth, with add-ons after that. The integration library is smaller than WordPress’s. And if your designer moves on, the pool of local Webflow pros is smaller than the pool of WordPress people.

If you need lots of pages and integrations, a big local content plan (service pages for Spring, Tomball, Magnolia, Cypress and Katy, say) and the freedom to hire anyone, WordPress is the safer bet. Webflow pulls ahead when design quality and low maintenance matter more to you than plugin variety.

We’d steer a DIY owner away from building in Webflow alone. It’s a designer’s tool, and it feels like one by the second hour.

When to DIY and when to pay someone

DIY makes sense if you’re early, your budget is tight, you pick Squarespace or Wix, and you’re willing to spend a couple of weekends writing real copy. Use a template close to what you want and change as little of the design as possible. Spend your energy on the words, the photos of your actual team and trucks, and a clear call button.

Pay someone once the site has to carry real lead flow, you’re running Google Ads to it, you need a dozen service-area pages, or you’re migrating an older site that already ranks. Migrations do the most damage in our experience. URLs change, nobody sets up redirects, and six months of rankings vanish. Frequently asked questions

Is Wix or WordPress better for SEO?

WordPress gives you more control over URLs, structured data, redirects and site speed tuning, so it has a higher ceiling. Wix has closed much of the gap and now lets you change blog and product URL prefixes. For a typical local business, content quality, reviews and your Google Business Profile matter far more than the platform. A well-written Wix site will outrank a neglected WordPress one.

Is Squarespace good for a small business website?

Yes, for many small businesses. It is the easiest platform to keep looking polished, hosting and security are handled for you, and the Core plan costs $29 a month on annual billing after the July 2026 increase. It is less flexible for SEO and integrations, and its export to other platforms is partial, so it fits best when the site is simple and staff will do the edits.

Is Webflow worth it for a small business?

Webflow is worth it when you have a designer who knows it and design quality matters to your brand. It is fast, needs no plugin updates, and its Premium site plan costs $25 a month billed yearly. It is a poor fit for owners building alone, because the learning curve is steep, and its code export leaves out CMS content, forms and ecommerce.

Can I move my Wix site to WordPress later?

You can move your content and domain, but not the site itself. Wix says sites must be hosted on its servers, so moving means rebuilding the design on WordPress, copying pages and posts, and setting up 301 redirects from every old URL to its new address. Plan the redirects before launch, or you risk losing rankings you built on Wix.

What is the cheapest way to build a small business website?

The cheapest workable route is a Squarespace or Wix template you set up yourself, at roughly $17 to $39 a month plus a domain. WordPress software is free, but hosting, premium plugins and maintenance usually cost more over a few years unless you handle updates yourself. Cheap is fine to start, as long as you own the domain and the content.
